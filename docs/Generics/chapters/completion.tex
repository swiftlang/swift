\chapter{Completion}\label{chap:completion}

\IndexDefinition{Knuth-Bendix algorithm}%
\index{completion!z@\igobble|seealso{Knuth-Bendix algorithm}}
\lettrine{K}{nuth-Bendix completion} is the central algorithm in the Requirement Machine. Completion attempts to construct a \index{complete presentation}\index{finite complete presentation}finite complete rewriting system from a monoid presentation, and as we already saw in \SecRef{sec:normal form algorithm}, a complete rewriting system allows us to decide if two words are equivalent, by computing and comparing their normal forms. Building on the results in \SecRef{rqm correctness}, completion is how we arrive at a \emph{decision procedure} for the \index{derived requirement!decision procedure}derived requirements formalism.

Recall that the key property of our encoding was that a \emph{derived} requirement defines a rewrite path over rewrite rules representing explicit requirements. Thus, by computing normal forms in our finite complete rewriting system, we can implement \index{generic signature query}generic signature queries and \index{requirement minimization}minimization.

This chapter begins with a self-contained description of the algorithm, together with some optimizations specific to the Requirement Machine. This can be supplemented by any textbook on rewriting, such as \cite{book2012string} or \cite{andallthat}. This is followed by some examples, and finally, a demonstration of one particular generic signature that cannot be solved by the Requirement Machine in \SecRef{sec:event horizon}, where the monoid presentation has a decidable word problem, but no finite complete presentation over any alphabet.

\paragraph{The algorithm.}  The original paper by \index{Donald~Knuth}Knuth and \index{Peter Bendix}Bendix described the algorithm for term rewriting systems~\cite{Knuth1983}; a correctness proof was later given by \index{Gerard Huet@G\'erard Huet}G\'erard Huet in \cite{HUET198111}. In our case, the terms are elements of a free monoid; this was studied in \cite{narendran}. A survey of related techniques appears in \cite{BUCHBERGER19873}. We will work through a bottom-up presentation, as a series of subroutines:
\begin{itemize}
\item \AlgRef{critical pair algo} forms a \emph{critical pair} when the left-hand sides of two rules are known to \emph{overlap} at some position.
\item \AlgRef{overlap trie lookup} finds all rules that overlap a given rule at a fixed position. This is the key step in the previous algorithm.
\item \AlgRef{find overlapping rule algo} finds all pairs of rules such that their left-hand sides overlap at one or more positions.
\item \AlgRef{knuthbendix} is the main entry point, and top-level loop, which repeatedly checks for critical pairs and resolves them until \emph{local confluence} is achieved.
\end{itemize}
We will also see some related algorithms to simplify the rewriting system in \SecRef{sec:rule simplification}.

\paragraph{Local confluence.}
Our ultimate goal is to construct a list of rules such that the induced \index{reduction relation}reduction relation $\Red$ satisfies the \index{Church-Rosser property}Church-Rosser property: if $x\Eq y$ are two \index{monoid congruence}equivalent words, then $x\Red z$ and $y\Red z$ for some word $z$. By \ThmRef{church rosser theorem}, this is equivalent to $\Red$ being \index{terminating reduction relation}terminating and \index{confluence}confluent. We already saw in \SecRef{sec:normal form algorithm} that one way to guarantee termination is to choose a suitable reduction order. While confluence is difficult to verify directly, there is a simpler related concept that is easier to check:
\begin{definition}
A reduction relation $\Red$ is \IndexDefinition{local confluence}\emph{locally confluent}, if whenever $s_1$ and $s_2$ are two positive \index{rewrite step!local confluence}rewrite steps with $\Src(s_1)=\Src(s_2)$, there exists a word $z$ such that $\Dst(s_1)\Red z$ and $\Dst(s_2)\Red z$.
\end{definition}
A 1941 result by \index{Newman's lemma}Max~Newman~\cite{newman} relates confluence with local confluence:
\begin{theorem}[Newman's Lemma]
If a reduction relation $\Red$ is \index{terminating reduction relation}terminating and locally confluent, then $\Red$ is confluent.
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{Overlapping rules.} To test for local confluence, we ``diverge'' from a word by only one step in two different directions, and then check if both sides can be reduced to the same word using our reduction relation. We will see this can be decided algorithmically, and also that we can ``repair'' any local \index{confluence violation}confluence violations we find.

A pair of positive \index{rewrite step!critical pair}rewrite steps with a common source define a \IndexDefinition{critical pair}\emph{critical pair}. A critical pair shows that some word can be rewritten in ``two different ways.'' We can answer if our rewrite rules define a locally confluent reduction relation by inspecting each critical pair. With any non-trivial list of rewrite rules, there are infinitely many such critical pairs, however, all but a finite subset can be disregarded. Suppose we have these two rules over some alphabet $A$:
\begin{gather*}
u_1\Red v_1\\
u_2\Red v_2
\end{gather*}
For any word $x\in A^*$, we can form the ``sandwich'' word $t := u_1xu_2$. Every such choice of $x$ defines a new critical pair; the occurrences of $u_1$ and $u_2$ within $t$ can be rewritten in two ways, by $s_1 := (u_1\Red v_1)xu_2$ and $s_2 := u_1x(u_2\Red v_2)$. Such critical pairs are called \IndexDefinition{orthogonal rewrite step}\index{rewrite step!orthogonal}\emph{orthogonal}. We can draw a \index{commutative diagram}commutative diagram like this:
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzcd}
&u_1xu_2\arrow[ld, Rightarrow, "s_1:=(u_1\Red v_1)xu_2"', bend right]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow, "s_2:=u_1x(u_2\Red v_2)", bend left]\\
v_1xu_2\arrow[rd, Rightarrow, "s_1' := v_1x(u_2\Red v_2)"', bend right]&&u_1xv_2\arrow[ld, Rightarrow, "s_2':=v_1x(u_2\Red v_2)", bend left]\\
&v_1xv_2
\end{tikzcd}
\end{center}
We can also visualize an orthogonal critical pair using the ``pictorial'' notation for rewrite steps we devised in \SecRef{sec:rewriting}:

Since $s_1$ and $s_2$ rewrite disjoint \index{factor}factors of $t$, an orthogonal critical pair cannot witness a local confluence violation. Regardless of whether we apply $s_1$ or $s_2$ first, there exists a complementary rewrite step $s_1'$ or $s_2'$ to rewrite $\Dst(s_1)$ or $\Dst(s_2)$ into the ``reduced sandwich'' $v_1xv_2$.

Clearly, in our quest to uncover local \index{confluence violation}confluence violations, we only need to inspect critical pairs that are \emph{not} orthogonal; that is, they must rewrite \emph{overlapping} \index{factor}factors of their common source word. There are only finitely many such critical pairs, and they are all generated by inspecting the left-hand sides of our rewrite rules. We can completely characterize them with the below definition.
\begin{definition}\label{overlappingrules}
Two rules $(u_1, v_1)$ and $(u_2, v_2)$ \IndexDefinition{overlapping rules}\emph{overlap} if one of the following holds:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The left-hand side of the second rule is contained entirely within the left-hand side of the first. That is, $u_1=xyz$ and $u_2=y$ for some $x$, $y$, $z\in A^*$. If we write down the words $u_1$ and $u_2$ and shift $u_2$ over until they line up, we get this:
\begin{align*}
x&yz\\
&y
\end{align*}
\item The left-hand side of the second rule has a prefix equal to a suffix of the left-hand side of the first. That is, $u_1=xy$ and $u_2=yz$ for some $x$, $y$, $z\in A^*$, with $|x|>0$ and $|z|>0$. If we write down the words $u_1$ and $u_2$ and shift $u_2$ over until they line up, we get this:
\begin{align*}
x&y\\
&yz
\end{align*}
\end{enumerate}
The above are the two ways in which a non-orthogonal critical pair can rewrite the same word. When the case distinction is important, we can talk about an overlap of the \emph{first kind}, or \emph{second kind}, respectively. In both cases, after a suitable assignment of $x$, $y$ and $z$, we define the \IndexDefinition{overlap word}\emph{overlap word} to be $xyz$, and the \IndexDefinition{overlap position}\emph{overlap position} to be $|x|$.
\end{definition}

Now, let's look at some concrete examples where the words are Requirement Machine terms.

Consider these three rules:
\begin{gather*}
\rT\cdot\assocsym{Collection}{SubSequence}\cdot\protosym{Equatable}\tag{1}\\
\rT\cdot\assocsym{Collection}{SubSequence}\Red\rU\tag{2}\\
\assocsym{Collection}{SubSequence}\cdot\assocsym{Collection}{Element}\Red\assocsym{Collection}{Element}\tag{3}
\end{gather*}
There is an overlap of the first kind between (1) and (2) at position 0; the left-hand side of (2) is contained entirely in the left-hand side of (1). Here, the overlap term is $\rT\cdot\assocsym{Collection}{SubSequence}\cdot\protosym{Equatable}$:
\begin{align*}
&\rT\cdot\assocsym{Collection}{SubSequence}\cdot\protosym{Equatable}\\
&\rT\cdot\assocsym{Collection}{SubSequence}
\end{align*}
There is an overlap of the second kind between (1) and (3) at position 1; the left-hand side of (3) begins with the prefix $\assocsym{Collection}{SubSequence}$, which is also a suffix of the left-hand side of (1). The overlap term is $\rT\cdot\assocsym{Collection}{SubSequence}\cdot\protosym{Equatable}$:
\begin{align*}
\rT\cdot{}&\assocsym{Collection}{SubSequence}\\
&\assocsym{Collection}{SubSequence}\cdot\assocsym{Collection}{Element}
\end{align*}
The definition of an overlap of the second kind requires both $x$ and $z$ to be non-empty. If we relaxed this condition, then we would \emph{also} have an overlap of the second kind between rule (2) and (1) at position 0:
\begin{align*}

\end{align*}
The overlap term and position is the same as the first case we saw already, so attempting to \index{resolving critical pair}resolve this critical pair would not reveal anything new. We adjust our definition to avoid duplicated work in this situation.

\end{align*}

\end{align*}

\paragraph{Resolving critical pairs.}
A critical pair exhibits some word $t$ being rewritten in two distinct ways. If we take the destination term of each of the two \index{rewrite step!critical pair}rewrite steps, we get a pair of terms that are known to be equivalent to $t$, and each other. For an overlap of the first kind, the two words are $(v_1,\,xv_2z)$; for the second kind, $(v_1z,\,xv_2)$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cc}
Overlap of the first kind&
Overlap of the second kind\\
\begin{tikzcd}
&xyz\arrow[ld, Rightarrow, "(u_1\Red v_1)"', bend right]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow, "x(u_2\Red v_2)z", bend left]\\
v_1&&xv_2z
\end{tikzcd}&
\begin{tikzcd}
&xyz\arrow[ld, Rightarrow, "(u_1\Red v_1)z"', bend right]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow, "x(u_2\Red v_2)", bend left]\\
v_1z&&xv_2
\end{tikzcd}
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
 We \IndexDefinition{resolving critical pair}\emph{resolve} a critical pair $(t_1,t_2)$ by reducing both sides with the reduction relation as constructed so far and comparing the reduced words. There are four possible outcomes:
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $t_1$ and $t_2$ reduce to the same word $t'$, we say the \IndexDefinition{trivial critical pair}critical pair is \emph{trivial}. (Note that this terminology offers an alternate definition of local confluence: a reduction relation is locally confluent if all critical pairs are trivial.)
\item If $t_1\Red t_1'$ and $t_2\Red t_2'$ with $t_1'\neq t_2'$, we found two distinct \index{irreducible term}irreducible words in the same \index{equivalence class!of terms}equivalence class: a \index{confluence violation}\emph{confluence violation}. If $t_2' < t_1'$, we repair the confluence violation by adding a new rewrite rule $(t_1', t_2')$. Having done so, if we then define $t':=t_2'$, we once again see that both $t_1$ and $t_2$ reduce to the same word $t'$.

\item If instead $t_1'<t_2'$, we have the same situation except we resolve it by adding a new rewrite rule $(t_2', t_1')$, and we let $t':=t_1'$.

\item If $t_1'$ and $t_2'$ are distinct but incomparable, we have a \index{non-orientable relation}non-orientable relation, and we must report an error. This cannot happen under the reduction order used by the Requirement Machine.
\end{enumerate}
We can draw a diagram for each case, showing a \index{subgraph}subgraph of the \index{rewrite graph}rewrite graph; dashed arrows indicate new rules being added:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccc}
Trivial overlap&
Adding a new rule&
Adding a new rule\\
\begin{tikzcd}
&t\arrow[ld, Rightarrow, bend right]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow, bend left]\\
t_1\arrow[d, Rightarrow]&&t_2\arrow[d, Rightarrow]\\
\vphantom{P}\cdots\arrow[rd, Rightarrow, bend right]&&\vphantom{P}\cdots\arrow[ld, Rightarrow, bend left]\\
&t'
\end{tikzcd}&
\begin{tikzcd}
&t\arrow[ld, Rightarrow, bend right]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow, bend left]\\
t_1\arrow[d, Rightarrow]&&t_2\arrow[d, Rightarrow]\\
t_1'\arrow[rd, Rightarrow, dashed, bend right]&&\vphantom{P}\cdots\arrow[ld, Rightarrow, bend left]\\
&t'
\end{tikzcd}&
\begin{tikzcd}
&t\arrow[ld, Rightarrow, bend right]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow, bend left]\\
t_1\arrow[d, Rightarrow]&&t_2\arrow[d, Rightarrow]\\
\vphantom{P}\cdots\arrow[rd, Rightarrow, bend right]&&t_2'\arrow[ld, Rightarrow, bend left, dashed]\\
&t'
\end{tikzcd}
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
After adding a new rewrite rule if necessary, we have a pair of rewrite paths $p_1$ and $p_2$, and a word $t'$. Note that $\Src(p_1)=\Src(p_2)=t$ and $\Dst(p_1)=\Dst(p_2)=t'$, so both paths have the same source and destination. We say $p_1$ and $p_2$ are \IndexDefinition{parallel rewrite paths}\emph{parallel} rewrite paths.

The next algorithm is part of the inner loop of the completion procedure. It is convenient to view a \index{critical pair}critical pair as a rewrite path of length 2, rather than a pair of rewrite steps with the same source---we compose the first step with the inverse of the second.

\begin{algorithm}[Construct critical pair]\label{critical pair algo}
Takes two rules $(u, v)$ and $(u', v')$, together with an overlap position $i$ where $0\leq i<|u|$. Returns a triple $(t_1, t_2, p)$ where $t_1$ and $t_2$ are words, and $p$ is a rewrite path with $\Src(p)=t_1$ and $\Dst(p)=t_2$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $i+|u'|\leq|u|$, we have an overlap of the first kind; $u=xu' z$ for some $x$ and $z$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Let $x:=u[:i]$ (the prefix of $u$ of length $i$), and $z:=u[i+|u'|:]$ (the suffix of $u$ of length $|u|-|u'|-i$).
\item Let $t_1:=v$, the result of rewriting $u$ with the first rule.
\item Let $t_2:=xv' z$, the result of rewriting $u$ with the second rule.
\item Let $p:=(v\Red u)\circ x\WL(u'\Red v')\WR z$. This is a rewrite path from $t_1$ to $t_2$.
\end{enumerate}
\item Otherwise, we have an overlap of the second kind; $u=xy$ and $u'=yz$ for some $x$, $y$ and $z$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Let $x:=u[:i]$ (the prefix of $u$ of length $i$), $z:=u'[|u|-i:]$ (the suffix of $u'$ of length $|u'|-|u|+i$). (We don't actually need $y:=u[i:]=u'[:|u|-i]$.)
\item Let $t_1:=vz$, the result of rewriting $xyz$ with the first rule.
\item Let $t_2:=xv'$, the result of rewriting $xyz$ with the second rule.
\item Let $p:=(v\Red u)\WR z\circ x\WL(u'\Red v')$.
\end{enumerate}
\item Return the triple $(t_1, t_2, p)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{An optimization.}
Now that we know how to process a single overlap and resolve a critical pair, the next chunk of code concerns enumerating all candidate overlaps. If our rewrite rules were truly arbitrary, we would need to consider all possible combinations: for every rewrite rule $u_1\Red v_1$, for every rewrite rule $u_2\Red v_2$, and for every position $i<|u_1|$, we would need to check if the corresponding \index{factor}factors of $u_1$ and $u_2$ are identical. However, we can do better. The bottom-up construction of a requirement machine from protocol components, and the partition of rewrite rules into \index{imported rule}imported rules and \index{local rule}local rules, enables an optimization where overlaps between certain pairs of rules need not be considered at all:
\begin{itemize}
\item We don't need to look for overlaps between imported rules. While an imported rule can overlap with another imported rule, all such critical pairs are trivial and do not need to be resolved again.
\item We don't need to look for overlaps between an imported rule and a local rule. An imported rule cannot overlap with a local rule.
\end{itemize}
Only two interesting pairings remain:
\begin{itemize}
\item A local rule can overlap with another local rule.
\item A local rule can overlap with an imported rule.
\end{itemize}

We now proceed to prove that this is indeed the case. First, suppose two imported rules overlap. We will consider three possibilities in turn:
\begin{itemize}
\item There is an overlap of the first kind.
\item There is an overlap of the second kind, and the second rule's left-hand side starts with an associated type symbol.
\item There is an overlap of the second kind, and the second rule's left-hand side starts with a protocol symbol.
\end{itemize}
In all three cases, we will conclude that both rules either originate from the same \index{protocol component!completion}protocol component, or two distinct components where one imports the other. This implies that the \index{trivial critical pair}critical pair is now trivial, having been resolved by completion of that protocol component which contains the other.

In the first case, it is immediate that both rules were imported from the same protocol component for \tP:
\begin{align*}
&\aPA\cdot\assocsym{Q}{B}\\
&\aPA
\end{align*}
In the second case, \tP\ and \tQ\ are either in the same protocol component, or \tQ\ is a protocol dependency of \tP, because we have $\GP\vdash\ConfReq{Self.A}{Q}$:
\begin{align*}
\aPA\cdot{}&\assocsym{Q}{B}\\
&\assocsym{Q}{B}\cdot\assocsym{R}{C}
\end{align*}
The final case similarly implies that \tQ\ is a protocol dependency of \tP:
\begin{align*}
\aPA\cdot{}&\pQ\\
&\pQ\cdot\pR
\end{align*}

Next, we claim that imported rules cannot overlap with local rules. In a \index{query machine}query machine or \index{minimization machine}minimization machine for a generic signature, both sides of a local rule start with a \index{generic parameter symbol}generic parameter symbol. The left-hand side of an imported rule cannot start with, or contain, a generic parameter symbol, proving the claim. In a \index{protocol machine}requirement machine for a protocol, we can similarly rule out overlap of the first kind between an imported rule and a local rule, because the two rules must start with the same \index{protocol symbol}protocol or \index{associated type symbol}associated type symbol, and thus originate from the same protocol component. Now, suppose a protocol machine has an overlap of the second kind. We show that if the second rule, the one with left-hand side $\assocsym{Q}{B}\cdot\assocsym{R}{C}$, is a local rule, then the first rule is as well:
\begin{align*}
\aPA\cdot{}&\assocsym{Q}{B}\\
&\assocsym{Q}{B}\cdot\assocsym{R}{C}
\end{align*}
As before, \tQ\ must be a \index{protocol dependency graph}protocol dependency of \tP. But this time, we have the further assumption that \tQ\ belongs to the \emph{current} protocol component, so the appearance of \tP\ means that \tP\ must \emph{also} be a protocol dependency of \tQ. Thus, \tP\ and \tQ\ depend on each other, and are actually part of the same component; therefore both rules are local.

\paragraph{Another optimization.} If we fix a rule and position, we can find all overlaps involving this rule and position by performing a lookup into the \index{rule trie}rule \index{trie}trie, which we previously used to speed up the normal form algorithm in \SecRef{term reduction}. This further cuts down the work in enumerating overlaps. While the underlying data structure is the same, the lookup algorithm here differs from that used by term reduction; we must enumerate all matches, instead of stopping after the first one.

Consider a set of rewrite rules having the terms $a$, $ab$, $bc$, $bd$ and $acd$ as their left-hand sides. The rule with left-hand side $ab$ has overlaps with all other rules except for $acd$. Here is the rule trie, with thick borders denoting nodes associated with rewrite rules:
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}
  [level distance=10mm,
   every node/.style={fill=light-gray!60,circle,inner sep=1pt},
   level 1/.style={sibling distance=30mm},
   level 2/.style={sibling distance=20mm},
   level 3/.style={sibling distance=10mm}]
  \node [rounded corners, rectangle] {\strut root}
     child {node [thick, draw=black] {$\strut a$}
       child {node [thick, draw=black] {$\strut b$}}
       child {node {$\strut c$}
         child {node [thick, draw=black] {$\strut d$}}
       }
     }
     child {node {$\strut b$}
       child {node [thick, draw=black] {$\strut c$}}
       child {node [thick, draw=black] {$\strut d$}}
     };
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
When checking the left-hand side $ab$ for overlaps, we perform two lookups:
\begin{itemize}
\item At position 0, we look up $ab$. Starting from the root, we encounter the rule for $a$, followed by $ab$ (which is the left-hand side of our rule itself, so we skip it). The latter node is a leaf so we end the search.
\item At position 1, we look up $b$. The node for $b$ doesn't store a rewrite rule, however, it has child nodes. We've reached the end of our input sequence, so we recursively visit all children, and find the two final overlap candidates, $bc$ and $bd$.
\end{itemize}
This new kind of trie lookup can be thought of as a \index{coroutine}coroutine or an iterator, yielding zero or more results as the search proceeds. We implement it as a higher-order function taking a callback.

\begin{algorithm}[Overlap lookup in rule trie]\label{overlap trie lookup}
Takes a term $t$, an offset $i$ such that $0\leq i<|t|$, and a callback. For each rule $(u, v)$ where $t[i:]$ is a prefix of $u$ or $u$ is a prefix of $t[i:]$, invokes the callback with the rule $(u, v)$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item (Initialize) Let $N$ be the root node of the trie.
\item (End) If $i=|t|$, we've reached the end of the term. Perform a pre-order traversal of all child nodes of $N$, and for those children that have an associated rewrite rule, invoke the callback with that rule (this is the case where $t[i:]$ is a prefix of each~$u$).
\item (Traverse) Let $s_i$ be the $i$th symbol of $t$. Look up $s_i$ in $N$. If there is no such child node, return.
\item (Child) Otherwise, call this child node~$M$. If $M$ has an associated rule $u\Red v$, invoke the callback with this rule (in this case, $u$ is a prefix of $t[i:]$).
\item (Advance) Set $N \leftarrow M$. Set $i \leftarrow i+1$. Go back to Step~2.
\end{enumerate}
\end{algorithm}

The next algorithm feeds the results of \AlgRef{overlap trie lookup} into \AlgRef{critical pair algo} to build a list of all critical pairs among our rewrite rules. After \index{resolving critical pair}resolving these critical pairs, we will have to check for overlaps again, in case there are any involving the newly-added rules. To avoid repeated work,  the below algorithm maintains a set of visited overlaps, so that we can avoid building and resolving a critical pair we already know has been resolved.

\begin{algorithm}[Find overlapping rules]\label{find overlapping rule algo}
Takes a list of rewrite rules as input, and outputs a list of critical pairs. Also queries and updates a set of visited overlaps~$V$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item (Initialize) Set $i \leftarrow 0$. Let $n$ be the total number of local rules. Initialize the empty output list.
\item (Outer check) If $i=n$, we're done. Return the output list.
\item (Get rule) Let $(u,v)$ be the $i$th local rule. If this rule is marked as \index{left-simplified rule}\textbf{left-simplified}, \index{right-simplified rule}\textbf{right-simplified} or \index{substitution-simplified rule}\textbf{substitution-simplified}, skip it entirely and go to Step~7. Otherwise, set $ \leftarrow 0$.
\item (Inner check) If $j=|u|$, we're done. Go to Step~7.
\item (Find overlaps) Find all rules whose left-hand side is a prefix of the symbol range $u[j:]$ or vice versa, using \AlgRef{overlap trie lookup}. For each matching rule $(u', v')$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $(u', v')$ is marked as \textbf{left-simplified}, \textbf{right-simplified} or \textbf{substitution-simplified}, skip this rule.
\item If $((u, v),\,(u', v'),\,j)\in V$, skip this rule.
\item Otherwise, set $V\leftarrow V \cup \{((u, v),\,(u', v'),\,j)\}$, and build a critical pair for this overlap with \AlgRef{critical pair algo}. This returns a triple $(t_1, t_2, p)$; add it to the output list.
\end{enumerate}
\item (Inner loop) Set $j\leftarrow j+1$, and go back to Step~3.
\item (Outer loop) Set $i\leftarrow i+1$, and go back to Step~2.
\end{enumerate}
\end{algorithm}

We can now describe the main loop of the Knuth-Bendix completion procedure, which repeatedly finds and resolves critical pairs until no more non-trivial critical pairs remain. This process might not terminate, and we might find ourselves discovering new critical pairs and adding new rules to resolve them, forever. To prevent an infinite loop in the case of failure, we implement a \index{termination check}termination check; if we think we've done too much work already, we give up on constructing a finite complete rewriting system. We already mentioned the \textbf{left-simplified}, \textbf{right-simplified}, and \textbf{substitution-simplified} flags a few times; they are set by the rule simplification passes, with the first two described in \SecRef{sec:rule simplification} and the third one in \SecRef{subst simplification}. These passes are invoked at appropriate times in the main loop below.

\begin{algorithm}[Knuth-Bendix completion procedure]\label{knuthbendix} Takes a list of rewrite rules as input. Records new rewrite rules, as well as rewrite loops, and returns success or failure. On success, the rewrite rules define a finite complete rewriting system. Failure indicates we got a non-orientable rewrite rule, or we triggered the termination check.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Clear the flag.
\item Build a list of critical pairs with \AlgRef{find overlapping rule algo}.
\item Left-simplify all rules with \AlgRef{left simplification}.
\item Resolve each critical pair with \AlgRef{add rewrite rule}, and set the flag if any new rewrite rules were added.
\item Right-simplify all rewrite rules with \AlgRef{right simplification}.
\item Substitution-simplify all rewrite rules with \AlgRef{subst simplification algo}.
\item Check if the rule count, term length, or concrete nesting limits have been exceeded; if so, return failure.
\item If the flag was set, go back to Step~1. Otherwise, return success.
\end{enumerate}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Termination.} The \index{termination check}termination check is controlled by a handful of \index{frontend flag}frontend flags:
\begin{itemize}
\item \IndexFlag{requirement-machine-max-rule-count} \texttt{-requirement-machine-max-rule-count=<value>} sets the maximum number of local rules to \texttt{value}. Imported rules do not count toward this total, so this is hard to hit with realistic code. The default is a maximum of 4000 local rules.

\item \IndexFlag{requirement-machine-max-rule-length} \texttt{-requirement-machine-max-rule-length=<value>} sets the maximum length of the terms in a rule to \texttt{value}. To compute the actual quantity, we add the length of the longest \emph{user-written} rule; so the restriction in on the relative ``growth'' and not on the length of a type parameter written by the user. The default value is 12.

\item \IndexFlag{requirement-machine-max-concrete-nesting} \texttt{-requirement-machine-max-concrete-nesting=<value>} sets the upper bound on nested concrete types to \texttt{value}, to prevent \index{substitution simplification}substitution simplification from constructing an infinite type like \texttt{G<G<G<...>>>}. As with the limit on rule length, we add the maximum nesting depth of user-written rules to get the actual limit. The default value is 30.
\end{itemize}

While the first of the three is sufficient to detect non-termination, it takes a second or two for completion to record that many rules. The other two limits improve user experience in this case by rejecting clearly invalid programs sooner. The rule length limit being relative instead of just a total ban on terms of length 12 allows various pathological cases to succeed which would otherwise be needlessly rejected.

The following protocol, for example, presents the monoid $\mathbb{Z}_{14}$ and defines a rule of length 14, so the absolute rule length limit is really $14+12=26$. Completion does not add any longer rules so we accept it without issues:
\begin{Verbatim}
protocol Z14 {
  associatedtype A: Z14
    where Self == Self.A.A.A.A.A.A.A.A.A.A.A.A.A.A
}
\end{Verbatim}

If completion fails to build a rewriting system for \index{requirement minimization}minimization, we have a source location associated with some protocol or generic declaration. An error is diagnosed at this source location, and we proceed with minimization producing an empty list of requirements. If completion fails to build a rewriting system from an existing generic signature or \index{protocol component!completion}protocol component, there is no source location we can use for diagnostics; the compiler dumps the entire rewriting system so far, and aborts with a fatal error. The latter scenario is unusual; if we successfully constructed a generic signature from user-written requirements, we should be able perform completion on it again.

\paragraph{Debugging flags.}
A pair of debugging options can help us understand the operation of the completion procedure; both can be set together\footnote{With a single \texttt{-debug-requirement-machine=} flag, separating the subflags with commas.}, but be warned that they produce a large volume of output:
\begin{itemize}
\item \IndexTwoFlag{debug-requirement-machine}{completion} \texttt{-debug-requirement-machine=completion} will dump all overlapping rules and critical pairs.
\item \IndexTwoFlag{debug-requirement-machine}{add} \texttt{-debug-requirement-machine=add} will dump all rewrite rules and rewrite loops obtained while resolving critical pairs.
\end{itemize}

\section{Rule Simplification}\label{sec:rule simplification}

We impose two further conditions on our \index{complete presentation}\index{finite complete presentation}finite complete rewriting system:
\begin{enumerate}
\item No rule has a left-hand side that can be reduced by any other rule.
\item No rule has a right-hand side that can be reduced by any other rule.
\end{enumerate}
Such rewriting systems are called \IndexDefinition{left-reduced rewriting system}\emph{left-reduced} or \IndexDefinition{right-reduced rewriting system}\emph{right-reduced}, respectively, or simply \emph{reduced} if both conditions are met. Any finite complete rewriting system can be transformed into a reduced rewriting system with a pair of simplification passes that possibly delete and add rules.

In our implementation, we don't \emph{actually} delete rules, because we use the index of each rule as a stable reference elsewhere; instead, we set a pair of rule flags, \index{left-simplified rule}\textbf{left-simplified} and \index{right-simplified rule}\textbf{right-simplified}, and delete the rule from the \index{rule trie}\index{trie}rule trie. We've seen these flags mentioned already, so now we reveal their purpose. This will motivate the subsequent theory, setting the stage for the remaining two sections of this chapter.

\paragraph{Left simplification.} If the left-hand side of a rewrite rule $u_1\Red v_1$ can be reduced by another rewrite rule $u_2\Red v_2$, then $u_1=xu_2z$ for some $x$, $z\in A^*$, so we have an \index{overlapping rules}overlap of the first kind in the sense of \DefRef{overlappingrules}. Once we resolve all critical pairs, we don't need the first rule at all; we know that since $\Red$ is confluent, both ways of reducing the overlap term $u_1:=xu_2z$ produce the same result:
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzcd}
&u_1
\arrow[ld, Rightarrow, bend right, "(u_1\Red v_1)"']
\arrow[rd, Rightarrow, bend left, "x(u_2\Red v_2)z"]
\\
v_1\arrow[d, Rightarrow]&&xv_2z\arrow[d, Rightarrow]\\
\ldots\arrow[rd, Rightarrow, bend right]&&\ldots\arrow[ld, Rightarrow, bend left]\\
&t'&
\end{tikzcd}
\end{center}
The \IndexDefinition{left simplification}left simplification algorithm considers the left-hand side of each rule, and marks the rule if it finds a \index{factor}factor matching some other rule.
\begin{algorithm}[Left-simplify rules]\label{left simplification}
Takes the list of local rules as input. Has side effects.
\begin{enumerate}
\item (Initialize) Let $n$ be the total number of local rules, and set $i:=0$.
\item (Outer check) If $i=n$, return. Otherwise, say $(u, v)$ is the $i$th local rule, and set $j:=0$.
\item (Inner check) If $j=|u|$, go to Step~8.
\item (Search) Look up $u[:j]$ in the \index{rule trie}rule \index{trie}trie, where $u[:j]$ is the suffix of $u$ of length $|u|-j$.
\item (Decide) If the trie lookup returns no results, or it returns $(u, v)$ itself (which can only happen if $j=0$, so $u[:j]=u$), go to Step~7.
\item (Mark) Otherwise, the left-hand side of some other rule is a factor of $u$. Mark $u\Red v$ as \textbf{left-simplified} and go to Step~7.
\item (Inner loop) Set $j\leftarrow j+1$ and go back to Step~3.
\item (Outer loop) Set $i\leftarrow i+1$ and go back to Step~2.
\end{enumerate}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Right simplification.} The other case is when we have a rule $(u, v)$ whose right-hand side $v$ is not reduced, so $v\Red v'$ via some \index{positive rewrite path}positive rewrite path $p_v$. Now suppose we have a positive rewrite path $x(u\Red v)z\circ p$, where $\Dst(p)$ is an irreducible term $t'$. If we reduce $xuz$ to $xvz$ via $x(u\Red v)z$, we have two choices on how to proceed: we can follow $p$, or reduce $xvz$ to $xv' z$ via \index{whiskering}$x\WL p_v \WR z$. By confluence, the second choice must take us to $t'$ via a positive rewrite path $p'$ with $\Src(p')=xv' z$ and $\Dst(p')=t'$. Thus, we record a new rule $u\Red v'$ that obsoletes $u\Red v$.

The \IndexDefinition{right simplification}right simplification algorithm outputs a right-reduced rewriting system by attempting to reduce the right-hand side of each rewrite rule. While left simplification does not need to record new rewrite rules because completion has already resolved all overlaps of the first kind, right simplification actually records new rules as well as marking existing rules as having been simplified.

\begin{algorithm}[Right-simplify rules]\label{right simplification}
Takes the list of local rules as input. Has side effects.
\begin{enumerate}
\item (Initialize) Let $n$ be the total number of local rules, and set $i:=0$.
\item (Check) If $i=n$, return. Otherwise, let $(u, v)$ be the $i$th local rule.
\item (Reduce) Apply \AlgRef{term reduction trie algo} to $v$ to get a term $\tilde{v}$ and a rewrite path $p_v$. If $v=\tilde{v}$, the right-hand side $v$ is already reduced, so go to Step~7.
\item (Record) Invoke \AlgRef{add rewrite rule} to add a new rewrite rule $(u, \tilde{v})$.
\item (Mark) Mark the old rule as \textbf{right-simplified}.
\item (Loop) Increment $i$ and go back to Step~2.
\end{enumerate}
\end{algorithm}

Our justification for the validity of these passes worked from the assumption that we had a complete rewriting system; that is, that completion had already been performed. In practice, \AlgRef{knuthbendix} repeatedly runs both passes during completion, once per round of \index{critical pair}critical pair resolution. This is advantageous, because we can subsequently avoid considering overlaps that involve simplified rules. This strategy remains sound as long as we perform left simplification after computing critical pairs, but \emph{before} resolving them, which might add new rules. This narrows the candidates for left simplification to those rules whose overlaps have already been considered. As for the right simplification pass, it is actually fine to run at any time; we run it after \index{resolving critical pair}resolving critical pairs.

What we refer to as reduced rewriting systems are occasionally called ``normalized'', ``canonical'', or ``inter-reduced'' in the literature. The Requirement Machine also has a third \emph{substitution simplification} pass for rewriting system simplification. Substitution simplification reduces the substitution terms that appear in superclass, concrete type and concrete conformance symbols. We will discuss it in \ChapRef{chap:property map}.

\section{Associated Types}\label{sec:associated types}

A conformance rule $t\cdot\pP\Red t$ always overlaps with an associated type rule $\pP\cdot\nA\Red\aPA$, and resolving this critical pair defines a rule $t\cdot\nA\Red t\cdot\aPA$ (unless the right-hand side reduces further). These rewrite rules reduce those terms representing \index{unbound type parameter!in requirement machine}unbound type parameters to \index{bound type parameter!in requirement machine}bound type parameters. Thus, we will see how the bound and unbound type parameters from \SecRef{bound type params} manifest in our rewriting system.

\begin{example}\label{assoc type completion example}
We're going to look at this pair of protocol declarations, and the \index{protocol generic signature}protocol generic signature $\GP$:
\begin{Verbatim}
protocol Q {
  associatedtype B
}

protocol P {
  associatedtype A: Q
}
\end{Verbatim}

Protocol \tP\ has an \index{associated conformance requirement}associated conformance requirement $\AssocConfReq{Self.[P]A}{Q}{P}$, and $\GP$ has a conformance requirement $\ConfReq{\rT}{P}$, so the \index{protocol dependency graph}protocol dependency graph has the two edges $\GP\prec\tP$ and $\tP\prec\tQ$. We first build a rewriting system for \tQ. The rewriting system for $\GP$ imports rules from \tP\ and \tQ:
\begin{gather*}
\AssocIntro{Q}{B}\tag{1}\\
\midrule
\AssocIntro{P}{A}\tag{2}\\
\ProtoConf{\aPA}{Q}\tag{3}\\
\aPA\cdot\nB\Red\aPA\cdot\assocsym{Q}{B}\tag{\CRule{4}}\\
\midrule
\ProtoConf{\rT}{P}\tag{5}\\
\rT\cdot\nA\Red\rT\cdot\aPA\tag{\CRule{6}}\\
\end{gather*}
We can categorize the above rules as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item Associated type rules: (1) and (2).
\item Conformance requirements: (3) and (5).
\item Rules added by completion are indicated with an asterisk: (\CRule{4}) and (\CRule{6}).
\end{itemize}
We omit the identity conformance rules $\ProtoConf{\pQ}{Q}$ and $\ProtoConf{\pP}{P}$; in this example they would only clutter the presentation. They will serve a useful purpose later, in \ExRef{proto assoc rule}.

We now go through the construction step by step. Protocol \tQ\ does not depend on any other protocols. The rewriting system for \tQ\ is just rule (1):
\begin{gather*}
\AssocIntro{Q}{B}\tag{1}
\end{gather*}
Protocol \tP\ imports the single rule of \tQ, and adds rules (2) and (3):
\begin{gather*}
\AssocIntro{P}{A}\tag{2}\\
\ProtoConf{\aPA}{Q}\tag{3}
\end{gather*}

We need to check the left-hand side of rules (2) and (3) for overlaps with other rules. Rule (2) does not overlap with any other rules. Rule (3) overlaps with rule (1) on the term $\aPA\cdot\pQ\cdot\nB$:
\begin{align*}
\aPA\cdot{}&\pQ\\
&\pQ\cdot\nB
\end{align*}
Rule (3) is local to \tP, while (1) was imported from \tQ. The two sides of the critical pair reduce to $\aPA\cdot\nB$ and $\aPA\cdot\assocsym{Q}{B}$. Resolving this critical pair introduces rule (\CRule{4}):
\[
\aPA\cdot\nB\Red\aPA\cdot\assocsym{Q}{B}\tag{\CRule{4}}
\]
We also record a rewrite loop that defines rule (\CRule{4}) via rules (3) and (1):
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzcd}
&\aPA\cdot\pQ\cdot\nB
\arrow[ld, Rightarrow, bend right, "(\ProtoConf{\aPA}{Q}).\nB"']\\
\aPA\cdot\nB\arrow[rr, Rightarrow, bend right, dashed, "(\aPA\cdot\nB\Red\aPA\cdot\assocsym{Q}{B})"']
&&
\aPA\cdot\assocsym{Q}{B}\arrow[lu, Rightarrow, bend right, "\aPA\cdot(\AssocIntroInv{Q}{B})"']
\end{tikzcd}
\end{center}
Once again, we check for overlaps in the left-hand sides of our local rules---now (2), (3) and (4). We see that no more critical pairs remain, and we have a finite complete rewriting system for \tP.

Finally, we build the rewriting system for $\GP$. We import all rules from \tQ\ and \tP, and add one new local rule corresponding to the conformance requirement $\ConfReq{\rT}{P}$:
\[
\ProtoConf{\rT}{P}\tag{5}
\]
We need to check the left-hand side of rule (5) for overlaps with other rules. Indeed, rule (5) overlaps with rule (2) on the term
$\rT\cdot\pP\cdot\nA$:
\begin{align*}
\rT\cdot{}&\pP\\
&\pP\cdot\nA
\end{align*}
Resolving this critical pair introduces rule (\CRule{6}):
\[
\rT\cdot\nA\Red\rT\cdot\aPA\tag{\CRule{6}}
\]
We also record a rewrite loop that defines rule (\CRule{6}) via rules (5) and (2):
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzcd}
&\rT\cdot\pP\cdot\nA\arrow[ld, Rightarrow, "(\ProtoConf{\rT}{P})\cdot\nA"', bend right]\\
\rT\cdot\nA\arrow[rr, Rightarrow, "(\rT\cdot\nA\Red\rT\cdot\aPA)"', bend right, dashed]&&
\rT\cdot\aPA\arrow[ul, Rightarrow, "\rT\cdot(\AssocIntroInv{P}{A})"', bend right]
\end{tikzcd}
\end{center}
At this point, there might be overlaps involving one of rule (5) or (\CRule{6}); a quick check shows none remain, so we have a complete rewriting system for $\GP$.

Now, we get to the interesting part. Consider these two type parameters of $\GP$, and their corresponding terms:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The unbound type parameter \texttt{\rT.A.B}, and term $\rT\cdot\nA\cdot\nB$.
\item The bound type parameter \texttt{\rT.[P]A.[Q]B}, and term $\rT\cdot\aPA\cdot\assocsym{Q}{B}$.
\end{enumerate}
The second term is reduced, so the first term must reduce to the second term. Term reduction outputs a \index{positive rewrite path}positive rewrite path $p$ with $\Src(p)=\rT\cdot\nA\cdot\nB$ and $\Dst(p)=\rT\cdot\aPA\cdot\assocsym{Q}{B}$. This path involves rules (4) and (6) added by completion:
\[
p := (\rT\cdot\nA\Red\rT\cdot\aPA)\cdot\nB\circ \rT\cdot(\aPA\cdot\nB\Red\aPA\cdot\assocsym{B}{Q})
\]
Here is a diagram for $p$:
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=huge]
\rT\cdot\nA\cdot\nB\arrow[r, Rightarrow]&
\rT\cdot\aPA\cdot\nB\arrow[r, Rightarrow]&
\rT\cdot\aPA\cdot\assocsym{Q}{B}
\end{tikzcd}
\]
There is a ``telescoping'' effect, as term reduction processes the name symbols from left to right, replacing them with associated type symbols. As an aside, if we have an \emph{invalid} type parameter, such as \texttt{\rT.A.A}, the term reduces to $\rT\cdot\aPA\cdot\nA$ but no further; the final name symbol \nA\ cannot be ``resolved'' because the type parameter \texttt{\rT.A} does not \emph{have} a member type named \nA.

The \index{standard term}standard term $\rT\cdot\nA\cdot\nB$ reduces to $\rT\cdot\aPA\cdot\assocsym{Q}{B}$ because the unbound type parameter \texttt{\rT.A.B} is equivalent to the bound type parameter \texttt{\rT.[P]A.[Q]B}, and the latter is the reduced type of the former. The equivalence follows because we can derive a same-type requirement, and the left-hand side of this same-type requirement is the reduced type because no smaller type parameter can be shown to be equivalent:
\[\GP\vdash\SameReq{\rT.[P]A.[Q]B}{\rT.A.B}\]
Here is one possible derivation for this same-type requirement:
\begin{gather*}
\ConfStep{\rT}{P}{1}\\
\AssocBindStep{1}{\rT.[P]A}{\rT.A}{2}\\
\SymStep{2}{\rT.A}{\rT.[P]A}{3}\\
\AssocConfStep{1}{\rT.[P]A}{Q}{4}\\
\SameNameStep{3}{4}{\rT.A.B}{\rT.[P]A.B}{5}\\
\SymStep{5}{\rT.[P]A.B}{\rT.A.B}{6}\\
\AssocBindStep{4}{\rT.[P]A.[Q]B}{\rT.[P]A.B}{7}\\
\TransStep{7}{6}{\rT.[P]A.[Q]B}{\rT.A.B}{8}
\end{gather*}

By \ThmRef{derivation to path swift}, we can transform this derivation into a rewrite path from $\rT\cdot\nA\cdot\nB$ to $\rT\cdot\aPA\cdot\assocsym{Q}{B}$, involving the initial rewrite rules only. We call this path~$p'$:
\begin{multline*}
p' := (\ProtoConfInv{\rT}{P})\cdot\nA\cdot\nB \circ \rT\cdot(\AssocIntro{P}{A})\cdot\nB\\
\circ \rT\cdot(\ProtoConfInv{\aPA}{Q})\cdot\nB \circ \rT\cdot\aPA\cdot(\AssocIntro{Q}{B})
\end{multline*}
Unlike $p$, $p'$ is \emph{not} a positive rewrite path, because the first and third steps are negative; each one applies a conformance rule backwards. We can visualize $p'$ as a path in the rewrite graph, with the \index{negative rewrite step}negative \index{rewrite step}rewrite steps going up:
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=-5pt]
&\rT\cdot\pP\cdot\nA\cdot\nB\arrow[rd, Rightarrow, bend left]
&&\rT\cdot\aPA\cdot\pQ\cdot\nB\arrow[rd, Rightarrow, bend left]\\
\rT\cdot\nA\cdot\nB\arrow[ru, Rightarrow, bend left]&&
\rT\cdot\aPA\cdot\nB\arrow[ru, Rightarrow, bend left]&&
\rT\cdot\aPA\cdot\assocsym{Q}{B}
\end{tikzcd}
\end{center}

We now have two \index{parallel rewrite paths}parallel rewrite paths: $p$ (output by the normal form algorithm), and $p'$ (constructed from our derivation). Their composition $p'\circ p^{-1}$ is a rewrite loop; we can represent it as a diagram, with rules (\CRule{4}) and (\CRule{6}) indicated by dashed arrows:
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=-5pt]
&\rT\cdot\pP\cdot\nA\cdot\nB\arrow[ld, Rightarrow, bend right]
&&\rT\cdot\aPA\cdot\pQ\cdot\nB\arrow[ld, Rightarrow, bend right]\\
\rT\cdot\nA\cdot\nB\arrow[rr, Rightarrow, bend right, dashed]&&
\rT\cdot\aPA\cdot\nB\arrow[lu, Rightarrow, bend right]\arrow[rr, Rightarrow, bend right, dashed]&&
\rT\cdot\aPA\cdot\assocsym{Q}{B}\arrow[lu, Rightarrow, bend right]
\end{tikzcd}
\end{center}
Recall the two rewrite loops we recorded in completion. We take the first rewrite loop, and \index{whiskering}whisker it on the right by \nB; then, we whisker the second rewrite loop on the left by \rT. Now, the two rewrite loops visit the common term $\rT\cdot\aPA\cdot\nB$. Informally, we can ``glue'' them together at this point, and then we get $p'\circ p^{-1}$.

In fact, given any two parallel rewrite paths in a \index{complete presentation}\index{finite complete presentation}finite complete rewriting system, we can always ``tile'' the two-dimensional space between them if we take the finite set of rewrite loops generated by \index{resolving critical pair}resolving critical pairs, and glue them together along common edges or vertices after putting the loops ``in context'' via whiskering. We will develop this idea further in \ChapRef{chap:minimization}, but here is a thought to ruminate on in the meantime:
\begin{quote}
Words, generated by a finite set of symbols, are the zero-dimensional objects in the rewrite graph. Rewrite paths, generated by a finite set of rewrite rules, define an equivalence relation on words; they are the one-dimensional objects. Rewrite loops, generated by a finite set of critical pairs, define an equivalence relation on paths; they are the two-dimensional objects.
\end{quote}
An equivalence relation on paths is called a \emph{homotopy relation}.
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{overlap of first kind example}
Let's build on our previous example and look at how unbound type parameters reduce when they appear in requirements. We add a new protocol \tR, and declare a \index{constrained extension}constrained \index{protocol extension}protocol extension of \tP\ where \texttt{Self.A.B} conforms to \tR:
\begin{Verbatim}
protocol R {}

protocol Q {
  associatedtype B
}

protocol P {
  associatedtype A: Q
}

extension P where Self.A.B: R {}
\end{Verbatim}
When type checking these declarations, we need to build a generic signature for this \index{protocol extension}protocol extension. We start with requirement $\ConfReq{\rT}{P}$ from the \index{extended type}extended type, and add the user-written requirement $\ConfReq{\rT.A.B}{R}$. The rewriting system looks like the previous example but with two new rules:
\begin{gather*}
\ProtoConf{\rT\cdot\nA\cdot\nB}{R}\tag{7}\\
\ProtoConf{\rT\cdot\aPA\cdot\assocsym{Q}{B}}{R}\tag{8*}
\end{gather*}
Rule (7) corresponds to the user-written requirement. Rule (\CRule{8}) is added by completion, resolving the overlap between rule (7) and (6) on the term $\rT\cdot\nA\cdot\nB\cdot\pR$:
\begin{align*}
&\rT\cdot\nA\cdot\nB\cdot\pR\\
&\rT\cdot\nA
\end{align*}
We record a rewrite loop defining rule (\CRule{8}) via rule (4), (6) and (7):
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzcd}
&\rT\cdot\nA\cdot\nB\cdot\pR
\arrow[ld, Rightarrow, bend right, "(\rT\cdot\nA\Red\rT\cdot\aPA)\cdot\nB\cdot\pR"']\\
\rT\cdot\aPA\cdot\nB\cdot\pR
\arrow[dd, Rightarrow, "\rT.(\aPA\cdot\nB\Red\aPA\cdot\assocsym{Q}{B})\cdot\pR"]&&
\rT\cdot\nA\cdot\nB
\arrow[lu, Rightarrow, bend right, "(\ProtoConfInv{\rT\cdot\nA\cdot\nB}{R})"']
\\
&&
\rT\cdot\aPA\cdot\nB
\arrow[u, Rightarrow, "(\rT\cdot\aPA\Red\rT\cdot\nA)\cdot\nB"']
\\
\rT\cdot\aPA\cdot\assocsym{Q}{B}\cdot\pR
\arrow[rr, Rightarrow, dashed, bend right, "(\ProtoConf{\rT\cdot\aPA\cdot\assocsym{Q}{B}}{R})"']
&&
\rT\cdot\aPA\cdot\assocsym{Q}{B}
\arrow[u, Rightarrow, "\rT\cdot(\aPA\cdot\assocsym{Q}{B}\Red\aPA\cdot\nB)"']&&
\end{tikzcd}
\end{center}
Note that this was an overlap of the first kind, so rule (7) is now marked \index{left-simplified rule}\textbf{left-simplified}. Thus, rule (\CRule{8}) completely supersedes rule (7). Rule (\CRule{8}) survives minimization and maps to the requirement $\ConfReq{\rT.[P]A.[Q]B}{R}$, which appears in the generic signature that we output for this protocol extension:
\begin{quote}
\begin{verbatim}
<τ_0_0 where τ_0_0: P, τ_0_0.[P]A.[Q]B: R>
\end{verbatim}
\end{quote}

If our compiler invocation is generating a \index{serialized module}serialized module, we serialize the \index{protocol extension}protocol extension's generic signature and do not compute it again when this module is imported in the future. We might still need a rewriting system for generic signature queries though, in which case we will build a ``second-generation'' rewriting system:
\begin{enumerate}
\item First, we build a complete rewriting system from the user-written requirements of the protocol extension.
\item After finding a minimal set of rules, we convert minimal rules to requirements, and serialize the resulting generic signature.
\item In a subsequent compiler invocation, we deserialize this generic signature.
\item Then, we build a complete rewriting system from the requirements of this generic signature; rule (8) is now one of our initial rules.
\end{enumerate}

The rewriting system after step (1) is equivalent to that after step (4). That is, if we ignore \textbf{left-simplified} and \textbf{right-simplified} rules, both will have the same rules, up to permutation. (We can't completely prove this fact, because \index{requirement minimization}minimization is tricky. But this is the intended invariant here.)
\end{example}

The conformance requirement $\ConfReq{\rT.A.B}{R}$ in \texttt{where} clause of our protocol extension becomes redundant as a result, because every \nB\ (of a type conforming to \tQ) now conforms to \tR:

\begin{example}\label{two protocols same assoc}
Our next goal is to understand what happens when a type parameter conforms to two unrelated protocols that both declare an associated type with the same name:
\begin{Verbatim}
protocol P1 {
  associatedtype A
}

protocol P2 {
  associatedtype A
}
\end{Verbatim}
We're going to look at this generic signature:
\begin{quote}
\texttt{<\rT\ where \rT:~P1, \rT:~P2>}
\end{quote}
Every \index{equivalence class!of type parameters}equivalence class of type parameters can be uniquely identified by some \index{unbound type parameter!in requirement machine}unbound type parameter. In the above, the type parameters \texttt{\rT.[P1]A} and \texttt{\rT.[P2]A} must therefore be equivalent to \texttt{\rT.A}. We're going to see how this works out. Here is the complete rewriting system for the above generic signature:
\begin{gather*}
\AssocIntro{P1}{A}\tag{1}\\
\midrule
\AssocIntro{P2}{A}\tag{2}\\
\midrule
\ProtoConf{\rT}{P1}\tag{3}\\
\ProtoConf{\rT}{P2}\tag{4}\\
\rT\cdot\nA\Red\rT\cdot\assocsym{P1}{A}\tag{\CRule{5}}\\
\rT\cdot\assocsym{P2}{A}\Red\rT\cdot\assocsym{P1}{A}\tag{\CRule{6}}
\end{gather*}
Rules (1) and (2) are imported from \texttt{P1} and \texttt{P2}, and rules (3) and (4) correspond to the conformance requirements $\ConfReq{\rT}{P1}$ and $\ConfReq{\rT}{P2}$. Completion also adds two additional rules. Rule (\CRule{5}) is added when resolving the overlap between rule (3) and rule (1), exactly as in \ExRef{assoc type completion example}.

Rule (4) overlaps with rule (2) on the term $\rT\cdot\protosym{P2}\cdot\nA$:
\begin{align*}
\rT.&\protosym{P2}\\
&\protosym{P2}\cdot\nA
\end{align*}
This gives us rule (\CRule{6}), which has a new form we haven't seen before. One side of the critical pair reduces to $\rT\cdot\assocsym{P2}{A}$, and the other reduces to $\rT\cdot\assocsym{P1}{A}$ via rule (5).

We record a rewrite loop defining rule (\CRule{6}) via (2), (4) and (5):
\[
\FourLoopDerived%
{\rT\cdot\protosym{P2}\cdot\nA}%
{\rT\cdot\assocsym{P2}{A}}%
{\rT\cdot\assocsym{P1}{A}}%
{\rT\cdot\nA}%
{\rT\cdot(\AssocIntro{P2}{A})}%
{(\rT\cdot\assocsym{P2}{A}\Red \rT\cdot\assocsym{P1}{A})}%
{(\rT\cdot\assocsym{P1}{A}\Red \rT\cdot\nA)}%
{(\ProtoConf{\rT}{P2}}
\]

We can ``glue'' the rewrite loops defining rule (\CRule{5}) and (\CRule{6}) together into a single diagram:
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
&\rT\cdot\protosym{P2}\cdot\nA\arrow[ld, Rightarrow, bend right]&\\
\rT\cdot\assocsym{P2}{A}\arrow[rd, Rightarrow, bend right, dashed]&&\rT\cdot\nA\arrow[lu, Rightarrow, bend right]\\
&\rT\cdot\assocsym{P1}{A}\arrow[ru, Rightarrow, bend right, dashed]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow, bend right]&\\
&&\rT\cdot\protosym{P1}\cdot\nA\arrow[uu, Rightarrow, bend right]
\end{tikzcd}
\]
The two dashed arrows are rules (\CRule{5}) and (\CRule{6}). The solid arrows indicate a rewrite path joining $\rT\cdot\assocsym{P1}{A}$ with $\rT\cdot\assocsym{P2}{A}$ involving the initial rules only. This rewrite path corresponds to the following derivation:
\begin{gather*}
\ConfStep{\rT}{P1}{1}\\
\AssocBindStep{1}{\rT.[P1]A}{\rT.A}{2}\\
\ConfStep{\rT}{P2}{3}\\
\AssocBindStep{3}{\rT.[P2]A}{\rT.A}{4}\\
\SymStep{4}{\rT.A}{\rT.[P2]A}{5}\\
\TransStep{2}{5}{\rT.[P1]A}{\rT.[P2]A}{6}
\end{gather*}
Indeed, we see that the equivalence class with reduced type \texttt{\rT.[P1]A} contains three type parameters. Here they are with corresponding terms, in type parameter order. In this case, the reduced type and the reduced term coincide:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\textbf{Type}&\textbf{Term}\\
\toprule
\texttt{\rT.[P1]A}&$\rT\cdot\assocsym{P1}{A}$\\
\texttt{\rT.[P2]A}&$\rT\cdot\assocsym{P2}{A}$\\
\texttt{\rT.A}&$\rT\cdot\nA$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{example}

\section{More Critical Pairs}\label{sec:more critical pairs}

\begin{example}\label{protocol inheritance completion example}
Consider this protocol inheritance hierarchy, where \texttt{Bot} inherits from \texttt{Mid}, which inherits from \texttt{Top}, and \texttt{Mid} declares an associated type:
\begin{Verbatim}
protocol Top {}

protocol Mid: Top {
  associatedtype A
}

protocol Bot: Mid {}
\end{Verbatim}
We're going to examine the rewriting system for the generic signature $G_\texttt{Bot}$, made up rules imported from \texttt{Mid} and \texttt{Bot}, and the local rules (as before, we omit the identity conformance rules):
\begin{gather*}
\AssocIntro{Mid}{A}\tag{1}\\
\ProtoInherit{Mid}{Top}\tag{2}\\
\midrule
\AssocIntro{Bot}{A}\tag{3}\\
\ProtoInherit{Bot}{Mid}\tag{4}\\
\protosym{Bot}\cdot\assocsym{Mid}{A}\Red\assocsym{Bot}{A}\tag{\CRule{5}}\\
\ProtoInherit{Bot}{Top}\tag{\CRule{6}}\\
\midrule
\ProtoConf{\rT}{Bot}\tag{7}\\
\ProtoConf{\rT}{Mid}\tag{\CRule{8}}\\
\ProtoConf{\rT}{Top}\tag{\CRule{9}}\\
\rT\cdot\nA\Red\rT\cdot\assocsym{Bot}{A}\tag{\CRule{10}}\\
\rT\cdot\assocsym{Mid}{A}\Red\rT\cdot\assocsym{Bot}{A}\tag{\CRule{11}}
\end{gather*}
We're going to focus on a handful of interesting rules:
\begin{itemize}
\item While \texttt{Bot} does not declare any associated types, it inherits \nA\ from \texttt{Mid}; rule (3) is the \index{associated type rule}associated type rule for \nA.
\item Rules (\CRule{5}) and (\CRule{11}) are added by completion as are a consequence of the associated type rule.
\item Rules (\CRule{6}), (\CRule{8}) and (\CRule{9}) are also from completion. They express the ``transitive'' conformance requirements $\ConfReq{Self}{Top}_\texttt{Bottom}$, $\ConfReq{\rT}{Top}$ and $\ConfReq{\rT}{Mid}$.
\end{itemize}

Rule (4) overlaps with rule (1) on the term $\protosym{Bot}\cdot\protosym{Mid}\cdot\nA$:
\begin{align*}
\protosym{Bot}\cdot{}&\protosym{Mid}\\
&\protosym{Mid}\cdot\nA
\end{align*}
Resolving this critical pair records a rewrite loop defining rule (\CRule{5}) via (1), (3) and (4):
\begin{center}
\FourLoopDerived%
{\protosym{Bot}\cdot\protosym{Mid}\cdot\nA}%
{\protosym{Bot}\cdot\assocsym{Mid}{A}}%
{\assocsym{Bot}{A}}%
{\protosym{Bot}\cdot\nA}%
{\protosym{Bot}\cdot(\AssocIntro{Mid}{A})}%
{(\protosym{Bot}\cdot\assocsym{Mid}{A}\Red\assocsym{Bot}{A})}%
{(\AssocIntroInv{Bot}{A})}%
{(\ProtoInheritInv{Bot}{Mid})\cdot\nA}
\end{center}
Rule (\CRule{5}) says, if a term conforming to \texttt{Bot} is followed by $\assocsym{Mid}{A}$, we can reduce it to $\assocsym{Bot}{A}$.

Rule (4) overlaps with rule (3) on the term $\protosym{Bot}\cdot\protosym{Mid}\cdot\protosym{Top}$:
\begin{align*}
\protosym{Bot}\cdot{}&\protosym{Mid}\\
&\protosym{Mid}\cdot\protosym{Top}
\end{align*}
Resolving this critical pair records a rewrite loop defining rule (\CRule{6}) via rule (2) and (4):
\begin{center}
\FourLoopDerived%
{\protosym{Bot}\cdot\protosym{Mid}\cdot\protosym{Top}}%
{\protosym{Bot}\cdot\protosym{Top}}%
{\protosym{Bot}}%
{\protosym{Bot}\cdot\protosym{Mid}}%
{(\ProtoInherit{Bot}{Mid}).\protosym{Top}}%
{(\ProtoInherit{Bot}{Top})}%
{(\ProtoInheritInv{Bot}{Mid})}%
{\protosym{Bot}\cdot(\ProtoInheritInv{Mid}{Top})}
\end{center}
Rule (\CRule{6}) says, if a term conforms to \texttt{Bot}, it also conforms to \texttt{Top}. In general, after completion, rewrite rules of the form $\pP\cdot\pQ\Red\pP$ will encode the \index{transitive closure}transitive closure of the \index{inherited protocol}protocol inheritance relation.

This completes the rewriting system for \texttt{Bot}. Moving on to $G_\texttt{Bot}$, we see that rule (7) overlaps with rule (4) on the term $\rT\cdot\protosym{Bot}\cdot\protosym{Mid}$:
\begin{align*}
\rT\cdot{}&\protosym{Bot}\\
&\protosym{Bot}\cdot\protosym{Mid}
\end{align*}

\pagebreak

\noindent Resolving this critical pair defines rule (\CRule{8}) via rules (4), (7) and (\CRule{8}):
\begin{center}
\FourLoopDerived%
{\rT\cdot\protosym{Bot}\cdot\protosym{Mid}}%
{\rT\cdot\protosym{Mid}}%
{\rT}%
{\rT\cdot\protosym{Bot}}%
{(\ProtoConf{\rT}{Bot})\cdot\protosym{Mid}}%
{(\ProtoConf{\rT}{Mid})}%
{(\ProtoConfInv{\rT}{Bot})}%
{\rT\cdot(\ProtoInheritInv{Bot}{Mid})}
\end{center}
Rule (\CRule{8}) is the derived requirement $\ConfReq{\rT}{Mid}$. Rule (7) overlaps with rule (5) in the same way, defining rule (*9), which is the derived requirement $\ConfReq{\rT}{Top}$. Once again, we see the transitive closure of the protocol inheritance relation being computed.

Rule (7) overlaps with rule (3) on the term $\rT\cdot\protosym{Bot}\cdot\nA$, defining rule (*10), by the same general principle as in \ExRef{assoc type completion example}.

Finally, rule (\CRule{8}) overlaps with rule (1) on the term $\rT\cdot\protosym{Mid}\cdot\nA$:
\begin{align*}
\rT\cdot{}&\protosym{Mid}\\
&\protosym{Mid}\cdot\nA
\end{align*}

Resolving this critical pair defines rule (\CRule{11}) via rule (1), (3) and (\CRule{8}):
\begin{center}
\FourLoopDerived%
{\rT\cdot\protosym{Mid}\cdot\nA}%
{\rT\cdot\assocsym{Mid}{A}}%
{\rT\cdot\assocsym{Bot}{A}}%
{\rT\cdot\nA}%
{\rT\cdot(\AssocIntro{Mid}{A})}%
{(\rT\cdot\assocsym{Mid}{A}\Red\rT\cdot\assocsym{Bot}{A})}%
{\rT\cdot(\AssocIntroInv{Bot}{A})}%
{(\ProtoConfInv{\rT}{Mid}).\nA}
\end{center}

In this example, the relationship between \index{type parameter}type parameters and \index{term!in requirement machine}terms is more subtle than we've previously seen, because the two distinct \index{associated type symbol}associated type symbols $\assocsym{Mid}{A}$ and $\assocsym{Bot}{A}$ both correspond to the same associated type \emph{declaration}. On one hand, the \index{equivalence class!of terms}equivalence class of \texttt{\rT.[Mid]A} contains one other type parameter, \texttt{\rT.A}. However, we have \emph{three} equivalent terms: $\rT\cdot\assocsym{Mid}{A}$, $\rT\cdot\assocsym{Bot}{A}$, and $\rT\cdot\nA$. Not only that, but applying \AlgRef{build term generic} to the \index{reduced type parameter}reduced type parameter \texttt{\rT.[Mid]A} outputs $\rT\cdot\assocsym{Mid}{A}$, which is \emph{not} \index{irreducible term}irreducible; the reduced term here is $\rT\cdot\assocsym{Bot}{A}$, because $\assocsym{Bot}{A}<\assocsym{Mid}{A}$ in the \index{reduction order!in requirement machine}reduction order (\AlgRef{protocol reduction order}):
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\textbf{Type}&\textbf{Term}\\
\toprule
\texttt{\rT.[Mid]A}&$\rT\cdot\assocsym{Bot}{A}$\\
&$\rT\cdot\assocsym{Mid}{A}$\\
\texttt{\rT.A}&$\rT\cdot\nA$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Now, suppose we change the declaration of \texttt{Bot} to \emph{re-state} the associated type:
\begin{Verbatim}
protocol Bot: Mid {
  associatedtype A
}
\end{Verbatim}
The rewriting system is identical; we now call rule (3) an associated type rule instead of an \emph{inherited} associated type rule, but the rule remains the same. The equivalence class of \texttt{\rT.A} has three type parameters and three terms, because now every associated type symbol corresponds to a declaration:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\textbf{Type}&\textbf{Term}\\
\toprule
\texttt{\rT.[Bot]A}&$\rT\cdot\assocsym{Bot}{A}$\\
\texttt{\rT.[Mid]A}&$\rT\cdot\assocsym{Mid}{A}$\\
\texttt{\rT.A}&$\rT\cdot\nA$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

We defined \AlgRef{associated type order} of the type parameter order so that a \index{root associated type}root associated type declaration always precedes such a re-stated associated type declaration; therefore the reduced type parameter of our equivalence class remains \texttt{\rT.[Mid]A}. The reduced term is also unchanged, $\rT\cdot\assocsym{Bot}{A}$. This means that re-stating an associated type (or removing a re-stated associated type) does not change either the rewriting system or reduced type relation, and in particular, has no effect on calling convention, witness table layout, or any other aspect of the \index{ABI}ABI.

Note that because the correspondence is not immediate, the mapping of terms to type parameters must be defined in such a way to output a reduced type parameter given a reduced term, by a careful choice of associated type declaration at each step. This will be explained in \SecRef{implqueries}. This minor complication could be avoided if it weren't for inherited associated type symbols, and the fact that the reduction order on associated type symbols differs from the type parameter order on associated type declarations. However, both of those behaviors have important redeeming qualities, as we will see in \SecRef{sec:recursive conformances redux}.
\end{example}

We now ask if \texttt{f()} type checks; is \texttt{T.C.B} equivalent to \texttt{T.A}?

  associatedtype A
  associatedtype B
  associatedtype C: S where Self == Self.C.C, Self.B == Self.C.A

func f<T: S>(val: T.C.B) {
  let val2: T.A = val
}
\end{Verbatim}
Our protocol states three associated requirements:
\begin{gather*}
\AssocConfReq{Self.C}{S}{S}\\
\AssocSameReq{Self}{Self.C.C}{S}\\
\AssocSameReq{Self.B}{Self.C.A}{S}
\end{gather*}
A consequence of the two same-type requirements is that \texttt{\rT.A} is equivalent to \texttt{\rT.C.C.A}, which is equivalent to \texttt{\rT.C.B}. Here is the full derivation:
\begin{gather*}
\ConfStep{\rT}{S}{1}\\
\AssocConfStep{1}{\rT.C}{S}{2}\\
\AssocSameStep{2}{\rT.C.B}{\rT.C.C.A}{3}\\
\AssocSameStep{1}{\rT}{\rT.C.C}{4}\\
\SymStep{4}{\rT.C.C}{\rT}{5}\\
\SameNameStep{1}{5}{\rT.C.C.A}{\rT.A}{6}\\
\TransStep{3}{6}{\rT.C.B}{\rT.A}{7}
\end{gather*}
Our generic signature defines four infinite equivalence classes:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|l|l|l}
\toprule
\texttt{\rT}&\texttt{\rT.A}&\texttt{\rT.C}&\texttt{\rT.B}\\
\texttt{\rT.C.C}&\texttt{\rT.C.B}&\texttt{\rT.C.C.C}&\texttt{\rT.C.A}\\
\texttt{\rT.C.C.C.C}&\texttt{\rT.C.C.A}&\texttt{\rT.C.C.C.C.C}&\texttt{\rT.C.C.B}\\

\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Now, let's look at our fundamental source of truth, the rewriting system. We start with an \index{identity conformance rule}identity conformance rule, three associated type rules, and finally, three rules corresponding to user-written requirements:
\begin{gather*}
\ProtoInherit{S}{S}\tag{1}\\
\AssocIntro{S}{A}\tag{2}\\
\AssocIntro{S}{B}\tag{3}\\

Completion also adds \emph{ten} new rules; we will study a few at a time. Let's consider the first rule, $\ProtoInherit{S}{S}$. Every protocol has an identity conformance rule, but in the previous examples it didn't play an important role so we ignored it. The identity conformance rule always overlaps with itself:
\begin{align*}
\protosym{S}\cdot{}&\protosym{S}\\
&\protosym{S}\cdot\protosym{S}
\end{align*}
The critical pair is always \index{trivial critical pair}trivial:
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzcd}
\protosym{S}\cdot\protosym{S}\cdot\protosym{S}\arrow[d, Rightarrow, "(\ProtoInherit{S}{S})\cdot\protosym{S}"', bend right]\\
\protosym{S}\cdot\protosym{S}
\arrow[u, Rightarrow, "\protosym{S}\cdot(\ProtoInheritInv{S}{S})"', bend right]
\end{tikzcd}
\end{center}
Identity conformance rules also overlap with associated type rules. For example, rule~(1) overlaps with (2) on the term $\protosym{S}\cdot\protosym{S}\cdot\nA$:
\begin{align*}
\protosym{S}\cdot{}&\protosym{S}\\
&\protosym{S}\cdot\nA
\end{align*}
One side of this critical pair reduces to $\protosym{S}\cdot\assocsym{S}{A}$, while the other reduces to $\assocsym{S}{A}$. We define a new rule $\protosym{S}\cdot\assocsym{S}{A}\Red\assocsym{S}{A}$ and record a rewrite loop:
\begin{center}
\FourLoopDerived%
{\protosym{S}\cdot\protosym{S}\cdot\nA}%
{\protosym{S}\cdot\assocsym{S}{A}}%
{\assocsym{S}{A}}%
{\protosym{S}\cdot\nA}%
{\protosym{S}\cdot(\AssocIntro{S}{A})}%
{(\protosym{S}\cdot\assocsym{S}{A}\Red\assocsym{S}{A})}%
{(\AssocIntroInv{S}{A})}%
{(\ProtoInheritInv{S}{S})\cdot\nA}
\end{center}
In the same way we get such a rule for every associated type:
\begin{gather*}
\protosym{S}\cdot\assocsym{S}{A}\Red\assocsym{S}{A}\tag{\CRule{8}}\\
\protosym{S}\cdot\assocsym{S}{B}\Red\assocsym{S}{B}\tag{\CRule{9}}\\
\protosym{S}\cdot\assocsym{S}{C}\Red\assocsym{S}{C}\tag{\CRule{10}}
\end{gather*}
While we could have also written down similar rules in previous examples, they would not have illustrated anything of consequence. They will shortly; but first, we have a few overlaps between conformance rules and associated type rules. As in previous examples, we get the usual rules (\CRule{11}), (\CRule{12}), and (\CRule{13}) for reducing unbound type parameters; we also left-simplify (5), (6) and (7), replacing them with rules (\CRule{14}), (\CRule{15}), and (\CRule{16}):
\begin{gather*}
\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\nA\Red\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\assocsym{S}{A}\tag{\CRule{11}}\\
\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\nB\Red\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\assocsym{S}{B}\tag{\CRule{12}}\\
\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\nC\Red\protosym{S}\tag{\CRule{13}}\\
\ProtoConf{\assocsym{S}{C}}{S}\tag{\CRule{14}}\\
\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\assocsym{S}{C}\Red\protosym{S}\tag{\CRule{15}}\\
\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\assocsym{S}{A}\Red\assocsym{S}{B}\tag{\CRule{16}}
\end{gather*}

Here is the main event: rule (\CRule{15}) overlaps with (\CRule{16}) on the term $\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\assocsym{S}{A}$:
\begin{align*}
\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot{}&\assocsym{S}{C}\\
&\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\assocsym{S}{A}
\end{align*}
The right-hand side of this critical pair reduces the overlap term $\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\assocsym{S}{A}$ with rule (\CRule{15}), which gives us $\protosym{S}\cdot\assocsym{S}{A}$. Previously, terms for bound type parameters would begin with \emph{either} a protocol symbol or an associated type symbol, but not both; the associated type symbol already encodes the protocol. Indeed, this funny-looking term reduces to just $\assocsym{S}{A}$ via rule (\CRule{8}), defined as a consequence of that mysterious identity conformance rule (1).
\begin{center}
\FourLoopDerived%
{\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\assocsym{S}{A}}%
{\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\assocsym{S}{B}}%
{\assocsym{S}{A}}%
{\protosym{S}\cdot\assocsym{S}{A}}%
{\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot(\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\assocsym{S}{A}\Red\assocsym{S}{B})}%
{(\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\assocsym{S}{B}\Red\assocsym{S}{A})}%
{(\assocsym{S}{A}\Red\protosym{S}\cdot\assocsym{S}{A})}%
{(\protosym{S}\Red\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\assocsym{S}{C})\cdot\assocsym{S}{A}}
\end{center}

At last, and as predicated, we get the rule that proves $G_\texttt{S}\vdash\SameReq{\rT.A}{\rT.C.B}$:
\begin{gather*}
\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\assocsym{S}{B}\Red\assocsym{S}{A}\tag{\CRule{17}}
\end{gather*}

Now the \emph{encore}: rule (\CRule{15}) overlaps with rule (\CRule{14}) on the term $\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\protosym{S}$:
\begin{align*}
\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot{}&\assocsym{S}{C}\\
&\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\protosym{S}
\end{align*}
This critical pair resolves \index{trivial critical pair}trivially, by way of the identity conformance rule:
\begin{center}
\FourLoopTrivial%
{\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\protosym{S}}%
{\protosym{S}\cdot\protosym{S}}%
{\protosym{S}}%
{\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\assocsym{S}{C}}%
{(\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\assocsym{S}{C}\Red\protosym{S})\cdot\protosym{S}}%
{(\ProtoInherit{S}{S})}%
{(\protosym{S}\Red\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\assocsym{S}{C})}%
{\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot(\ProtoConfInv{\assocsym{S}{C}}{S})}
\end{center}

If the identity conformance rule had not been part of that initial set, this last critical pair would \emph{define} the identity conformance rule, and we would obtain the same rewriting system in the end. We don't need to explicitly add the identity conformance rule, after all. There is a practical consideration though. By making this rule part of the initial set, and crucially, marking it \index{permanent rule}\textbf{permanent}, we remove it from consideration in the rewriting system minimization algorithm. This cuts out a lot of unnecessary work.

It originates from a developer's bug report in 2020 \cite{sr12120}; \index{history}at the time, it broke the \Index{GenericSignatureBuilder@\texttt{GenericSignatureBuilder}}\texttt{GenericSignatureBuilder}'s minimization algorithm.

\section{Recursive Conformances}\label{sec:recursive conformances redux}

From previous examples, it may look like completion essentially enumerates \emph{all} derived requirements, but this cannot be true in general. We know from \SecRef{recursive conformances} that \index{recursive conformance requirement}recursive conformance requirements allow us to define \index{generic signature!infinite}generic signatures with an \index{theory!infinite}infinite theory of derived requirements. If the compiler is to allow such generic signatures, this infinite set of derived requirements must be encoded by a finite number of rewrite rules.

We will see that with recursive conformance requirements, successful termination of the Knuth-Bendix algorithm depends both on the existence of associated type symbols (and rules), and our choice of reduction order. This furnishes the concrete example for a scenario mentioned in \SecRef{word problem}.

\begin{example}\label{ex:protocol n fcrs}
Our first example is the \IndexProto{N}protocol \tN\ that we discussed in \ExRef{protocol n example}, \ExRef{protocol n graph}, and \ExRef{ex:protocol n fcrs}:
\begin{Verbatim}
protocol N {
  associatedtype A: N
}
\end{Verbatim}
Let's look at the complete rewriting system for $G_\tN$:
\begin{gather*}
\ProtoInherit{N}{N}\tag{1}\\
\AssocIntro{N}{A}\tag{2}\\
\ProtoConf{\pN\cdot\nA}{N}\tag{3}\\
\ProtoConf{\aNA}{N}\tag{\CRule{4}}\\
\pN\cdot\aNA\Red\aNA\tag{\CRule{5}}\\
\aNA\cdot\nA\Red\aNA\cdot\aNA\tag{\CRule{6}}\\
\midrule
\ProtoConf{\rT}{N}\tag{7}\\
\rT\cdot\nA\Red\rT\cdot\aNA\tag{\CRule{8}}
\end{gather*}
Rules (\CRule{4}), (\CRule{5}), (\CRule{6}) and (\CRule{8}) are defined by the rewrite loops shown in \FigRef{recursive n loops}. Rule~(3) is marked \textbf{left-simplified} because its left-hand side $\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\pN$ can always be reduced by the rewrite path $(\AssocIntro{N}{A}).\pN\circ(\ProtoConf{\aNA}{N})$.

In our rewriting system, the type parameter terms have \rT\ as the first symbol, and either \nA\ or $\aNA$ as each subsequent symbol. Any such term reduces to a term of the same length:
\[\rT\cdot\aNA\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\Red \rT\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA\]
The reduced terms are those that do not contain \nA; that is, a reduced term has the form $\rT\cdot\aNA^n$. Also, every type parameter of $G_\tN$ conforms to \tN. We have two infinite families of derived conformance requirements, where the subject types are the bound and unbound type parameters from each equivalence class:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l}
\toprule
\ConfReq{\rT.[N]A}{N}\\
\ConfReq{\rT.[N]A.[N]A}{N}\\
\ConfReq{\rT.[N]A.[N]A.[N]A}{N}\\
\ldots\\
\midrule
\ConfReq{\rT.A}{N}\\
\ConfReq{\rT.A.A}{N}\\
\ConfReq{\rT.A.A.A}{N}\\
\ldots\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Each derived requirement corresponds to an equivalence of terms $t\cdot\pN\Eq t$, where $t$ is a type parameter term. We can explicitly construct rewrite paths that witness these equivalences. Let's temporarily denote the positive rewrite step for rules (\CRule{4}), (\CRule{6}) and (\CRule{8}) by $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\gamma$ respectively:
\begin{gather*}
\alpha := (\ProtoConf{\aNA}{N})\\
\beta := (\aNA\cdot\nA\Red\aNA\cdot\aNA)\\
\gamma := (\rT\cdot\nA\Red\rT\cdot\aNA)
\end{gather*}
For a derived requirement with bound subject type, $t=\rT\cdot\aNA^n$. We can rewrite $t\cdot\pN$ to $t$ with a single rewrite step, using rule $\alpha$ to eliminate the suffix $\pN$ while leaving the rest of the term unchanged:
\begin{gather*}
\rT\WL\alpha\\
\rT\cdot\aNA\WL\alpha\\
\rT\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA\WL\alpha\\
\ldots
\end{gather*}
For those derived requirements with unbound subject types, $t=\rT\cdot\nA$. To rewrite $t\cdot\pN$ to $t$, we first reduce $t\Red t'$, eliminate $\pN$ with rule $\alpha$, and reverse the reduction to obtain~$t$. The reduction is given by a path $p_n$, where each $p_n$ rewrites $\rT\cdot\nA^n\Red\rT\cdot\aNA^n$:
\begin{gather*}
p_1:=\gamma\\
p_2:=(\gamma\WR\nA\cdot\pN)\circ(\rT\WL\beta\WR\pN)\\
p_3:=(\gamma\WR\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\pN)\circ(\rT\WL\beta\WR\nA\cdot\pN)\circ(\rT\cdot\aNA\WL\beta\WR\pN)\\
\ldots
\end{gather*}
Thus, the derived requirements with unbound subject types correspond to the following rewrite paths:
\begin{gather*}
(p_1\WR\pN)\circ(\rT\WL\alpha)\circ p_1^{-1}\\
(p_2\WR\pN)\circ(\rT\cdot\aNA\WL\alpha)\circ p_2^{-1}\\
(p_3\WR\pN)\circ(\rT\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA\WL\alpha)\circ p_3^{-1}\\
\ldots
\end{gather*}
For example, $\rT\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\pN$ and $\rT\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\nA$ both reduce to $\rT\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA$; we can join them with this rewrite path:
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzcd}
\rT\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\pN
\arrow[d, Rightarrow, "\gamma"']&
\rT\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\\
\rT\cdot\aNA\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\pN
\arrow[d, Rightarrow, "\beta"']&
\rT\cdot\aNA\cdot\nA\cdot\nA
\arrow[u, Rightarrow, "\gamma^{-1}"']\\
\rT\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA\cdot\nA\cdot\pN
\arrow[d, Rightarrow, "\beta"']&
\rT\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA\cdot\nA
\arrow[u, Rightarrow, "\beta^{-1}"']\\
\rT\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA\cdot\pN
\arrow[r, Rightarrow, "\alpha"', bend right]&
\rT\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA
\arrow[u, Rightarrow, "\beta^{-1}"']
\end{tikzcd}
\end{center}

Thus, our eight rules encode two infinite families of derived requirements.

\smallskip

Now, we will show that the associated type rule $\AssocIntro{N}{A}$ was essential. Let's start over with our the initial rules, but delete the symbol $\aNA$ and rule~(2) before attempting completion. Only two rules remain:
\begin{gather*}
\ProtoInherit{N}{N}\tag{1}\\
\ProtoConf{\pN\cdot\nA}{N}\tag{3}
\end{gather*}
We see that rule (3) rule overlaps with itself on the term $\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\pN$:
\begin{align*}
\pN\cdot\nA\cdot{}&\pN\\
&\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\pN
\end{align*}
Resolving this critical pair introduces a new rule $\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\pN\Red\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\nA$:
\begin{center}
\FourLoopDerived%
{\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\pN}%
{\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\pN}%
{\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\nA}%
{\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\pN\cdot\nA}%
{(\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\pN\Red\pN\cdot\nA)\cdot\nA\cdot\pN}%
{(\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\pN\Red\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\nA)}%
{(\pN\cdot\nA\Red\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\pN)\cdot\nA}%
{\pN\cdot\nA\cdot(\pN\cdot\nA\Red\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\pN)}
\end{center}
After adding this rule, we check for overlaps again. The new rule overlaps with rule~(3) on $\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\pN$, and also with itself on $\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\pN$. Rule~(3) also overlaps with the new rule on $\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\pN$. It is apparent that resolving those critical pairs will introduce new rules, and this process will never end. We get an infinite family of critical pairs, indexed by $m$, $n\in\NN$:
\begin{center}
\FourLoopDerived%
{\pN\cdot\nA^m\cdot\pN\cdot\nA^n\cdot\pN}%
{\pN\cdot\nA^{m+n}\cdot\pN}%
{\pN\cdot\nA^{m+n}}%
{\pN\cdot\nA^m\cdot\pN\cdot\nA^n}%
{(\pN\cdot\nA^m\cdot\pN\Red\pN\cdot\nA^m)\cdot\nA^n\cdot\pN}%
{(\pN\cdot\nA^{m+n}\cdot\pN\Red\pN\cdot\nA^{m+n})}%
{(\pN\cdot\nA^m\Red\pN\cdot\nA^m\cdot\pN)\cdot\nA^n}%
{\pN\cdot\nA^m\cdot(\pN\cdot\nA^n\Red\pN\cdot\nA^n\cdot\pN)}
\end{center}
These critical pairs define an infinite sequence of rewrite rules:
\begin{gather*}
\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\pN\Red\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\\
\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\pN\Red\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\\
\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\pN\Red\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\\
\ldots
\end{gather*}

Adding the symbol $\aNA$ together with the rule $\AssocIntro{N}{A}$ is a \index{Tietze transformation}Tietze transformation, so we know it does not change the equivalence relation on terms. However, it ensures that completion terminates. Successful completion also depends on associated type symbols preceding name symbols, so $\aNA<\nA$. Otherwise, we once again end up with an infinite sequence of rewrite rules:
\begin{gather*}
\aNA\cdot\nA\cdot\pN\Red\aNA\cdot\nA\\
\aNA\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\pN\Red\aNA\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\\
\aNA\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\pN\Red\aNA\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\\
\ldots
\end{gather*}

Let's summarize this example using \index{monoid presentation}monoid presentations. If we write $p$, $a$, $b$ instead of $\pN$, \nA, $\aNA$, the associated conformance requirement $\ConfReq{Self.A}{N}_\tN$ together with the identity conformance rule defines this presentation over $\{p, a\}$:
\[M_1 := \Pres{p,a}{pp \Eq p, pap\Eq pa}\]
We saw that completion does not terminate on $M_1$; abstractly, we can understand it as producing an \index{infinite monoid presentation}\emph{infinite} complete presentation $M_1'$, containing a family of rewrite rules parameterized by $i\in\NN$:
\[M_1' := \Pres{p,a}{pa^ip\Eq pa^i}\]
To obtain a finite complete presentation, we added the symbol $b$ and its defining rewrite rule $pa\Eq b$ to $M_1$. This yields the presentation $M_2$:
\[M_2 := \Pres{p,a,b}{pp\Eq p,\,pap\Eq pa,\,pa\Eq b}\]
Applying completion to $M_2$ with the reduction order $p<b<a$ succeeds, after adding the two rewrite rules $bp\Eq b$ and $ba\Eq bb$, giving us the finite complete presentation $M_2'$:
\[M_2' := \Pres{p,a,b}{pp\Eq p,\,pa\Eq b,\,bp\Eq b,\,ba\Eq bb}\]

We haven't met \index{requirement minimization}rewriting system minimization yet, but to minimize $M_2^{\prime}$, we eliminate all rewrite rules added by completion except for $bp\Eq b$, which we need because the original rewrite rule $pap\Eq pa$ was deleted. This leaves us with $M_3$:
\[M_3:=\Pres{p,a,b}{pp\Eq p,\,pa\Eq b,\,bp\Eq b}\]
The rule $bp\Eq b$ is the minimal conformance requirement $\ConfReq{Self.[N]A}{N}_\tN$ with a bound type parameter as the subject type. All of the above presentations define the \emph{same} monoid, up to isomorphism. Furthermore, if we apply completion to $M_3$, we get back $M_2^{\prime}$.
\end{example}

Here's a question. What if we only had associated type symbols that directly correspond to associated type declarations? That is, what if we got rid of the symbol $\assocsym{O}{A}$, together with rule (7), the inherited associated type rule $\AssocIntro{O}{A}$? The resulting monoid would again be equivalent, because this is a \index{Tietze transformation}Tietze transformation. However, we will see that completion fails.

Rule (8) still overlaps with rule (2), but instead of rule (\CRule{11}), we now get a rule $\protosym{O}\cdot\nA\Red\protosym{O}\cdot\aNA$ when we resolve this critical pair:
\begin{center}

\end{center}
\smallskip
Rule (9) now overlaps with $\protosym{O}\cdot\nA\Red\protosym{O}\cdot\aNA$. We get a new rule $\ProtoConf{\protosym{O}\cdot\aNA}{O}$:
\begin{center}
\FourLoopDerived%
{\protosym{O}\cdot\nA\cdot\protosym{O}}%
{\protosym{O}\cdot\aNA\cdot\protosym{O}}%
{\protosym{O}\cdot\aNA}%
{\protosym{O}\cdot\nA}%
{(\protosym{O}\cdot\nA\Red\protosym{O}\cdot\aNA)\cdot\protosym{O}}%
{(\protosym{O}\cdot\aNA\cdot\protosym{O}\Red\protosym{O}\cdot\aNA)}%
{\protosym{O}\cdot(\aNA\Red\nA)}%
{(\protosym{O}\cdot\nA\Red\protosym{O}\cdot\nA\cdot\protosym{O})}
\end{center}
\smallskip
Now, the rule $\ProtoConf{\protosym{O}\cdot\aNA}{O}$ overlaps with itself on the term $\protosym{O}\cdot\aNA\cdot\protosym{O}\cdot\aNA\cdot\protosym{O}$:
\begin{align*}
\protosym{O}\cdot\aNA\cdot{}&\protosym{O}\\
&\protosym{O}\cdot\aNA\cdot\protosym{O}
\end{align*}
The overlaps of this rule with itself generate an infinite family of critical pairs indexed by $m$,~$n\in\NN$:
\begin{center}
\FourLoopDerived%
{\protosym{O}\cdot\aNA^m\cdot\protosym{O}\cdot\aNA^n\cdot\protosym{O}}%
{\protosym{O}\cdot\aNA^{m+n}\cdot\protosym{O}}%
{\protosym{O}\cdot\aNA^{m+n}}%
{\protosym{O}\cdot\aNA^m\cdot\protosym{O}\cdot\aNA^n}%
{}%
{(\protosym{O}\cdot\aNA^{m+n}\cdot\protosym{O}\Red\protosym{O}\cdot\aNA^{m+n})}%
{}%
{}
\end{center}
\smallskip
These critical pairs define an infinite family of rewrite rules:
\begin{gather*}
\protosym{O}\cdot\aNA\cdot\protosym{O}\Red\protosym{O}\cdot\aNA\\
\protosym{O}\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA\cdot\protosym{O}\Red\protosym{O}\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA\\
\protosym{O}\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA\cdot\protosym{O}\Red\protosym{O}\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA\\
\ldots
\end{gather*}

Recall that the \index{protocol reduction order}reduction order on protocols was defined to compare the number of elements in the \index{protocol inheritance closure}protocol inheritance closure before comparing names. So if neither \tN\ nor \texttt{O} inherited any other protocols, we would have $\pN<\protosym{O}$. However, since \texttt{O} inherits from \tN, we have $\protosym{O}<\pN$ and thus $\assocsym{O}{A}<\aNA$. We won't go into details, but successful completion of the above \emph{also} depends on the reduction order being so. If instead $\aNA<\assocsym{O}{A}$, our requirement machine would not have a (finite) complete presentation (we will see below that we can still describe an \emph{infinite} complete presentation, but such objects are not easily computable.)

\smallskip

To summarize the theory, let's write $a$, $b$, $c$, $p$, $q$ instead of \nA, $\aNA$, $\assocsym{O}{A}$, $\pP$, $\protosym{O}$. The identity conformance rules and user-written requirements of \tN\ and \texttt{O} define the following presentation:
\[M_1 := \Pres{a,n,q}{nn\Eq n,\, nq\Eq n,\, qq\Eq q,\,nan\Eq na,\,qaq\Eq qa}\]
Completion doesn't terminate with the above; abstractly, we get an \index{complete presentation}\index{infinite complete presentation}infinite complete presentation over the same generators:
\[M_1' := \Pres{a,n,q}{na^in\Eq na^i,\, qa^in\Eq qa^i,\,qa^iq\Eq qa^i}\]
The first Tietze transformation adds $b$ with defining rewrite rule $na\Eq b$, as in the previous example:
\[M_2 := \Pres{a,b,n,q}{nn\Eq n,\, qq\Eq q,\, qn\Eq q,\, nan\Eq na,\,qaq\Eq qa,\,na\Eq b}\]
We saw that completion still fails; we get the following \index{infinite complete presentation}infinite complete presentation:
\[M_2^{\prime} := \Pres{a,b,n,q}{nn\Eq n,\,na\Eq b,\,bn\Eq b,\,ba\Eq bb,\,qb^iq\Eq qb^i,\, qb^ia\Eq qb^{i+1}}\]
However, we then add $c$ with defining rewrite rule $qa\Eq c$:
\[M_3 := \Pres{a,b,c,n,q}{nn\Eq n,\, qq\Eq q,\, qn\Eq q,\, nan\Eq na,\,qaq\Eq qa,\,na\Eq b,\,qa\Eq c}\]
Completion succeeds on $M_3$ using a reduction order where $c<b<a$ and $p<n$, and we get a finite complete rewriting system. We've already listed the numerous rules once, so we won't do it again. Let's call this finite complete presentation $M_2'$:
\[M_3' := \Pres{a,b,c,n,q}{\mbox{\ldots 15 rules \ldots}}\]
The minimized rewriting system corresponds to this presentation:
\[M_4 :=\Pres{a,b,c,n,q}{nn\Eq n,\, na\Eq b,\, bn\Eq b,\, qq\Eq q,\, qa\Eq c,\, qn\Eq q,\, cq\Eq c}\]
If we start with $M_3$ and apply completion, we again get $M_2'$.
\end{example}

\section{Regular Languages}\label{sec:regular languages}

For the proof in the next section, we will need the concept of a \emph{regular language}. A \emph{language} in this sense is a subset of the free monoid over some alphabet, so regular languages are also known as \emph{regular sets}. In fact, we only need a few definitions, together with the statement of the pumping lemma. A complete treatment of this subject can be found in \cite{transductions}.

First, an informal introduction. Regular languages appear in programming in the form of \index{regular expression!see{regular language}}\emph{regular expressions}. Here is a typical regular expression, written in Unix notation:
\begin{quote}
\texttt{ta(p|q)*a}
\end{quote}
This regular expression will match various strings such as \texttt{taa} and \texttt{tappqqa}, but not \texttt{pat}. We think of the regular expression as defining a \emph{subset} of the free monoid $\{t,p,q,a\}^*$, and again, this subset contains $taa$ and $tappqqa$, but not $pat$. In our notation, the same regular expression will instead look like this:
\[
ta \cdot \{p,q\}^* \cdot a
\]
For concision when specifying regular languages as above, we say that a word ``$ta$'' or ``$a$'' actually stands for the singleton set containing that word, like $\{ta\}$ or $\{a\}$. The regular expression concatenation (``$\cdot$'') and Kleene star (``${}^*$'') operations, together with set union, can then be seen as operations on sets of words.

\begin{definition}
Let $A^*$ be the \index{free monoid}free monoid over some set $A$, and let $X$, $Y \subseteq A^*$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The \index{concatenation}\emph{concatenation} of $X$ and $Y$, denoted $XY$ or $X \cdot Y$, is defined as the set of all possible words $xy$ formed by taking some $x \in X$ and $y \in Y$.
\item If $n \geq 0$, then $X^n$ is the concatenation of $X$ with itself $n$ times, which is the set of all words formed by concatenating any $n$ elements taken from $X$, with repetitions allowed. In particular, $X^0=\{\varepsilon\}$ is the singleton set consisting of the \index{empty word}empty word, while $X^1=X$.
\item The \IndexDefinition{Kleene star}\emph{Kleene star} of $X$, denoted by $X^*$, is the set of all words that can be formed by concatenating elements of $X$:
\[
X^* := X^0 \cup X^1 \cup X^2 \cdots \cup X^n \cup \cdots
\]
\item We also define $X^+ := XX^* = X^* \setminus \{\{\varepsilon\}\}$; this is the set of all words formed by concatenating \emph{one} or more elements of $X$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

A regular language, or a regular subset of $A^*$, is then one that can be constructed by applying these operations finitely many times to some number of finite sets:

\begin{definition}
Let $A^*$ be the free monoid over some set $A$. The family of \IndexDefinition{regular language}\emph{regular languages} of $A^*$ is defined inductively as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $X \subset A^*$ is a finite set, then $X$ is a regular language.
\item If $X$, $Y \subseteq A^*$ are regular languages, their \index{union}set union $X \cup Y$ is a regular language.
\item If $X$, $Y \subseteq A^*$ are regular language, their \index{concatenation}concatenation $XY$ is a regular language.
\item If $X \subseteq A^*$ is a regular language, the Kleene star $X^*$ is a regular language.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

A standard result is that regular languages are precisely those that can be recognized by a \index{finite state machine}finite state machine. We can then use this to show that regular languages satisfy some closure properties, by explicitly constructing a finite state machine for each case.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:regular language closure}
Let $X$, $Y \subseteq A^*$, and assume $X$ and $Y$ are regular languages.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The intersection $X \cap Y$ is a regular language.
\item The \index{complement}complement $A^* \setminus X$ is a regular language.
\item The set formed by reversing each word in $X$ is a regular language.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

Consider again our earlier example $ta \cdot \{p,q\}^* \cdot a$. Suppose that $u$ is a word in this regular language, and $|u|>3$. Then $u=ta \cdot x \cdot a$ for some $x \in \PAndQ^*$, and $|x| > 0$. It then follows that $ta \cdot x^2 \cdot a$, $ta \cdot y^3 \cdot a$, $\cdots$, are also elements of our language. This sort of construction generalizes to all regular languages:

\begin{lemma}[Pumping lemma]\label{pumping lemma}
Let $X \subseteq A^*$ be a regular set. Then there exists a natural number $\ell > 0$ such that any word $u \in X$ with $|u| \geq \ell$ has a factorization:
\[
u = xyz
\]
with the property that $|y| > 0$, $|xy| \leq \ell$, and for all $n \geq 0$:
\[xy^nz \in X\]
\end{lemma}

The proof can be found in \cite{transductions}, but the general idea is the following. When $X$ is finite, we set $\ell$ to be longer than the longest word in $X$, and the conclusion is vacuously true. Otherwise, if $X$ is infinite, we can always find a word $u \in X$ whose length exceeds the number of states in the finite state machine for $X$. This finite state machine must visit at least one state more than once when given~$u$, and since it cannot ``remember'' the fact that it visited the same state before, we can repeat the sequence of symbols visited along such a path any number of times to obtain longer elements of $X$. This gives us the factor $y$ in the statement of the lemma.

\begin{remark}
Our statement of the pumping lemma yields a factorization ``from the start'' with $|x| \geq 0$ and $|z| \geq |u| - \ell$. However, by part (3) of \LemmaRef{lemma:regular language closure}, regular languages are closed under reversal, which implies a symmetric form of the pumping lemma where the factorization is ``from the end'' instead, so $|yz| \leq \ell$, $|x| \geq |u| - \ell$, and $|z| \geq 0$. In the next section, we will refer to this as applying the pumping lemma ``from the start'' or ``from the end.''
\end{remark}

Now, we will relate all of this to complete rewriting systems. Recall the definition of an \index{irreducible word}irreducible word from \SecRef{sec:normal form algorithm}, and consider the set of all irreducible words of some monoid presentation $\AR$, that is, all words from $A^*$ that do not contain the left-hand side of a rule from $R$. We will denote this set by \index{$\Irr(R)$}\index{$\Irr(R)$!z@\igobble|seealso{irreducible word}}$\Irr(R)$ in what follows. The case where $\Irr(R)$ is a regular set is of interest to us, because we can apply the pumping lemma to irreducible words. In particular, this is the case when $R$ is finite.

\begin{remark}
The notation $\Irr(R)$ depends on the choice of alphabet $A$. For example, if $R=\{(a,\varepsilon)\}$ and $A=\{a\}$, then $Irr(R) = \{ \varepsilon \}$, but if $A=\{a,b\}$, then $\Irr(R)=\{b\}^*$. On the other hand, note that the definition of $\Irr(R)$ does not depend on the \emph{right-hand} sides of~$R$ at all.
\end{remark}

We will state some properties of $\Irr(R)$ that are useful to us now.

\begin{lemma}\label{irr r facts}
Let $\AR$ be any monoid presentation, with $R$ not necessarily finite.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $x \not\in \Irr(R)$ and $y \in A^*$, then $xy \not\in \Irr(R)$ and $yx \not\in \Irr(R)$. (That is, if any factor of a word is reducible, the entire word is reducible.)
\item If $x \in A^*$, $y \in A^*$, and $xy\in \Irr(R)$, then $x \in \Irr(R)$, and $y \in \Irr(R)$. (That is, any factor of an irreducible word is irreducible.)
\item If the left-hand sides of $R$ form a regular set (which in particular is always true if $R$ is finite), then $\Irr(R)$ is a regular set.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If $x \not\in \Irr(R)$, then $x$ has a factor of $u$ for some $(u,v)\in R$. Thus, for any $y \in A^*$, both $xy$ and $yx$ also have a factor of $u$, so $xy\not\in\Irr(R)$ and $yx\not\in\Irr(R)$. This gives us (1), and (2) is the contrapositive statement.

For (3), suppose that $R$ is finite, and let $n := |R|$. The complement of $\Irr(R)$ in $A^*$ is the set of all words that contain a left-hand side of $R$ as a factor. We can express this set as follows:
\[
A^* \setminus \Irr(R) = (A^* \cdot u_1 \cdot A^*) \cup \cdots \cup (A^* \cdot u_n \cdot A^*)
\]
By \LemmaRef{lemma:regular language closure}, it follows that $A^* \setminus \Irr(R)$ is a regular set, and therefore so is $\Irr(R)$. (In fact, part 3 is also true when $R$ is infinite with a regular set of left-hand sides, but we do not need this fact.)
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
In \SecRef{sec:recursive conformances redux}, we looked at two complete presentations of the monoid $\Pres{p,a}{pap \Eq pa}$. The first presentation was finite:
\[
\Pres{p,a,c}{pa \Eq c, cp \Eq c, ca \Eq cc}
\]
The \emph{reducible} words with respect to this presentation are those that contain at least one occurrence of $pa$, $cp$, or $ca$:
\[
\{p, a, c\}^* \cdot \{pa, cp, ca\}^* \cdot \{p, a, c\}^*
\]
The only words not in this set are those where $a$ does not follow $p$ or $c$, and $p$ does not follow $c$. Thus, the irreducible words are precisely:
\[
a^* \cdot p^* \cdot c^*
\]
Here, $\ell = 1$, because any non-empty word will contain $p$, $a$, or $c$, and we can repeat that letter to get longer irreducible words.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
We also saw an infinite complete presentation of $\Pres{p,a}{pap \Eq pa}$. We take the original alphabet $A := \{p, a\}$, and this set of rules:
\[
R := \{ (pa^n p, pa^n) \mid \text{for all $n \geq 1$} \}
\]
Here, the left-hand sides are elements of the regular set $p \cdot a^+ \cdot p$. The irreducible words are the following, and once again, $\ell = 1$:
\[
a^* \cdot p^* \cdot a^*
\]
\end{example}

While the pumping lemma allows us to construct long irreducible words, the next lemma allows us to take two long irreducible words and overlap them to form an even longer irreducible word.

\begin{lemma}\label{irreducible overlap lemma}
Suppose that $xy\in \Irr(R)$ and $yz\in\Irr(R)$. Then, we either have $xyz\in\Irr(R)$, or there exists a rule $(u,v)\in R$ with $|u| \geq |y|$. In particular, if $R$ is finite and $y$ is longer than any left-hand side in $R$, we must have $xyz \in \Irr(R)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Assume that $xyz \not\in \Irr(R)$, and write $xyz=x'uz'$ where $u$ is the left-hand side of a rule in $R$, and $x'$, $y' \in A^*$. Suppose that to the contrary, $|u|<|y|$. Either $|xy| < |x'u|$, or $|xy| \geq |x'u|$. If $|xy| < |x'u|$, then $x'u \not\in \Irr(R)$ is a prefix of $xy \in \Irr(R)$, which is impossible by \LemmaRef{irr r facts}. On the other hand, if $|xy| \geq |x'u|$, then $|z| \leq |z'|$, and now $uz'\not\in \Irr(R)$ is a suffix of $yz \in \Irr(R)$, which is again impossible. So $|u| \geq |y|$.
\end{proof}

\section{The Event Horizon}\label{sec:event horizon}

In \SecRef{word problem}, we took a monoid with an undecidable word problem, and translated its defining relations into the same-type requirements of a Swift protocol declaration. This yielded a protocol whose generic signature has an undecidable theory, so it cannot be accepted by the Swift compiler for inherent reasons.

We will now see a generic signature with a decidable theory, but one that nonetheless is not accepted by the current implementation. When we translate this generic signature into a monoid presentation, we get a monoid that does not admit a finite complete presentation over any alphabet, a phenomenon we discussed in \SecRef{sec:normal form algorithm}. In fact, the \Index{GenericSignatureBuilder@\texttt{GenericSignatureBuilder}}\texttt{GenericSignatureBuilder} of \IndexSwift{5.6}Swift~5.6 and earlier would accept this signature, and this was perhaps the only notable regression that came with the re-implementation.

This example does not involve same-type requirements at all. We declare two protocols that look like copies of \IndexProto{N}protocol \tN, with a single recursive associated type each, and we give both associated types the same name, as in \ExRef{two protocols same assoc}. Finally, we declare a generic parameter, and state a conformance requirement to both protocols. That's it:
\VerbInput{completion/tpqa.swift}
Here is the generic signature of the struct \texttt{G}:
\begin{quote}
\begin{verbatim}
<τ_0_0 where τ_0_0: P, τ_0_0: Q>
\end{verbatim}
\end{quote}
We will begin by investigating exactly what happens when the the Requirement Machine encounters this generic signature, before we state and prove the main result of this section. Much like the generic signature of protocol \tN, the generic signature of struct \texttt{G} generates an infinite set of type parameters, where each equivalence class has a representative of unique \index{type parameter length}length:
\begin{gather*}
\rT \\
\texttt{\rT.A}\\
\texttt{\rT.A.A}\\
\texttt{\rT.A.A.A}\\
\ldots
\end{gather*}
Specific to this example however, is that each equivalence class conforms to both \tP~and~\tQ. Within each equivalence class, the elements differ in whether each dependent member type is unbound, bound to \texttt{[P]A}, or bound to \texttt{[Q]A}. For example, the following are all equivalent to \texttt{\rT.A.A}:
\begin{gather*}
\texttt{\rT.[P]A.[Q]A}\\
\texttt{\rT.[Q]A.[P]A}\\
\texttt{\rT.A.[Q]A}\\
\texttt{\rT.[P]A.A}\\
\ldots
\end{gather*}

The requirement machines for \tP\ and \tQ\ are identical to protocol \tN\ from \ExRef{ex:protocol n fcrs}, except for a difference in protocol names. When we build the requirement machine for our generic signature, we first import rules from \tP\ and \tQ. Only some of the rules are relevant for our purposes; they are listed below:
\begin{gather*}
\AssocIntro{P}{A}\tag{1}\\
\ProtoConf{\assocsym{P}{A}}{P}\tag{2}\\
\assocsym{P}{A}\cdot\nA\Red\assocsym{P}{A}\cdot\assocsym{P}{A}\tag{3}\\
\midrule
\AssocIntro{Q}{A}\tag{4}\\
\ProtoConf{\assocsym{Q}{A}}{Q}\tag{5}\\
\assocsym{Q}{A}\cdot\nA\Red\assocsym{Q}{A}\cdot\assocsym{Q}{A}\tag{6}\\
\midrule
\ProtoConf{\rT}{P}\tag{7}\\
\ProtoConf{\rT}{Q}\tag{8}
\end{gather*}

Rules (7) and (8) overlap with rules (1) and (4), respectively. Resolving the first critical pair gives us this new rule:
\begin{gather*}
\rT\cdot\nA\Red\rT\cdot\assocsym{P}{A}\tag{9}
\end{gather*}
The second critical pair, $(\rT \cdot \nA, \rT \cdot \aQA)$, has a left-hand side that reduces to $\rT \cdot \aPA$ by an application of (9). We then orient this critical pair to get:
\begin{gather*}
\rT\cdot\assocsym{Q}{A}\Red\rT\cdot\assocsym{P}{A}\tag{10}
\end{gather*}
So far, this looks like the situation in \ExRef{two protocols same assoc}. We check left-hand sides for overlaps again. Now, rule (10) overlaps with (5) on the term $\rT\cdot\assocsym{Q}{A}\cdot\protosym{Q}$, and (10) also overlaps with (5) on the term $\rT\cdot\assocsym{Q}{A}\cdot\nA$. Resolving these critical pairs adds two new rules:
\begin{gather*}
\ProtoConf{\rT\cdot\assocsym{P}{A}}{Q}\tag{11}\\
\rT\cdot\assocsym{P}{A}\cdot\assocsym{Q}{A}\Red\rT\cdot\assocsym{P}{A}\cdot\assocsym{P}{A}\tag{12}
\end{gather*}
We now enter the third round. Rule (11) overlaps with (5) on $\rT\cdot\assocsym{P}{A}\cdot\assocsym{Q}{A}\cdot\protosym{Q}$, and rule (11) also overlaps with (6) on $\rT\cdot\assocsym{P}{A}\cdot\assocsym{Q}{A}\cdot\nA$.
A pattern begins to emerge:
\begin{gather*}
\ProtoConf{\rT\cdot\assocsym{P}{A}\cdot\assocsym{P}{A}}{Q}\tag{17}\\
\rT\cdot\assocsym{P}{A}\cdot\assocsym{P}{A}\cdot\assocsym{Q}{A}\Red \rT\cdot\assocsym{P}{A}\cdot\assocsym{P}{A}\cdot\assocsym{P}{A}\tag{18}
\end{gather*}
We add two new rules, but once again, this introduces two \emph{more} unresolved critical pairs. If we continue the process, we see that completion is attempting to enumerate a rewriting system containing two infinite families of rewrite rules, for all $n \geq 0$:
\begin{gather*}
\ProtoConf{\rT\cdot{\assocsym{P}{A}}^n}{Q}\\
\rT\cdot{\assocsym{P}{A}}^n\cdot\assocsym{Q}{A}\Red \rT\cdot{\assocsym{P}{A}}^{n+1}
\end{gather*}
Eventually, we hit the Knuth-Bendix \index{termination check}iteration limit and diagnose an error.

\smallskip

When we looked at protocol \tN\ in \ExRef{ex:protocol n fcrs}, we saw that successful completion required introducing an \index{associated type symbol}associated type symbol, together with an \index{associated type rule}associated type rule making this new symbol equivalent to an existing term. We also had to ensure that associated type symbols precede name symbols in our \index{reduction order}reduction order. We then saw that situations like protocol~\texttt{O} from \ExRef{ex:protocol o fcrs} motivated a further tweak to the reduction order, to take protocol inheritance into account.

In the current example, our strategy of introducing associated type symbols $\aPA$ and $\aQA$ equivalent to $\pP\cdot\nA$ and $\pQ\cdot\nA$ is insufficient. Knuth-Bendix completion still loops forever, generating an infinite sequence of rules that appear to follow a simple pattern. In fact, we're out of luck. We cannot hope to obtain a finite complete presentation here by introducing more symbols, or further tweaking the reduction order.

We will now dispense with the associated type symbols $\aPA$ and $\aQA$ since they are of no use to us here, and write $t$, $p$, $q$, $a$ instead of $\rT$, $\pP$, $\pQ$, $\nA$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:monoid m is not fcrs}
The monoid $M$ with presentation below has a decidable word problem, but no \index{finite complete presentation}finite complete presentation over any alphabet:
\[
M := \Pres{t,p,q,a}{\CC{t}{p},\,\CC{t}{q},\, \CC{pa}{p},\, \CC{qa}{q}}
\]
\end{theorem}

The proof takes the rest of this section. From now on, $M$ will always denote the above monoid, and $A$~and~$R$ will refer to the alphabet and rules in the above presentation:
\begin{gather*}
A := \{ t, p, q, a \}\\
R := \{ (tp, t), (tq, t), (pap, pa), (qaq, qa) \}
\end{gather*}
The letters $t$, $p$, $q$, $a$ will only ever denote those specific elements of~$A$. We use the remaining lowercase letters as indeterminates. The monoid congruence $\Eq_R$ generated by~$R$ will simply be denoted~$\Eq$. Since the rules are symmetric in $p$~and~$q$, many results will hold when $p$ is replaced with $q$, or vice versa. To avoid needless repetition, we introduce the convention that $s \in \PAndQ$.

\paragraph{The word problem.}
While Knuth-Bendix failed us, we can solve the word problem in $M$ ``by hand.'' We begin by noting that words containing~$t$ will turn out to behave differently from those that do not. Since none of the defining relations in~$R$ insert or remove~$t$, the number of occurrences of~$t$ in a word is well-defined under~$\Eq$, and the words not involving $t$ are closed under the monoid operation. Thus, we can split off ``the $t$ part'' from the rest. For this reason, it is convenient to define the submonoid of~$M$ consisting of those elements represented by words that do not involve~$t$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:tpqa m0}
Let $M_0$ be the submonoid of $M$ generated by $\{p, q, a\}$. This monoid has the following presentation:
\[
M_0 := \Pres{p, q, a}{\CC{pa}{p},\, \CC{qa}{q}}
\]
Also, let $A_0 := \{p,q,a\}$ and $R_0 := \{ (pap, pa), (qaq, qa) \}$.
\end{definition}

If we apply Knuth-Bendix completion to $R$, but this time without adding associated type symbols first, we again observe an infinite sequence of rules that follow a simple pattern. In fact, the left-hand sides of these rules enumerate a \emph{regular set}. We can thus describe this infinite set of rules in finite terms. We will denote this set by $\RInf$. This is known as a \index{regular complete presentation}\emph{regular complete presentation} of our monoid $M$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:tpqa infinite complete}
We define $\RInf \subset A^* \times A^*$ to consist of the following:
\begin{alignat}{2}
&(pa^np,\, pa^n) \qquad &\text{for all $n > 0$} \tag{$1_n$} \\
&(qa^nq,\, qa^n) \qquad &\text{for all $n > 0$} \tag{$2_n$} \\
&(ta^np,\, ta^n) \qquad &\text{for all $n \geq 0$} \tag{$3_n$} \\
&(ta^nq,\, ta^n) \qquad &\text{for all $n \geq 0$} \tag{$4_n$}
\end{alignat}
The reduction relation $\Red_{\RInf}$ generated by $\RInf$ will be denoted simply by $\Red$.
\end{definition}

The intuition behind rules $(1_n)$ and $(2_n)$ is that we can erase an $s \in \PAndQ$ whenever it appears after a previous occurrence of $s$ that is followed by one or more $a$'s. The intuition behind rules $(3_n)$ and $(4_n)$ is that we can erase any $s \in \PAndQ$ that appears to the right of $t$.

We will see shortly that $\Pres{A}{\RInf}$ is a complete presentation, but certainly it is certainly not a \emph{finite} complete presentation. Our ultimate goal is to show that if $\Pres{C}{S}$ is some \emph{other} complete presentation of~$M$, then that set of rules~$S$ must be infinite, too. We will first prove that $\Pres{A}{\RInf}$ is a complete presentation of $M$, as claimed. We will then see how to compute normal forms with respect to $\RInf$, which solves the word problem in $M$. We will use these normal forms to study the structure of $M$, before we move on to the main proof.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:rinf complete}
The presentation $\Pres{A}{\RInf}$ is a complete presentation of $\AR$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
It is clear that $R \subset \RInf$, so every rule in $R$ is a consequence of $\RInf$. We also need to establish that every rule in $\RInf$ is a consquence of $R$, or in other words, for each $(u, v) \in \RInf$, we have $u \Eq_R v$. Finally, we will show that $\Red$ is terminating and confluent.

To establish that the first two rule families $(1_n)$ and $(2_n)$ are consequences of~$R$, we must show that for $s \in \PAndQ$ and $n > 0$, we have $sa^ns \Eq sa^n$. We argue by induction on $n$. When $n=1$, we have $(sas,sa) \in R$. When $n>1$, we assume that $sa^{n-1}s \Eq sa^{n-1}$ by the induction hypothesis, so that:
\begin{align*}
sa^ns & = sa^{n-1}as \\
& \Eq sa^{n-1}sas \\
& \Eq sa^{n-1}sa \\
& \Eq sa^{n-1}a = sa^n
\end{align*}
For the second two rule families $(3_n)$ and $(4_n)$, we must show that for $s \in \PAndQ$ and $n \geq 0$, we have $ta^ns \Eq ta^n$. When $n=0$, we have $(ts, t) \in R$. When $n > 0$, we make use of the previous identity $sa^ns \Eq sa^n$, so that:
\begin{align*}
ta^ns & \Eq tsa^ns \\
& \Eq tsa^n \\
& \Eq ta^n
\end{align*}
We conclude that $\Pres{A}{\RInf}$ is a presentation of $M$.

We turn our attention to the reduction relation $\Red$. Notice how the right-hand side of each rule in $\RInf$ is obtained by erasing a single letter from the left-hand side. By induction, it follows that the $\RInf$-normal form of a word is always a \emph{non-contiguous substring} of the original word. Thus, the length of any positive rewrite path over $\RInf$ is bounded by the length of the initial word, establishing termination.

It remains to establish confluence. Rules with overlapping left-hand sides are listed in in \FigRef{fig:tpqa overlaps}. Both sides of each critical pair reduce to the same word, as shown in \FigRef{fig:tpqa critical pairs}. Thus $\Red$ is terminating and confluent, and therefore $\Pres{A}{\RInf}$ is a complete presentation of $M$.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
The $\RInf$-normal form of $x \in A^*$ will be denoted by $\Phi(x)$.
\end{definition}

Since $\RInf$ is infinite, we cannot compute $\Phi$ with \AlgRef{alg:normal form}, which expects to receive a finite list of rules. However, we will now see two ways of directly computing~$\Phi$, which establishes that $M$ has a decidable word problem.

The first implementation is based on the observation that each infinite family of rules in $\RInf$ can be encoded as a regular expression substitution. There is more than one way to do it, but here is a program to compute $\Phi$ written in \index{Perl}Perl~\cite{perl}:

The above program applies rewrite rules until fixed point, and each rule will delete a single letter from the string. In the worst case, this takes time quadratic in the length of the input, for example when reducing $pap^n \Red pap^{n-1} \Red \cdots \Red pap \Red pa$.

Our second implementation is more efficient. By making use of specific properties of the rules of $\RInf$, we can compute the normal form in a single pass over the input string. While this efficiency gain is of no practical consequence here, we will take a closer look at this approach, because the algorithm will help build intuition for the main proof.

First, notice how the pure powers of $a$ are the simplest elements of $M_0$ (and thus $M$). None of the original defining relations in $R$ have a side consisting of $a$ alone, so not only is each $a^n$ irreducible under $\RInf$, but also it is a singleton equivalence class, not equivalent to any other word. That is, the letter $a$ generates a submonoid of~$M_0$ that is isomorphic to $\NN$. The words that represent the elements of this submonoid we will denote by the regular expression $a^* \subset A_0^*$. The next lemma says that we can disregard a prefix or suffix from $a^*$ when computing the $\RInf$ normal form of a word.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:tpqa phi a}
Let $x \in a^*$, $y \in A^*$, and $z \in a^*$. Then $\Phi(xyz) = x \cdot \Phi(y) \cdot z$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
None of the rules in $\RInf$ have a left-hand side that begins or ends in~$a$, so we always have $\Phi(ay)=a \cdot \Phi(y)$ and $\Phi(ya)=\Phi(y) \cdot a$ for all $y \in A^*$. The general case follows by induction.
\end{proof}

To compute $\Phi(x)$ in a single pass over $x$, we start with the case where $x \in A_0^*$. We can skip any occurrences of $a$ at the beginning of the word, by the above lemma. If we encounter an $s \in \PAndQ$ followed by $a$, we enter a state where subsequent occurrences of the same letter $s$ are erased, by application of rule families $(1_n)$ and $(2_n)$. This continues until we encounter the \emph{other} letter $s' \in \PAndQ$ with $s' \neq s$, at which point we flip into the opposite state, where we're now ready to erase~$s'$ as soon as we see at least one $a$, then we flip again, and so on. Finally, any suffix of $a$'s can again be skipped. This is demonstrated below. The erased letters are underlined:
\[
\Phi(appa\underline{p}\underline{p}qa\underline{q}paaa) = appaqapaaa
\]
To compute normal forms for words $x \in A^* \setminus A_0^*$, we first look at the case where $x$ begins with $t$. We can erase any subsequent $p$'s and $q$'s by application of rule families $(3_n)$ and $(4_n)$ , so that only $t$'s and $a$'s remain to the right of the first $t$:
\[
\Phi(ta\underline{pq}a\underline{q}t\underline{p}a\underline{pp}a\underline{qq}) = taataa
\]
For an arbitrary word $x \in A^*$ then, we compute $\Phi(x)$ by looking at the longest prefix from $A_0^*$ first, alternating between erasing $p$'s and $q$'s. Once we encounter $t$, we enter the ``$t$ state'' from which we cannot return, where we erase all subsequent occurrences of both $p$ and $q$.

A monoid is \IndexDefinition{cancellative monoid}\emph{cancellative} if for all triples of elements $x$, $y$, $z$ such that $xz \Eq yz$, it follows that $x \Eq y$, so we can ``cancel'' the $z$. Both $M$ and $M_0$ fail to be cancellative in a very strong way. This can be observed in the above examples, but even more trivially, $p \NotEq 1$ and yet $pap \Eq pa$. The failure of cancellativity with triples of one specific form will allow us set up a contradiction in our main proof later:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:ai s aj distinct}
Suppose that $s \in \PAndQ$. Let $i$, $j$, $i'$, $j' \geq 0$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $(i, j) \neq (i', j')$, then $a^i s a^j \not\Eq a^{i'} s a^{j'}$.
\item If $i + j = i' + j'$, then $s a^i s a^j \Eq s a^{i'} s a^{j'}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For (1), note that $a^i s a^j$ does not contain the left-hand side of any rule in $\RInf$, so it is irreducible. For (2), the $\RInf$ normal form of $sa^i s a^j$ is just $s a^{i+j}$ by one application of the rule $(sa^is, sa^i)$. Similarly, the right-hand side reduces to $s a^{i'+j'}$.
\end{proof}

We will also need another result about elements of this specific form.

\newcommand{\AS}{a \cdot (s^*)}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:ai s aj class}
Suppose that $s \in \PAndQ$, $m$, $n \geq 0$, and $x \in A^*$. Then $x \Eq a^m s a^n$ if and only if $x$ belongs to this regular set:
\[
a^m \cdot s \cdot ( \AS )^n
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If $x$ belongs to the above regular set, then $x \Red a^m s a^n$, by repeated application of rules $(sa^ks, sa^k)$, so $x \Eq a^m s a^n$.

For the other direction, suppose that $x \Eq a^m s a^n$. Since $a^m s a^n \in \Irr(\RInf)$, we in fact have $x \Red a^m s a^n$. An inspection of the right-hand sides of $\RInf$ shows that the only rules that can appear in this positive rewrite path are rules of the form $(sa^ks, sa^k)$. Thus, $x$ is obtained from $a^m s a^n$ by inserting occurrences of $s$ at some number of positions that must all follow the one after the first occurrence of $s$, but the first $s$ must always be followed by an $a$.
\end{proof}
For example, $aapaa$ is equivalent to $aapapa$, $aapappa$, $aapapapp$, and so on. Indeed, the defining relations in $R$ allow us to insert and remove $p$'s and $q$'s in various ways, but the number of $a$'s in a word always remains fixed. We can count the number of $a$'s as a measure of ``length.''

\begin{definition}\label{def:tpqa rank}
Let $\Rank \colon A^* \rightarrow \NN$ be the homomorphism which maps a word $x \in A^*$ to the number of occurrences of the letter $a$ in $x$.
\end{definition}
\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:tpqa rank}
The $\Rank$ mapping is well-defined on the equivalence classes of $\Eq$, surjective onto $\NN$, and the restriction $\Rank\colon a^* \rightarrow \NN$ is a monoid isomorphism.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
As mentioned, the defining relations in $R$ do not change the number of $a$'s, so $x \Eq y$ implies that $\Rank(x) = \Rank(y)$ for any $x$, $y \in A^*$. As for $a^*$, each element of $a^*$ is a singleton equivalence class, and has the form $a^n$ for some $n \in \NN$. Thus $\Rank(a^n)=n$. This shows that $\Rank$ is surjective, and when restricted to $a^*$, also injective.
\end{proof}

That's enough about $a$ for now. To understand $t$, $p$, and $q$, we will look at various \IndexDefinition{ideal}\emph{ideals} of $M$.

\begin{definition}
For this definition, assume $M$ is any monoid.
\begin{itemize}
\item A subset $I \subseteq M$ is a \IndexDefinition{left ideal}\emph{left} ideal if it is closed under multiplication on the left, meaning that for all $x \in M$ and $y \in I$, we have $xy \in I$.
\item  Analogously, $I \subseteq M$ is a \emph{right} ideal if for all $x \in I$ and $y \in M$, we have $xy \in I$.
\item  A \emph{two-sided} ideal is one that is both a left and a right ideal, so for all $x \in M$, $y \in I$, and $z \in M$, we have $xyz \in I$.
\item  If $y \in M$ is some element, the left ideal \emph{generated} by $y$, denoted by $My$, is the set of all products $xy$ with $x \in M$.
\item  Similarly, we can talk about a right ideal generated by $x$, denoted by $xM$. This is the set of all products $xy$ with $y \in M$.
\item Finally, the two-sided ideal generated by $y$ is denoted by $MyM$. This is the set of all $xyz$, with $x$, $z \in M$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

By definition, an ideal is a subset of the presented monoid---so it is a set of equivalence classes of words. We're going to abuse notation slightly, and also talk about \emph{words} as being elements of certain ideals. This will be taken to mean that the element of $M$ represented by that word belongs to the ideal.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:tpqa right ideal}
The letters of $A$ generate pairwise-disjoint right ideals that partition $M$:
\[
M := \{1\} \cup tM \cup pM \cup qM \cup aM
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If $x \in A$ is a letter, then $z \in xM$ if and only if $z \Eq xy$ for some $y \in A^*$. None of the rules in $R$ can change the first letter of a word, so $z$ must also start with $x$. There are four letters in our presentation, so apart from the identity element 1, these four right ideals form a disjoint partition of $M$.
\end{proof}
We now use the $\Rank$ mapping from \DefRef{def:tpqa rank} together with the above lemma to describe the equivalence class of $ta^n$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:tpqa tai class}
If $x \in A_0^*$ and $\Rank(x) = n$, then $tx \Eq ta^n$. Conversely, every element in the equivalence class of $ta^n$ has this form, so for $n \geq 0$, the equivalence class of $ta^n$ is the regular language given by the following expression:
\[
t \cdot \{p, q\}^* \cdot (a \cdot \{p, q\}^*)^n
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For the first part, suppose that $x \in A_0^*$, and consider $\Phi(tx)$. We walk $tx$ from left to right, skipping over $a$, while erasing $p$ and $q$. We are left with $ta^n$, where $n$ is the number of occurrences of $a$ in $x$, so $n = \Rank(x)$.

For the second part, recall from \LemmaRef{lemma:tpqa right ideal} that the letters of $A$ generate disjoint right ideals, so in particular, any word equivalent to $ta^n$ belongs to the regular set $t \cdot A_0^*$. We also just showed that $tx \Eq ty$ if and only if $\Rank(x) = \Rank(y)$, and so the equivalence class of $ta^n$ is precisely the set of all $tx$ where $x \in A_0^*$ and $\Rank(x)=n$. A moment's thought reveals that this is exactly the regular expression shown.
\end{proof}

For example, the above tells us that $taa$ is equivalent to $tpaqa$, $taaqp$, and so on. 

The left ideal structure of $M$ is more subtle. We're going to start by looking at the left ideals $M_0p$ and $M_0q$ of $M_0$, before discussing the situation in $M$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:tpqa p q ideal}
Suppose that $x \in A_0^*$, and $s \in \PAndQ$. Then $x \in M_0s$ if and only if $\Phi(x) =ysa^n$ for some $y \in A_0^*$ and $n \geq 0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
First, assume that $x \in M_0s$, so $x \Eq zs$ for some $z \in A_0^*$. Note that $xas \Eq zsas \Eq zsa \Eq xa$. By \LemmaRef{lemma:tpqa phi a}, $\Phi(xa)=\Phi(x) \cdot a$. Therefore, $\Phi(\Phi(x) \cdot as)=\Phi(x) \cdot a$. To reduce $\Phi(x) \cdot as$ to $\Phi(x)\cdot a$, we must apply a rule $(sa^ns, sa^n)$ for some $n > 0$. Thus, $\Phi(x)=ysa^{n-1}$ for some $y \in A_0^*$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:disjoint partition}
The following is a disjoint partition of $M_0$:
\[
M_0 := \{a\}^* \cup M_0p \cup M_0q
\]
The following is a disjoint partition of $M$:
\[
M := \{a\}^* \cup M_0p \cup M_0q \cup MtM
\]
Furthermore, the left ideals $Mp$ and $Mq$ of $M$ are related to the left ideals $M_0p$ and $M_0q$ of $M_0$ by the following:
\begin{gather*}
Mp = M_0p \cup MtM \\
Mq = M_0q \cup MtM
\end{gather*}
In particular, $Mp \cap Mq = MtM$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Suppose that $x \in A_0^*$. If $x \in a^*$, then $\Phi(x)$ does not contain $p$ or $q$, so $x$ is not in either $M_0p$ or $M_0q$ by the previous lemma. Otherwise, if $x \in M_0p$, then $\Phi(x)$ ends with $pa^m$ for some $m \geq 0$, so $\Phi(x)$ cannot simultaneously end with $qa^n$ for some other $n \geq 0$, and thus $x \not\in M_0q$. Similarly, if $x \in M_0q$, then $x \not\in M_0p$.

If we look at all of $M$, we see that each element of $M$ either belongs to $M_0$, or it is contained in the two-sided ideal $MtM$. In other words, $M_0 = M \setminus MtM$.

Finally, consider $Mp$ and $Mq$. If $x \in MtM$, we can write $x=ytz$ with $y \in A^*$, $z \in A_0^*$. By \LemmaRef{lemma:tpqa tai class}, we know that $tzp \Eq tzq \Eq tz$, so $x \in Mp \cap Mq$. For the other direction, suppose that $x \in Mp \cap Mq$. Since $M_0p \cap M_0q = \varnothing$, we have $x \in MtM$.
\end{proof}

Multiplication by $a$ does not change the left ideal membership of an element.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:tpqa closed a}
Suppose that $x \in A_0^*$, $s \in \PAndQ$, and $n > 0$. Then $x \in M_0s$ if and only if $x a^n \in M_0s$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By \LemmaRef{lemma:tpqa p q ideal}, $x \in M_0s$ if and only if $\Phi(x)=ysa^m$ for some $y \in A_0^*$ and $m \geq 0$. By \LemmaRef{lemma:tpqa phi a}, this holds if and only if $\Phi(xa^n) = ysa^{m+n}$ for all $n \geq 0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:m0 s regular set}
Suppose that $x \in A_0^*$ and $s \in \PAndQ$. Then $x \in M_0s$ if and only if $x$ is an element of this regular set:
\[
x \in a^* \cdot \PAndQ \cdot A_0^* \cdot s \cdot a^*
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By \CorollaryRef{cor:disjoint partition}, we know that $x \in A_0^* \setminus a^*$, or in other words:
\[
x \in a^* \cdot \PAndQ \cdot A_0^*
\]
By \LemmaRef{lemma:tpqa p q ideal}, $x$ also has a suffix in $s \cdot a^*$, so:
\[
x \in A_0^* \cdot s \cdot a^*
\]
Intersecting these two regular sets gives us the desired statement.
\end{proof}

Suppose we take a word in $A_0^*$, and replace some prefix with another word, while leaving the suffix unchanged. We would like to understand exactly when this can change the left ideal membership of the word in question.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:tpqa prefix change}
Let $x$, $y$, $z \in A_0^*$, $s \in \PAndQ$. If $xz \in M_0s$, and $yz \not\in M_0s$, then $x \in M_0s$, $y \not\in M_0s$, and $z \in a^*$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Consider the word $z$. If $z \not\in a^*$, then by \CorollaryRef{cor:disjoint partition}, we either have $z \in M_0p$ or $z \in M_0 q$. If $z \in M_0p$, then $xz \in M_0p$ because $M_0p$ is a left ideal, so in fact $s=p$, but now, we also have $yz \in M_0p$, which we assumed is not the case. Thus, $z \not\in M_0p$, and a similar argument shows that $z \not\in M_0q$. The only remaining possibility is $z \in a^*$, so let's say that $z=a^n$ for some $n \geq 0$. We have $xa^n \in M_0s$ and $ya^n \not\in M_0s$ by assumption. Applying \LemmaRef{lemma:tpqa closed a}, we conclude that $x \in M_0s$ and $y \not\in M_0s$.
\end{proof}

We will illustrate the above with two examples. In the first example, the invariant suffix is an element of $\{ a \}^*$, so replacing the prefix can ``get us out'' of the left ideal $M_0p$:
\begin{gather*}
(paqapa)aaaaa \in M_0p \\
(qaapaaq)aaaaa \in M_0q \\
(aaa)aaaaa \in \{ a \}^*
\end{gather*}
However, if the suffix already contains $p$ or $q$, we're stuck no matter what we do:
\begin{gather*}
(paqapa)aaqaa \in M_0q \\
(qaapaaq)aaqaa \in M_0q \\
(aaa)aaqaa \in M_0q
\end{gather*}
This observation will play a key role in our main proof.

\paragraph{Finite complete presentations of $M$.}
Now, we assume that we have a finite complete presentation $\Pres{C}{S}$ of $M$, and derive a contradiction. The contradiction will necessarily depend on the assumption that $S$ is finite, because as we saw, $\Pres{A}{\RInf}$ is a perfectly reasonable \emph{infinite} complete presentation of $M$. In what follows, lowercase Greek letters will denote words over $C^*$, while Roman letters besides $t$, $p$, $q$, $a$ will continue to denote words over $A^*$.

We begin by fixing an isomorphism between the monoids presented by $\Pres{C}{S}$ and $\AR$, and lift this isomorphism to a pair of homomorphisms of free monoids between $C^*$ and $A^*$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:tpqa psi xi}
There is a ``decoding'' \index{monoid homomorphism}homomorphism $\Psi$, which maps each symbol of $C$ to a word over $A^*$:
\[
\Psi\colon C^* \rightarrow A^*
\]
There is also an ``encoding'' \index{monoid homomorphism}homomorphism $\Xi$ for the other direction:
\[
\Xi\colon A^* \rightarrow C^*
\]
These mappings satisfy certain properties:
\begin{itemize}
\item For all $\rho$, $\sigma \in C^*$, $\rho \Eq_{S} \sigma$ if and only if $\Psi(\rho) \Eq \Psi(\sigma)$.
\item For all $x$, $y \in A^*$, $x \Eq y$ if and only if $\Xi(x) \Eq_{S} \Xi(y)$.
\item For all $x \in A^*$, $x \Eq \Psi(\Xi(x))$.
\item For all $\sigma \in C^*$, $\sigma \Eq_{S} \Xi(\Psi(\sigma))$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

Without loss of generality, we can also assume that $\Psi$ is \IndexDefinition{non-erasing homomorphism}\emph{non-erasing}, meaning that it satisfies $|\Psi(\alpha)|>0$ for all $\alpha \in C$. For if $\Phi(\alpha) = 1$ for some $\alpha \in C$, we have $\alpha \Eq_S 1$, so we can erase this symbol from any rules of $S$ that mention it, delete any resulting trivial rules, and finally, delete the symbol from $C$.

Finally, we assume that no rule in $S$ has a left-hand side that contains the left-hand side of some other rule. That is, we say that $S$ does not contain any overlaps of the first kind (\DefRef{overlappingrules}). If any such rules were to appear in a complete presentation that is handed to us, we could simply apply \AlgRef{left simplification} to remove them, without changing the reduction relation $\Red_S$. Only needing to consider overlaps of the second kind simplifies a section of our proof.

\begin{definition}
Since $\Pres{C}{S}$ is complete, $\Irr(S)$ contains one unique representative from each equivalence class of $\Eq_S$, and the \index{normal form algorithm}normal form algorithm assigns to each word in $C^*$ an irreducible word, equivalent to the original word via $\Eq_{S}$. We denote this mapping by $\Lambda$:
\[
\Lambda\colon C^* \rightarrow \Irr(S)
\]
\end{definition}

From any finite complete presentation~$\Pres{C}{S}$ of~$M$, we can split off a finite complete presentation of~$M_0$, by removing all letters and rules mapped to $A^* \setminus A_0^*$ by~$\Psi$.

\begin{definition}
Let $C_0 \subsetneq C$ consist of all $\alpha \in C$ with $\Psi(\alpha) \in A_0^*$, and let $S_0 \subsetneq S$ be the set of all $(\zeta,\eta)\in S$ with $\zeta$, $\eta \in C_0^*$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:tpqa extract m0}
The presentation $\Pres{C_0}{S_0}$ is a complete presentation of $M_0$, and:
\[\Irr(S) \cap C_0^* = \Irr(S_0)\]
Furthermore, our three mappings restrict as follows:
\begin{align*}
\Lambda&\colon C_0^*\rightarrow \Irr(S_0) \\
\Psi&\colon C_0^* \rightarrow A_0^* \\
\Xi&\colon A_0^* \rightarrow C_0^*
\end{align*}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $x$, $y \in A_0^*$, and assume that $x \Eq y$. We have $\Xi(x) \Eq_S \Xi(y)$. Since $\Xi(x)$, $\Xi(y) \in C_0^*$, we also have $\Xi(x) \Eq_{S_0} \Xi(y)$. For the other direction, assume that $\mu \Eq_{S_0} \nu$ for some $\mu$, $\nu \in C_0^*$. Then $\mu \Eq_S \nu$, and therefore $\Psi(\mu) \Eq \Psi(\nu)$. Finally, the restriction of $\Red_S$ to $C_0^*$ remains terminating and confluent, so $\Pres{C_0}{S_0}$ is complete.
\end{proof}

We're going to look at the $S$-normal forms of words of the form $ta^n$, with $n \geq 0$. This will ultimately allow us to construct two equivalent irreducible words in $\Irr(S_0)$, contradicting the assumption that $\Pres{C}{S}$ is a finite complete presentation.

To start, suppose that $\lambda \in C^*$ satisfies $\Psi(\lambda) \Eq ta^n$. Recall that \LemmaRef{lemma:tpqa tai class} gave us a regular expression for the equivalence class of $ta^n$ over $A^*$. In particular, an element in this equivalence class has the form $tx$, where $x \in A_0^*$, and $\Rank(x)=n$. This allows us to split up $\lambda$ into a ``$t$-like'' symbol from $C \setminus C_0$, followed by a ``$C_0^*$ suffix.''

\begin{definition}
Let $T$ be the set of all $\tau \in C$ such that $\Psi(\tau) \Eq ta^n$ for some $n \geq 0$.
\end{definition}

It is certainly possible that $C \setminus C_0$ may contain symbols that are not in $T$, for example we might have an $\alpha \in C$ such that $\Psi(\alpha) = ata$. However, $T$ cannot be empty, because at the very least, $\Lambda(\Xi(t))$ must be a single symbol, and an element of $T$. For if $\Lambda(\Xi(t))$ had length greater than one, then the first symbol would give us a distinct irreducible word that also decodes to a word equivalent to $t$, which is a contradiction.

\begin{lemma}\label{t a decoding lemma}
Suppose that $\lambda \in C^*$, and $\Psi(\lambda) \Eq ta^n$ for some $n \geq 0$. Then, $\lambda = \tau \sigma$ for some $\tau \in T$ and $\sigma \in C_0^*$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Since $\Psi(\lambda)$ is non-empty, we can write $\lambda=\tau \sigma$ for some $\tau \in C$ and $\sigma \in C^*$. Since $\Psi(\tau \sigma) \Eq ta^n$, we can apply \LemmaRef{lemma:tpqa tai class} to write $\Psi(\tau \sigma) = tx$ for some $x \in A_0^*$. Since $\Psi$ is non-erasing, $\Psi(\tau)=ty$, where $y$ is a prefix of $x$. Since $y \in A_0^*$, $ty \Eq ta^n$ where $n=\Rank(y)$, and so $\tau \in T$. Finally, since $\Psi(\sigma)$ is a suffix of $x$, it follows that $\sigma \in C_0^*$.
\end{proof}

In particular, suppose we have a rewrite path over $S$ that joins two words in the equivalence class of $ta^n$. Every intermediate word visited by this path decodes to a word equivalent to $ta^n$, and thus every intermediate word has the form given above.

Recall that at each step of the \index{normal form algorithm}normal form algorithm, we scan the input string for occurrences of a left-hand side of a rule. It may be the case that the input string contains more than one such occurrence.
\begin{definition}\label{def:rightmost strategy}
The specific choice of reduction made by the normal form algorithm is referred to as the \emph{strategy}:
\begin{itemize}
\item The \emph{leftmost} strategy rewrites the first occurrence of a
left-hand side.
\item The \emph{rightmost} strategy rewrites the last occurrence of a left-hand side.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

As long as the reduction relation is confluent, any strategy will always yield the same normal form when iterated to fixed point. For this reason, we left the strategy unspecified when we first encountered the normal form algorithm (\AlgRef{alg:normal form}). The implementation of the normal form algorithm in the Requirement Machine (\AlgRef{term reduction trie algo}) used the leftmost strategy. For our proof below, we will consider what happens if we compute $S$-normal forms using the rightmost strategy, for the following reason.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:rightmost}
Suppose that $\sigma \in C^*$, and we use the rightmost strategy to construct a positive rewrite path from $\sigma$ to $\Lambda(\sigma)$.  If $\mu (\zeta \Red \eta) \nu$ is a \index{rewrite step}rewrite step along this path, and $\zeta'$ is a proper suffix of $\zeta$, then $\zeta' \nu \in \Irr(S)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Recall from the discussion following \DefRef{def:tpqa psi xi} that $S$ does not contain any overlaps of the first kind, so no rule has a left-hand side that contains the left-hand side of some other rule. Thus, $\zeta' \in \Irr(S)$, and if $\zeta' \nu$ were to contain the left-hand side of some other rule from~$S$, it would either straddle the boundary between $\zeta'$ and $\nu$, or be contained entirely within $\nu$. In either case, the source of our rewrite step, $\mu \zeta \nu$, would contain another reduction with a right whisker that is a proper suffix of $\nu$. This contradicts our assumption that the positive rewrite path was constructed via the rightmost strategy.
\end{proof}

Finally, we get to the first of two key steps in our proof of \ThmRef{thm:monoid m is not fcrs}.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:distill long suffix}
If $\Pres{C}{S}$ is a finite complete presentation of $M$ and $\Pres{C_0}{S_0}$ is the associated finite complete presentation of $M_0$, then for any $l \geq 0$, we can find a pair of words $\zeta$, $\nu \in C_0^*$, such that:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\zeta\nu \in \Irr(S_0)$,
\item $\Psi(\zeta) \in M_0p \cup M_0q$,
\item $\Psi(\nu) \in a^*$,
\item $|\nu| \geq l$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Consider $\Lambda(\Xi(ta^n)) \in \Irr(S)$. By \LemmaRef{lemma:tpqa extract m0}, we can write:
\[
\Lambda(\Xi(t a^n))=\tau \sigma\]
for some $\tau \in T$ and $\sigma \in C_0^*$. We will choose a sufficiently large~$n$ that depends on~$l$ later, but for now, just assume that we are working with an arbitrary $n \geq 0$. We begin by constructing a different word over $C^*$ that is equivalent to $\tau \sigma$.

Since $\Psi(\tau\sigma) \Eq ta^n$, we have, for both $s \in \PAndQ$:
\[
\Psi(\tau\sigma) \cdot s \Eq \Psi(\tau\sigma)
\]
or in other words:
\[
\tau\sigma \cdot \Xi(s) \Eq_S \tau\sigma
\]
However, if we look at the $C_0^*$ suffix $\sigma$, then by \LemmaRef{lemma:tpqa p q ideal}, there is at least one $s \in \PAndQ$ such that $\Psi(\sigma) \not\in M_0s$. On the other hand, $\Psi(\sigma) \cdot s \in M_0s$ by definition, so in fact:
\[
\Psi(\sigma) \cdot s \NotEq \Psi(\sigma)
\]
or equivalently:
\[
\sigma \cdot \Xi(s) \NotEq_S \sigma
\]

Next, we apply the normal form algorithm to $\tau \sigma \cdot \Xi(s)$, using the rightmost stategy described in \DefRef{def:rightmost strategy}. We get a positive rewrite path with source $\tau\sigma \cdot \Xi(s)$ and destination $\tau \sigma$, involving rules from~$S$. These words are distinct, so our path has at least one step.

By \LemmaRef{t a decoding lemma}, each intermediate word visited along our path has the form $\tau_i \sigma_i$ for some $t$-like symbol $\tau_i \in T$, and $C_0^*$ suffix $\sigma_i \in C_0^*$. Consider how these $C_0^*$ suffixes change as we move along our path. The $C_0^*$ suffix of the source of the first step is $\sigma \cdot \Xi(s)$, and this is an element of the left ideal $M_0s$. The $C_0^*$ suffix of the destination of the final step is $\sigma$, which was \emph{not} an element of $M_0s$, by our choice of $s \in \PAndQ$.

It follows that our rewrite path must contain at least one step, say with source $\tau_i \sigma_i$ and destination $\tau_{i+1} \sigma_{i+1}$, where the left ideal membership of the $C_0^*$ suffix must flip:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\Psi(\sigma_i) \in M_0s$
\item $\Psi(\sigma_{i+1}) \not\in M_0s$
\end{enumerate}

Now, a rewrite step with source $\tau_i \sigma_i$ and destination $\tau_{i+1} \sigma_{i+1}$ takes one of two forms:
\begin{enumerate}
\item We're applying a rule $(\zeta,\eta) \in S_0$ to the $C_0^*$ suffix of a word, leaving the $t$-like symbol~$\tau_i$ unchanged. In this case, our step has a non-empty left whisker of the form $\tau_i \mu$, for some $\mu \in C_0^*$:
\[
\tau_i \mu (\zeta \Red \eta) \nu
\]
In this case, $\tau_{i+1} = \tau_i$, $\sigma_i = \mu \zeta \nu$, and $\sigma_{i+1} = \mu \eta \nu$.
\item We're applying a rule $(\tau_i \zeta, \tau_{i+1} \eta) \in S \setminus S_0$ to a prefix of a word, or possibly the entire word. In this case, our step has an empty left whisker, and a right whisker $\nu \in C_0^*$:
\[
(\tau_i \zeta, \tau_{i+1} \eta) \nu
\]
In this case, $\sigma_i = \zeta \nu$ and $\sigma_{i+1} = \eta \nu$.
\end{enumerate}

Notice for any step of the first kind, we have $\sigma_i \Eq_S \sigma_{i+1}$. Since $\sigma_i \in M_0s$, in this case it also follows that $\sigma_{i+1} \in M_0s$. Thus, our path must contain at least one step of the second kind, say $( \tau_i \zeta \Red \tau_{i+1} \eta ) \nu$, with $\tau_i$, $\tau_{i+1} \in T$, $\zeta$, $\eta$, $\nu \in C_0^*$. This time, $\sigma_i \NotEq \sigma_{i+1}$. In fact, by assumption, we have:
\begin{gather*}
\Psi(\zeta) \cdot \Psi(\nu) = \Psi(\zeta \nu) = \Psi(\sigma_i) \in M_0s \\
\Psi(\eta) \cdot \Psi(\nu) = \Psi(\eta \nu) = \Psi(\sigma_{i+1}) \not\in M_0s
\end{gather*}
By \LemmaRef{lemma:tpqa prefix change}, we conclude that $\Psi(\nu) \in a^*$, $\Psi(\zeta) \in M_0s$, and $\Psi(\eta) \not\in M_0s$.

Furthermore, we assumed that our positive rewrite path was constructed via the rightmost strategy. By \LemmaRef{lemma:rightmost}, we also have $\zeta \nu \in \Irr(S_0) \subsetneq \Irr(S)$, since $\zeta$ is a proper suffix of the left-hand side $\tau_i \zeta$ of a rule in $S$.

Thus, we have a word $\zeta\nu \in \Irr(S_0)$, with $\Psi(\zeta) \in M_0s$ and $\Psi(\nu) \in a^*$, as desired.

It remains to show that we can choose a sufficiently large $n$ when computing $\Lambda(\Xi(ta^n))$, to obtain $\nu$ with $|\nu| \geq l$. We now use the assumption that both $C$ and $S$ are finite.

Since $C$ is finite, we note that there are only finitely many words in $C^*$ of any given length. Since $S$ is finite, we let $\Enn$ be the length of the longest left-hand side in~$S$.

On the other hand, the words $ta^n$ belong to distinct equivalence classes for distinct~$n$. We pick a sufficiently large $n$ with the property that for any word $\tau \sigma$, not necessarily irreducible, with $\tau \in T$, $\sigma \in C_0^*$, and $\Psi(\tau \sigma) \Eq ta^n$, we have $|\tau\sigma| \geq n(S) + l + 1$.

We then have $\tau_i \zeta \nu \geq n(S) + l + 1$ above. Recall that $\zeta$ is a proper suffix of a left-hand side in~$S$, so $|\zeta| < \Enn$. Since $|\tau| = 1$, we must have $|\nu| \geq l$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Finite complete presentations of  $M_0$.}
Finite complete presentations of~$M_0$ are easy to construct, and we saw one at the beginning of this section when we first looked at the requirement machine for our generic signature. Here, $c$ and $d$ play the role of associated type symbols:
\[
\Pres{p,q,a,c,d}{pa \Eq c,\, qa \Eq d,\, ca \Eq cc,\, cp \Eq c,\, da \Eq dd,\, dq \Eq d}
\]
Let's see if there are any irreducible words $\zeta\eta$ that satisfy the conditions of \LemmaRef{lemma:distill long suffix} with $\eta$ of arbitrary length. If $\Psi(\zeta) \in M_0s$ for some $s \in \PAndQ$, then $\zeta$ contains one of $\{p, q, c, d\}$. However, an $a$ that follows any of those letters is always reducible here. Since $\zeta\eta$ is irreducible, we conclude that $\eta$ cannot contain $a$ at all, and any $a$'s must only appear at the beginning of $\zeta$. For example:
\begin{gather*}
\Lambda(aaaqpaqaaa) = aaaqcddd
\end{gather*}

This is already enough to conclude that this specific finite complete presentation of $M_0$ cannot possibly be \emph{extended} to a finite complete presentation of $M$. However, this is not the only finite complete presentation of $M_0$. For example, we can add three symbols $A \Eq aa$, $c \Eq paaaa$, $d \Eq qaaaa$, and attempt Knuth-Bendix completion with the shortlex order where $c < d < p < q < A < a$. This yields a finite complete presentation with 32 rules that we will not show here.

If we compute some normal forms in this more complicated presentation, we now observe the following pattern:
\begin{gather*}
\Lambda(pa) = pa \\
\Lambda(paa) = pA \\
\Lambda(paaa) = pAa \\
\Lambda(paaaa) = c \\
\Lambda(paaaaa) = ca \\
\cdots
\end{gather*}
This presentation has two symbols, $a$ and $A$, that map into $a^*$ under $\Phi$. An irreducible word may have a suffix $a$, $A$, or $Aa$, but if it contains any other letter, these are the \emph{only} possible suffixes that decode to $a^*$; anything longer reduces to $c$ or $d$, which ``gobble up'' four $a$'s at a time. Thus, our second finite complete presentation of $M_0$ cannot be extended to a finite complete presentation of $M$, for the same reason.

We will now show that \emph{every} finite complete presentation of $M_0$ has this property, where the maximum length of an $a^*$ suffix is bounded if the word contains at least one other symbol that does not decode to a word in $a^*$. This shows that \LemmaRef{thm:monoid m is not fcrs} leads to a contradiction, and the original assumption that $\Pres{C}{S}$ is a finite complete presentation of $M$ cannot hold, which proves \ThmRef{thm:monoid m is not fcrs}.

We now use the material from \SecRef{sec:regular languages}. Since $S_0$ is finite, $\Irr(S_0)$ is a regular language by \LemmaRef{irr r facts}, and the \index{pumping lemma}pumping lemma applies. For the duration of this section, let $\Ell$ denote the pumping length of $\Irr(S_0)$, and let $\EnnZ$ denote the length of the longest left-hand side in $S_0$.

\begin{lemma}\label{name factor lemma}
Let $\Pres{C_0}{S_0}$ be a finite complete presentation of $M_0$, and let $s \in \PAndQ$. Suppose that $\zeta \in C_0$ and $\eta \in C_0^*$ satisfy the following conditions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\zeta\eta \in \Irr(S_0)$,
\item $\Psi(\zeta) \in M_0s$,
\item $\Psi(\eta) \in a^*$.
\end{enumerate}
Then $|\eta| < \Ell$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
We will assume that $|\eta| \geq \Ell$, and derive a contradiction. Since $\eta \geq \Ell$, applying \index{pumping lemma}\LemmaRef{pumping lemma} to $\zeta \eta$ ``from the right'' yields a factorization:
\[\zeta \eta = \zeta \eta_0 \eta_1 \eta_2\]
with the property that:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $|\eta_1| > 0$,
\item $|\eta_1\eta_2| \leq \Ell$,
\item $\zeta \eta_0 \eta_1^n \eta_2 \in \Irr(S_0)$ for all $n \geq 0$.
\end{enumerate}
Furthermore, $\Psi(\eta) \in a^*$ implies that $\Psi(\eta_0)$, $\Psi(\eta_1)$, $\Psi(\eta_2) \in a^*$. Now, $\Irr(S_0)$ is closed under taking factors (part 2 of \LemmaRef{irr r facts}), so we can drop the suffix $\eta_2$ from $\eta_0 \eta_1^n \eta_2$, and conclude that for all $n \geq 0$:
\[
\zeta \eta_0 \eta_1^n \in \Irr(S_0) \tag{1}
\]
Here is a picture. The second row shows a regular expression for the image of the first row under $\Psi$; recall that \LemmaRef{lemma:m0 s regular set} gave us the regular expression for $\Psi(\zeta)$:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{array}{|c|c|c|}
\hline
\zeta & \eta_0 & \eta_1 \\
\hline
a^* \cdot \PAndQ \cdot A_0^* \cdot s \cdot a^* & a^* & a^+ \\
\hline
\end{array}
\]
\end{ceqn}

Next, we will construct three words $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma \in C_0^*$ such that:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\Psi(\alpha) \in a^+$,
\item $\Psi(\beta) \in a^* \cdot s \cdot \{\AS\}^*$,
\item $\Psi(\gamma) \in \{\AS \}^+$,
\item $\alpha^m \beta \gamma^n \in \Irr(S_0)$ for all $m$, $n \geq 0$.
\end{enumerate}
To accomplish this, we will first construct three words $\alpha'$, $\beta'$, $\gamma'$, and then fiddle around to get the desired words $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$.

For each pair $i$, $j > 0$, we can form the word $a^i s a^j$, map this word to a word in $C_0^*$, apply $\Lambda$ to get an irreducible word in $\Irr(C_0)$. Since no two such words are equivalent under $\Eq$ by \LemmaRef{lemma:ai s aj distinct}, all of these normal forms are also distinct elements of $C_0^*$. By \LemmaRef{lemma:ai s aj class}, we have:
\[
\Psi(\Lambda(\Xi(a^i s a^j))) \in a^+ \cdot s \cdot \{\AS\}^+
\]
Since the alphabet $C_0$ is finite, the words $\Lambda(\Xi(a^i s a^j))$ become arbitrarily long as we increase $i$ and $j$. It follows that when $i$ and $j$ are sufficiently large, we can write $\Lambda(\Xi(a^i s a^j))$ in the form:
\[
\Lambda(\Xi(a^i s a^j)) = \alpha' \beta' \gamma'
\]
where:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\Psi(\alpha') \in a^+$ and $|\alpha'| \geq \Ell$,
\item $\Psi(\beta') \in a^* \cdot s \cdot \{\AS\}^*$ and $|\beta'| \geq \EnnZ$,
\item $\Psi(\gamma') \in \{\AS\}^+$ and $|\gamma'| \geq \Ell$.
\end{enumerate}
Here is a picture---the bottom row shows the regular expression for the image of the top row under $\Psi$:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{array}{|c||c||c|}
\hline
\alpha' & \beta' & \gamma' \\
\hline
a^+ & a^* \cdot s \cdot \{\AS\}^* & \{\AS\}^+ \\
\hline
\end{array}
\]
\end{ceqn}
Since $|\alpha'| \geq \Ell$, we can apply \index{pumping lemma}\LemmaRef{pumping lemma} to the word $\alpha' \beta'$ ``from the left'' to get a factorization $\alpha' \beta' = \alpha_0 \alpha_1 \alpha_2 \beta'$, where:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $|\alpha_1| > 0$,
\item $|\alpha_0 \alpha_1| \leq \Ell$,
\item $\alpha_0 \alpha_1^n \alpha_2 \beta' \in \Irr(S_0)$, for all $n \geq 0$.
\end{enumerate}
By \LemmaRef{irr r facts}, we can chop off the prefix $\alpha_0$, and conclude that $\alpha_1^n \alpha_2 \beta' \in \Irr(S_0)$, for all $n \geq 0$. Here is a picture:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{array}{|c|c||c|}
\hline
\alpha_1^n & \alpha_2 & \beta' \\
\hline
a^+ & a^* & a^* \cdot s \cdot \{\AS\}^* \\
\hline
\end{array}
\]
\end{ceqn}

Similarly, we can apply \index{pumping lemma}\LemmaRef{pumping lemma} to $\beta' \gamma'$ ``from the right,'' to get a factorization $\beta' \gamma' = \beta' \gamma_2 \gamma_1 \gamma_0$, where:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $|\gamma_1| > 0$,
\item $|\gamma_1 \gamma_0| \leq \Ell$,
\item $\beta' \gamma_2 \gamma_1^n \gamma_0 \in \Irr(S_0)$, for all $n \geq 0$.
\end{enumerate}
By \LemmaRef{irr r facts}, we can chop off the suffix $\gamma_0$, and conclude that $\beta' \gamma_2 \gamma_1^n \in \Irr(S_0)$, for all $n \geq 0$. Here is a picture:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{array}{|c||c|c|}
\hline
\beta' & \gamma_2 & \gamma_1 \\
\hline
a^* \cdot s \cdot \{\AS\}^* & \{\AS\}^* & \{\AS\}^+ \\
\hline
\end{array}
\]
\end{ceqn}

We've constructed two infinite families of irreducible words, the first ending with $\beta'$ and the second starting with $\beta'$:
\begin{align*}
\alpha_1^m \alpha_2 & \beta' \\
& \beta' \gamma_2 \gamma_1^n
\end{align*}
Recalling that $|\beta'| \geq \EnnZ$, we apply \LemmaRef{irreducible overlap lemma}, and conclude that for all $m$, $n \geq 0$, we have:
\[
\alpha_1^m \alpha_2 \beta' \gamma_2 \gamma_1^n \in \Irr(S_0)
\]

Finally, we set:
\begin{align*}
\alpha &:= \alpha_1 \\
\beta &:= \alpha_2 \beta' \gamma_2 \\
\gamma &:= \gamma_1
\end{align*}
Here is a picture:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{array}{|c|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
\alpha & \multicolumn{3}{c|}{\beta} & \gamma \\
\hline
\alpha_1 & \alpha_2 & \beta' & \gamma_2 & \gamma_1 \\
\hline
a^+ & a^* & a^* \cdot s \cdot \{\AS\}^* & \{\AS\}^* & \{\AS\}^+ \\
\hline
\end{array}
\]
\end{ceqn}

Notice that $\Psi(\alpha) \in a^+$ and $\Psi(\gamma) \in \{\AS \}^+$ by construction. Also, by concatenating the three regular expressions for $\alpha_2$, $\beta'$, and $\gamma_2$, we see that:
\[
\Psi(\beta) \in
(a^*) \cdot (a^* \cdot s \cdot \{\AS\}^*) \cdot (\{\AS\}^*) = a^* \cdot s \cdot \{\AS\}^*
\]

Now, recall our words $\zeta$, $\eta_0$, $\eta_1$ from (1). All that remains to get our contradiction is to ``connect the two pumps.'' We set:
\begin{gather*}
i := \Rank(\Psi(\eta_1)) \\
j := \Rank(\Psi(\alpha)) \\
k := \Rank(\Psi(\gamma))
\end{gather*}
Notice that $i$, $j$, $k > 0$, because the regular expressions for $\eta_1$, $\alpha$, and $\gamma$ all involve at least one occurrence of $a$.

Since $\Rank$ is a homomorphism, we have:
\[\Rank(\Psi(\eta_1^j)) = \Rank(\Psi(\alpha^i))\]
Furthermore, since $\Psi(\eta_1)$, $\Psi(\alpha) \in a^+$ and the restriction of $\Rank$ to $a^*$ is injective by \LemmaRef{lemma:tpqa rank}:
\[
\Psi(\eta_1^j) = \Psi(\alpha^i)
\]
On the other hand, two distinct irreducible words cannot map to the same word under $\Psi$, so we conclude that:
\[
\eta_1^j = \alpha^i
\]

For example, if $i=3$ and $j=4$, we have the following situation:
\begin{ceqn}
\begin{align*}
\begin{array}{|c|c|}
\hline
\zeta & \eta_0 \\
\hline
\cdots & \cdots \\
\hline
\end{array}
\quad&
\begin{array}{|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
\eta_1 & \eta_1 & \eta_1 & \eta_1 \\
\hline
aaa & aaa & aaa & aaa \\
\hline
\end{array}\\[\medskipamount]
&{\setlength{\arraycolsep}{6.75pt}
\begin{array}{|c|c|c|}
\hline
\alpha & \alpha & \alpha \\
\hline
aaaa & aaaa & aaaa \\
\hline
\end{array}}
\quad
\begin{array}{|c|c|}
\hline
\beta & \gamma \\
\hline
\cdots & \cdots \\
\hline
\end{array}
\end{align*}
\end{ceqn}

Now, for sufficiently large $l>0$, we have $|\alpha^{il}| > \EnnZ$. We fix such an $l > 0$, and we see that, for all $m$, $n \geq 0$:
\begin{gather*}
\alpha^{ilm} \beta \gamma^n \in \Irr(S_0) \\
\zeta \eta_0 \eta_1^{jlm} \in \Irr(S_0)
\end{gather*}
Now, we also assume that $m > 0$. Consider the word $\zeta \eta_0 \alpha^{ilm} \beta \gamma^n$. Since $|\alpha^{ilm}| > \EnnZ$, by \LemmaRef{irreducible overlap lemma}, for all $m > 0$, $n \geq 0$:
\[
\zeta \eta_0 \alpha^{ilm} \beta \gamma^n \in \Irr(S_0)
\]
Finally, the fun part. We also have:
\begin{gather*}
\Rank(\alpha^{k}) = jk\\
\Rank(\gamma^{j}) = jk
\end{gather*}
So, we consider these two words:
\begin{gather*}
\zeta \eta_0 \alpha^{il} \beta \gamma^j \\
\zeta \eta_0 \alpha^{il + k} \beta
\end{gather*}
We showed above that both of these words are in $\Irr(S_0)$. But also:
\begin{gather*}
\Rank(\eta_0 \alpha^{il} \beta \gamma^j) = \Rank(\eta_0) + ijl + \Rank(\beta) + jk \\
\Rank(\eta_0 \alpha^{il+k} \beta) = \Rank(\eta_0) + ijl + jk + \Rank(\beta)
\end{gather*}
Therefore our two words are actually equivalent under $\Eq$:
\[
\zeta \eta_0 \alpha^{il} \beta \gamma^j \Eq \zeta \eta_0 \alpha^{il + k} \beta
\]
