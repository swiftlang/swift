\chapter{Completion}\label{chap:completion}

\IndexDefinition{Knuth-Bendix algorithm}%
\index{completion!z@\igobble|seealso{Knuth-Bendix algorithm}}
\lettrine{K}{nuth-Bendix completion} is the central algorithm in the Requirement Machine. Completion attempts to construct a \index{complete presentation}\index{finite complete presentation}finite complete rewriting system from a monoid presentation by adding new rules. As we saw in \SecRef{sec:normal form algorithm}, a complete rewriting system allows us to decide if two words are equivalent, by computing and comparing their normal forms. Together with the results of \SecRef{sec:rqm correctness}, completion is how we arrive at a \emph{decision procedure} for the \index{derived requirement!decision procedure}derived requirements formalism. Recall that our encoding of explicit requirements as rewrite rules has the property that a \emph{derived} requirement then becomes a rewrite path over those rewrite rules. Thus, once we have a finite complete rewriting system, we can answer \index{generic signature query}generic signature queries and perform \index{requirement minimization}minimization.

This chapter begins with a self-contained description of the Knuth-Bendix algorithm, together with some optimizations specific to the Requirement Machine. This can be supplemented by any textbook on rewriting, such as \cite{book2012string} or \cite{andallthat}. We will then look at some examples of completion with specific generic signatures. Finally, we will end the chapter by studying a generic signature that cannot be solved by the Requirement Machine, in \SecRef{sec:event horizon}. Unlike the undecidable example from \ThmRef{undecidablemonoid}, this generic signature defines a monoid with a decidable word problem, but nonetheless, one that cannot be presented by a finite complete rewriting system.

\paragraph{The algorithm.}  The original paper by Knuth and Bendix described the algorithm for term rewriting systems~\cite{Knuth1983}; a correctness proof was later given by Huet in \cite{HUET198111}. A survey of techniques related to completion appears in \cite{BUCHBERGER19873}. In our case, the terms being rewritten are words in a free monoid; this situation was studied in \cite{narendran}. We will work through a bottom-up description of the algorithm, as a series of subroutines:
\begin{itemize}
\item \AlgRef{add rule derived algo} adds a new rule discovered while resolving a \emph{critical pair}.
\item \AlgRef{critical pair algo} resolves a critical pair, where the left-hand sides of two given rules are known to \emph{overlap} at a fixed position.
\item \AlgRef{overlap trie lookup} finds all rules that overlap a given rule at a fixed position.
\item \AlgRef{find overlapping rule algo} finds all rules with overlapping left-hand sides.
\item \AlgRef{alg:knuthbendix} is the main loop, which repeatedly resolves critical pairs until \emph{local confluence} is achieved.
\end{itemize}
We will then see two more algorithms to simplify the rewriting system in \SecRef{sec:rule simplification}.

\paragraph{Local confluence.}
Our ultimate goal is to construct a list of rules whose \index{reduction relation}reduction relation $\Red$ satisfies the \index{Church-Rosser property}Church-Rosser property, which as we recall, means that if $x\Eq y$ are two \index{monoid congruence}equivalent words, then $x\Red z$ and $y\Red z$ for some word $z$. By \ThmRef{church rosser theorem}, this is equivalent to the reduction relation being \index{terminating reduction relation}terminating and \index{confluence}confluent. As we saw in \SecRef{sec:normal form algorithm}, termination can be established using a reduction order. While confluence is difficult to verify directly, a related concept is easier to check.
\begin{definition}
A pair of \index{positive rewrite step}positive rewrite steps $(s_1, s_2)$ with $\Src(s_1)=\Src(s_2)$ is called a \IndexDefinition{critical pair}\emph{critical pair}.
\end{definition}
\begin{definition}
A reduction relation $\Red$ is \IndexDefinition{local confluence}\emph{locally confluent} if every critical pair $(s_1, s_2)$ has the property that there exists a word $z$ such that $\Dst(s_1)\Red z$ and $\Dst(s_2)\Red z$.
\end{definition}
So local confluence is the special case of confluence where we ``diverge'' by one step in each direction. The following \index{Newman's lemma}result~\cite{newman} relates confluence with local confluence:
\begin{theorem}[Newman's Lemma]
If a reduction relation $\Red$ is both \index{terminating reduction relation}terminating and locally confluent, then it is confluent.
\end{theorem}

We will see that local confluence can be decided algorithmically, and furthermore, we can ``repair'' any local \index{confluence violation}confluence violations we find.

\paragraph{Overlapping rules.}
Any non-trivial reduction relation has infinitely many critical pairs, but it turns out that all but a finite subset can be disregarded. Suppose we have two rules $(u, v)$ and $(u', v')$ over some alphabet $A$. By taking the left-hand sides of both rules together with any word $x\in A^*$, we can form the ``sandwich'' word $t := uxu'$. Every such choice of $x$ yields a new critical pair; the occurrences of $u$ and $u'$ within $t$ can be rewritten in two ways, by $s_1 := (u\Red v)xu'$ and $s_2 := ux(u'\Red v')$. Such critical pairs are called \IndexDefinition{orthogonal critical pair}\emph{orthogonal}. Here is a diagram:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
&uxu'\arrow[ld, Rightarrow, "s_1:=(u\Red v)xu'"']\arrow[rd, Rightarrow, "s_2:=ux(u'\Red v')"]\\
vxu'&&uxv'
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\end{ceqn}
Since $s_1$ and $s_2$ rewrite disjoint \index{factor}factors of $t$, an orthogonal critical pair cannot witness a local confluence violation. Regardless of whether we apply $s_1$ or $s_2$ first, there exists a complementary rewrite step $s_1'$ or $s_2'$ to rewrite $\Dst(s_1)$ or $\Dst(s_2)$ into the ``reduced sandwich'' $vxv'$:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
&uxu'\arrow[ld, Rightarrow, "s_1:=(u\Red v)xu'"']\arrow[rd, Rightarrow, "s_2:=ux(u'\Red v')"]\\
vxu'\arrow[rd, Rightarrow, "s_1' := vx(u'\Red v')"']&&uxv'\arrow[ld, Rightarrow, "s_2':=(v\Red v)xv'"]\\
&vxv'
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\end{ceqn}
We can also visualize an orthogonal critical pair using the ``pictorial'' notation for rewrite steps from \SecRef{sec:rewriting}:

\[
\begin{array}{cc}

\end{array}

\hline

Thus, to uncover local \index{confluence violation}confluence violations, we only need to inspect critical pairs that are \emph{not} orthogonal; that is, they must rewrite \emph{overlapping} \index{factor}factors of their common source word. There are only finitely many such critical pairs, and they can all be discovered by inspecting the left-hand sides of our rewrite rules.

\begin{definition}\label{overlappingrules}
A rule $(u, v)$ \IndexDefinition{overlapping rules}\emph{overlaps} with a rule $(u', v')$ if one of the following holds:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Overlap of the first kind.} The left-hand side of the first rule contains the left-hand side of the second. That is, $u=xyz$ and $u'=y$ for some $x$, $y$, $z\in A^*$:
\begin{align*}
x&yz\\
&y
\end{align*}
\item \textbf{Overlap of the second kind.} The left-hand side of the first rule has a suffix equal to a prefix of the left-hand side of the second rule. That is, $u=xy$ and $u'=yz$ for some $x$, $y$, $z\in A^*$, with $|x|>0$ and $|z|>0$:
\begin{align*}
x&y\\
&yz
\end{align*}
\end{enumerate}
In both cases, after a suitable choice of $x$, $y$, and~$z$, we define the \IndexDefinition{overlap word}\emph{overlap word} to be $xyz$, and the \IndexDefinition{overlap position}\emph{overlap position} to be $|x|$. Note that this definition is not symmetric; to say that $(u, v)$ overlaps with $(u', v')$ is not the same as $(u', v')$ overlapping with $(u, v)$. Both might still be true, of course.
\end{definition}
\begin{example}

Now, let's look at some concrete examples of overlaps where the words are Requirement Machine terms. Consider these three rules:
\begin{gather*}
\rT\cdot\assocsym{Collection}{SubSequence}\cdot\protosym{Equatable}\tag{1}\\
\rT\cdot\assocsym{Collection}{SubSequence}\Red\rU\tag{2}\\
\assocsym{Collection}{SubSequence}\cdot\assocsym{Collection}{Element}\Red\assocsym{Collection}{Element}\tag{3}
\end{gather*}
There is an overlap of the first kind between 1 and 2 at position 0; the left-hand side of 2 is contained entirely in the left-hand side of 1. Here, the overlap word is $\rT\cdot\assocsym{Collection}{SubSequence}\cdot\protosym{Equatable}$:
\begin{align*}
&\rT\cdot\assocsym{Collection}{SubSequence}\cdot\protosym{Equatable}\\
&\rT\cdot\assocsym{Collection}{SubSequence}
\end{align*}
There is also an overlap of the second kind between 1 and 3 at position 1; the left-hand side of 1 ends with the suffix $\assocsym{Collection}{SubSequence}$, which is a prefix of the left-hand side of 3. The overlap word is $\rT\cdot\assocsym{Collection}{SubSequence}\cdot\protosym{Equatable}$:
\begin{align*}
\rT\cdot{}&\assocsym{Collection}{SubSequence}\\
&\assocsym{Collection}{SubSequence}\cdot\assocsym{Collection}{Element}
\end{align*}
The definition of an overlap of the second kind requires both $x$ and $z$ to be non-empty. If we relaxed this condition, then we would \emph{also} have an overlap of the second kind between rule 2 and 1 at position 0:
\begin{align*}
&\rT\cdot\assocsym{Collection}{SubSequence}\\
&\rT\cdot\assocsym{Collection}{SubSequence}\cdot\protosym{Equatable}
\end{align*}
The overlap word and position are the same as the first example above, so attempting to \index{resolve critical pair}resolve this critical pair would not reveal anything new. Our definition avoids duplicated work in this case.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
It is important to note that the same two rules can overlap more than once at \emph{different} positions; in this case we must consider every possible overlap. Here is an abstract example over the alphabet $\{a, b\}$. Suppose we have two rules with left-hand sides $bbaba$ and $abab$. The first rule overlaps with the second rule at position 2:
\begin{align*}
bb&aba\\
  &abab
\end{align*}
There is another overlap between the first rule and the second rule at position 4:
\begin{align*}
bbab&a\\
    &abab
\end{align*}
Finally, the second rule overlaps with the first rule at position 3:
\begin{align*}
aba&b\\
   &bbaba
\end{align*}
The initial implementation of the Requirement Machine neglected to consider overlaps at more than one position, which could result in incorrect behavior~\cite{rqmoverlapbug}.
\end{example}

\paragraph{Resolving critical pairs.}
The two destination words of a critical pair are joined by \index{rewrite step!critical pair}rewrite steps, so they are equivalent under the \index{monoid congruence}monoid congruence generated by our rules. With the notation of \DefRef{overlappingrules}, where the two rules are $(u, v)$ and $(u', v')$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cc}
Overlap of the first kind&
Overlap of the second kind\\
\begin{tikzcd}
&xyz\arrow[ld, Rightarrow, "(u\Red v)"']\arrow[rd, Rightarrow, "x(u'\Red v')z"]\\
v&&xv'z
\end{tikzcd}&
\begin{tikzcd}
&xyz\arrow[ld, Rightarrow, "(u\Red v)z"']\arrow[rd, Rightarrow, "x(u'\Red v')"]\\
vz&&xv'
\end{tikzcd}
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
We now consider what happens if we reduce both sides using the normal form algorithm. This is called \IndexDefinition{resolve critical pair}\emph{resolving} the critical pair. Let's call the destination words of our rewrite steps $(t_1, t_2)$. We first apply \AlgRef{alg:trie normal form} to compute the normal form of $t_1$ and $t_2$ using the rewrite rules we have so far. Then, we compare the normal forms with our reduction order. There are several possible outcomes:
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $t_1$ and $t_2$ reduce to the same word, we say the \IndexDefinition{trivial critical pair}critical pair is \emph{trivial}.
\item If $t_1\Red \Lambda(t_1)$ and $t_2\Red \Lambda(t_2)$ with $\Lambda(t_1)\neq \Lambda(t_2)$, we found two distinct \index{irreducible word}irreducible words in the same \index{equivalence class!of terms}equivalence class: a \index{confluence violation}\emph{confluence violation}.

\begin{enumerate}
\item If $\Lambda(t_2) < \Lambda(t_1)$ in our reduction order, we repair the confluence violation by adding a new rewrite rule $(\Lambda(t_1), \Lambda(t_2))$. Now, $t_1$ and $t_2$ will both reduce to the same word $\Lambda(t_2)$.

\item If instead $\Lambda(t_1) < \Lambda(t_2)$, we resolve the critical pair by adding a new rewrite rule $(\Lambda(t_2), \Lambda(t_1))$, so that both $t_1$ and $t_2$ now reduce to $\Lambda(t_1)$.

\item If $\Lambda(t_1)$ and $\Lambda(t_2)$ are distinct but incomparable, we have a \index{non-orientable relation}non-orientable rule, and completion fails with an error.
\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
Note that case 1 above yields an alternate definition of local confluence: a reduction relation is locally confluent if all critical pairs are trivial. Case 2c cannot happen under the reduction order used by the Requirement Machine. The below diagram illustrates cases 1, 2a, and 2b; dashed arrows indicate new rules that must be added to resolve the critical pair:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccc}
Trivial critical pair&
Adding a new rule&
Adding a new rule\\
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=1em]
&t\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow]\\
t_1\arrow[d, Rightarrow]&&t_2\arrow[d, Rightarrow]\\
\vphantom{P}\cdots\arrow[rd, Rightarrow]&&\vphantom{P}\cdots\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\\
&\Lambda(t)
\end{tikzcd}&
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=1em]
&t\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow]\\
t_1\arrow[d, Rightarrow]&&t_2\arrow[d, Rightarrow]\\
\Lambda(t_1)\arrow[rd, Rightarrow, dashed]&&\vphantom{P}\cdots\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\\
&\Lambda(t_2)
\end{tikzcd}&
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=1em]
&t\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow]\\
t_1\arrow[d, Rightarrow]&&t_2\arrow[d, Rightarrow]\\
\vphantom{P}\cdots\arrow[rd, Rightarrow]&&\Lambda(t_2)\arrow[ld, Rightarrow, dashed]\\
&\Lambda(t_1)
\end{tikzcd}
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

In each case, we get two rewrite paths with the same source word~$t$, and the same destination word, say~$\Lambda(t)$. Let's refer to these paths as $p_1$ and $p_2$. If we compose the first path~$p_1$ with the \index{inverse rewrite path}inverse of the second path~$p_2$, we get a \IndexDefinition{rewrite loop}\emph{rewrite loop} $\ell$:
\[
\ell := p_1 \circ p_2^{-1}
\]
Note that $\ell$ starts and ends at the same word $t$, visiting $\Lambda(t)$ along the way:
\begin{gather*}
\Src(\ell)=\Src(p_1)=t \\
\Dst(\ell)=\Dst(p_2^{-1})=\Src(p_2)=t
\end{gather*}
We say that $t$ is the \IndexDefinition{basepoint}\emph{basepoint} of our rewrite loop $\ell$. A rewrite loop applies some sequence of rewrite rules to the basepoint, then rewrites it back to the basepoint, via a possibly \emph{different} mix of rewrite rules. In a sense, resolving a critical pair ``completes'' the critical pair to form a rewrite loop. The next algorithm is a variant of \AlgRef{add rewrite rule} which records this rewrite loop.

\begin{algorithm}[Record rule with loop]\label{add rule derived algo}
As input, takes a pair of words $(u, v)$ and a rewrite path $p$ with $\Src(p)=u$ and $\Dst(p)=v$. Records a rewrite loop, and possibly adds a new rule, returning true if a rule was actually recorded.
\begin{enumerate}
\item (Reduce) Use \AlgRef{alg:trie normal form} to compute $\Lambda(u)$ and a path $p_1$ from $u$ to $\Lambda(u)$.
\item (Reduce) Use \AlgRef{alg:trie normal form} to compute $\Lambda(v)$ and a path $p_2$ from $v$ to $\Lambda(v)$.
\item (Path) Let $p := p_1^{-1} \circ p \circ p_2$. Now, $\Src(p)=\Lambda(u)$ and $\Dst(p)=\Lambda(v)$.
\item \index{trivial critical pair}(Trivial) If $\Lambda(u)=\Lambda(v)$, then $p$ is already a loop with basepoint $\Lambda(u)$. Record this loop and return false.
\item (Compare) Otherwise, compare $\Lambda(u)$ and $\Lambda(v)$ with \AlgRef{rqm reduction order}.
\item (Error) If $\Lambda(u)$ and $\Lambda(v)$ are \index{incomparable elements}incomparable, signal an error.
\item (Orient) If $\Lambda(v)>\Lambda(u)$, swap $\Lambda(u)$ with $\Lambda(v)$, and set $p \leftarrow p^{-1}$.
\item (Record) Now, $\Lambda(u)>\Lambda(v)$. Add $(\Lambda(u),\Lambda(v))$ to the \index{rule array}rule array. Append this rule to $p$, to get a rewrite loop $\ell := p \circ (\Lambda(u) \Red \Lambda(v))$ with basepoint $\Lambda(u)$.
\item (Trie) Use \AlgRef{trie insert algo} to update the rule trie. Record $\ell$. Return true.
\end{enumerate}
\end{algorithm}

By collecting rewrite loops during completion, we can track the relationship between the original rules and any new rules we add. This is sometimes known as \IndexDefinition{proof logging}\emph{proof logging}, and is described in \cite{loggedrewriting} and \cite{homotopicalcompletion}. We use the set of rewrite loops to compute the \index{minimal requirement}minimal requirements for a generic signature in \ChapRef{chap:minimization}. Only local rules are subject to \index{requirement minimization}minimization, so we only record rewrite loops involving local rules when constructing a \index{minimization machine}minimization machine. \index{query machine}Query machines are not used for minimization, so we do not need to record rewrite loops when constructing query machines at all.

\begin{algorithm}[Resolve critical pair]\label{critical pair algo}
Takes two rules $(u, v)$ and $(u', v')$, together with an overlap position $i$ where $0\leq i<|u|$. Returns true if a new rule was recorded.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $i+|u'|\leq|u|$, we have \textbf{an overlap of the first kind}, so $u=xu'z$ for some words $x$ and $z$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Let $x:=u[:i]$ (the prefix of $u$ of length $i$), and $z:=u[i+|u'|:]$ (the suffix of $u$ of length $|u|-|u'|-i$).
\item Let $t_1:=v$, the result of rewriting $u$ with the first rule.
\item Let $t_2:=xv' z$, the result of rewriting $u$ with the second rule.
\item Let $p:=(v\Red u)\circ x(u'\Red v')z$. This is a rewrite path from $v$ to $xv'z$.
\end{enumerate}
\item Otherwise, we have \textbf{an overlap of the second kind}, so $u=xy$ and $u'=yz$ for some words $x$, $y$, and $z$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Let $x:=u[:i]$ (the prefix of $u$ of length $i$), $z:=u'[|u|-i:]$ (the suffix of $u'$ of length $|u'|-|u|+i$). (We don't actually need $y:=u[i:]=u'[:|u|-i]$.)
\item Let $t_1:=vz$, the result of rewriting $xyz$ with the first rule.
\item Let $t_2:=xv'$, the result of rewriting $xyz$ with the second rule.
\item Let $p:=(v\Red u)z\circ x(u'\Red v')$. This is a rewrite path from $vz$ to $xv'$.
\end{enumerate}
\item Invoke \AlgRef{add rule derived algo} with $t_1$, $t_2$, and $p$, and return its result.
\end{enumerate}
\end{algorithm}

Given a rule and a position in its left-hand side, we can find all rules that this rule overlaps with at this position by performing a lookup into the \index{rule trie}rule \index{trie}trie, which previously helped speed up the normal form algorithm in \SecRef{sec:rqm normal form}.

\begin{algorithm}[Overlap lookup in rule trie]\label{overlap trie lookup}
Takes a word $t$, a position $i$ such that $0\leq i<|t|$, and a callback. For each rule $(u, v)$ where $t[i:]$ is a prefix of $u$ or $u$ is a prefix of $t[i:]$, invokes the callback with the rule $(u, v)$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item (Initialize) Let $N$ be the root node of the trie.
\item (End) If $i=|t|$, we've reached the end of our word. Perform a pre-order traversal of all child nodes of $N$, and for those children that have an associated rewrite rule, invoke the callback with that rule (this is the case where $t[i:]$ is a prefix of each~$u$).
\item (Traverse) Let $s_i$ be the $i$th symbol of $t$. Look up $s_i$ in $N$. If there is no such child node, return.
\item (Child) Otherwise, call this child node~$M$. If $M$ points at a rule $(u, v)$, invoke the callback with this rule (in this case, $u$ is a prefix of $t[i:]$).
\item (Advance) Set $N \leftarrow M$. Set $i \leftarrow i+1$. Go back to Step~2.
\end{enumerate}
\end{algorithm}

Note that unlike \AlgRef{alg:trie normal form}, the above algorithm enumerates all matches, instead of stopping after the first one. While we implemented it as a higher-order function, another viable approach would be to model this lookup with an iterator.

\begin{example}
Consider a set of rewrite rules with left-hand sides $a$, $ab$, $bc$, $bd$, and $acd$. Their right-hand sides do not change anything below, so we omit them. The key fact is that the left-hand side $ab$ overlaps with all other left-hand sides except for $acd$. Here is the rule trie, with thick borders denoting nodes that point at rewrite rules:

\end{tabular}
&

When checking the left-hand side $ab$ for overlaps, we perform a lookup at each position:
\begin{itemize}
\item At position 0, we look up $ab$. Starting from the root, we process the rule for~$a$ first, followed by $ab$ (which means we've found our rule itself, so we skip the latter). The node for $ab$ is a leaf, so we move on to the next position.
\item At position 1, we look up $b$. The node for $b$ doesn't store a rewrite rule, however, it has child nodes. We've reached the end of our input word, so we recursively visit all children, and find the two final overlap candidates, $bc$ and $bd$.
\end{itemize}
\end{example}

\paragraph{Another optimization.}
We now know how to scan a given rule for overlaps with other rules, and how to resolve critical pairs for any overlaps we find in this manner. The next step is to enumerate all overlaps among all the left-hand sides of all rules. However, because of how our rules are constructed, we don't actually have to scan the left-hand sides of \emph{all} rules for overlaps. Recall the bottom-up construction of a requirement machine from protocol components, and the partition of rewrite rules into \index{imported rule}imported rules and \index{local rule}local rules, from \ChapRef{chap:basic operation}. There are four combinations to consider:
\begin{enumerate}
\item An imported rule overlaps with another imported rule.
\item An imported rule overlaps with a local rule.
\item A local rule overlaps with an imported rule.
\item A local rule overlaps with another local rule.
\end{enumerate}
As it turns out, it suffices to only consider cases 3 and 4, so we only need to scan the left-hand sides of local rules for overlaps. We will now sketch a proof of this claim.

For case 1, we claim that while an imported rule can overlap with another imported rule, all such critical pairs must be trivial. Recall the following:
\begin{itemize}
\item The discussion of \index{valid type parameter!in requirement machine}valid type parameters, from \SecRef{generic signature validity}.
\item The construction of rewrite rules in protocol machines, from \SecRef{sec:rqm terms}.
\end{itemize}
In a protocol machine, each side of each rule must start with a protocol symbol or associated type symbol, say $\pP$ or $\aPA$. Let's call this the \emph{root protocol} of the rule. The root protocol is always one of the protocols in the protocol component. Furthermore, if another protocol symbol or associated type symbol subsequently appears in the left-hand side, say $\pQ$ or $\aQB$, then  $\tQ$ must be a \index{protocol dependency}protocol dependency of $\tP$. For example, say we have a conformance rule, like this:
\[
\aPA \cdot \pQ \Red \aPA
\]
The above implies that we can derive the conformance requirement $\GP \vdash\ConfReq{Self.A}{Q}$. Similarly, say we have a same-type rule, like this:
\[
\aPA \cdot \aQB \Red \assocsym{P}{B}
\]
The term $\aPA \cdot \aQB$ corresponds to the \index{bound type parameter!in requirement machine}bound type parameter \texttt{Self.[P]A.[Q]B}. This must be a valid type parameter of the protocol generic signature $\GP$. Since it is a valid type parameter, \texttt{Self.[P]A} must conform to $\tQ$ in this generic signature. Once again, we must have $\GP \vdash\ConfReq{Self.A}{Q}$, and thus $\tQ$ is a protocol dependency of $\tP$.

Thus, suppose we're constructing a requirement machine, and we have two imported rules that overlap. By \DefRef{overlappingrules}, the initial symbol of the second rule's left-hand side must occur in the first rule's left-hand side. But this means that the first rule's root protocol must depend on the second rule's root protocol, as per the above discussion. We conclude that either both protocols are in the same component, or the first protocol component depends on the second. Either way, will have already considered this overlap when constructing the protocol machine for the second protocol component.

Next, to remove case 2 from consideration, we argue that an imported rule cannot ever overlap with a local rule. If the current requirement machine is a generic signature machine, both sides of our local rule would start with a \index{generic parameter symbol}generic parameter symbol. Since the left-hand side of an imported rule cannot contain a generic parameter symbol, no such overlap can occur. On the other hand, if the current requirement machine is a protocol machine, the root protocol of the local rule would be a dependency of the root protocol of the imported rule, by the same argument as in case 1. Once again, this is impossible by construction.

\paragraph{The main loop.}
The next algorithm scans the left-hand side of each local rule with \AlgRef{overlap trie lookup} and collects the results in a list. We check the \textbf{left-simplified}, \textbf{right-simplified}, and \textbf{substitution-simplified} flags that were introduced in \SecRef{sec:rqm rules}. The first two will be set by a pair of algorithms in \SecRef{sec:rule simplification}, and the third one is set by the algorithm in \SecRef{subst simplification}. In a conventional implementation of Knuth-Bendix, all rules are ``local rules,'' and the \textbf{substitution-simplified} is not present.

\begin{algorithm}[Find overlapping rules]\label{find overlapping rule algo}
Takes the list of local rules as input, and outputs a list of overlaps to check. 
\begin{enumerate}
\item (Initialize) Set $i \leftarrow 0$. Let $n$ be the total number of local rules. Initialize an empty list of triples.
\item (Outer check) If $i=n$, we're done. Return the output list.
\item (Get rule) Let $(u,v)$ be the $i$th local rule.
\item (Skip) If this rule is marked \index{left-simplified rule}\textbf{left-simplified}, \index{right-simplified rule}\textbf{right-simplified} or \index{substitution-simplified rule}\textbf{substitution-simplified}, skip it entirely and go to Step~9.
\item (Scan) Otherwise, set $j \leftarrow 0$.
\item (Inner check) If $j=|u|$, we're done. Go to Step~9.
\item (Find overlaps) Apply \AlgRef{overlap trie lookup} to check $u$ for overlaps at position $j$. For each rule $(u', v')$ that this yields:
\begin{enumerate}
\item (Skip) If the matching rule is marked \textbf{left-simplified}, \textbf{right-simplified} or \textbf{substitution-simplified}, skip this rule.
\item (Record) Otherwise, add $((u, v),\,(u', v'),\,j)$ to the output list.
\end{enumerate}
\item (Inner loop) Set $j\leftarrow j+1$, and go back to Step~6.
\item (Outer loop) Set $i\leftarrow i+1$, and go back to Step~2.
\end{enumerate}
\end{algorithm}

Since we may invoke the above algorithm multiple times, in the real implementation we also avoid repeated work by maintaining a set of all visited overlaps across invocations. This set can be checked and updated in Step~7d.

Finally, we get to the main loop of the Knuth-Bendix completion procedure. We repeatedly scan the left-hand sides of all local rules for overlaps, and resolve any critical pairs we discover. If no more non-trivial critical pairs remain, we have our hallowed finite complete rewriting system, and we're done.

However, it is possible that the ``repeatedly'' part might not terminate, if every round of critical pair resolution introduces new non-trivial critical pairs in a manner that does not converge. To prevent an infinite loop in this situation, we add a \index{termination check}termination check; if we think we've done too much work already, we give up on constructing a finite complete rewriting system and report failure.

\begin{algorithm}[Knuth-Bendix completion procedure]\label{alg:knuthbendix} Takes a mutable array of rules as input. Adds new rewrite rules, and returns success or failure. On success, the rewrite rules define a \index{finite complete presentation}finite complete rewriting system. On failure, we either encountered a non-orientable rewrite rule, or we hit the termination check.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Clear the flag.
\item Build a list of overlaps with \AlgRef{find overlapping rule algo}.
\item Left-simplify all rules with \AlgRef{alg:simplify left}.
\item For each overlap in the list produced by Step~1, resolve the critical pair with \AlgRef{critical pair algo}. If this algorithm reports that a rule was added, set the flag.
\item Right-simplify all rewrite rules with \AlgRef{alg:simplify right}.
\item Substitution-simplify all rewrite rules with \AlgRef{subst simplification algo}.
\item Check if the rule count, rule length, or concrete nesting limits have been exceeded; if so, report failure.
\item If the flag is set, go back to Step~1. Otherwise, report success.
\end{enumerate}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Termination.} The \index{termination check}termination check is controlled by a handful of \index{frontend flag}frontend flags:
\begin{itemize}
\item \IndexFlag{requirement-machine-max-rule-count} \texttt{-requirement-machine-max-rule-count=<value>} sets the maximum number of local rules to \texttt{value}. The default is a maximum of 4000 local rules. Imported rules do not count toward this total, so this is hard to hit with realistic code.

\item \IndexFlag{requirement-machine-max-rule-length} \texttt{-requirement-machine-max-rule-length=<value>} sets the maximum length of the terms in a rule to \texttt{value}. To compute the actual quantity, we add the length of the longest \emph{user-written} rule; so the restriction in on the relative ``growth'' and not on the length of a type parameter written by the user. The default value is 12.

\item \IndexFlag{requirement-machine-max-concrete-nesting} \texttt{-requirement-machine-max-concrete-nesting=<value>} sets the upper bound on nested concrete types to \texttt{value}, to prevent \index{substitution simplification}substitution simplification from constructing an infinite type like \texttt{G<G<G<...>>>}. As with the limit on rule length, we first add the maximum nesting depth of all user-written rules to get the actual limit. The default value is 30.
\end{itemize}

While the first of the three is sufficient to detect non-termination, it can take a while for completion to record that many rules. The purpose behind the other two limits is to improve user experience by rejecting ``clearly invalid'' problem instances more quickly. The rule length limit is relative to the initial length of the rules, and not a complete ban on rules of length 12 and above, to allow completion to succeed in certain pathological instances, as the next example demonstrates.

\begin{example}
The following protocol presents the monoid $\mathbb{Z}_{14}$. The initial set of rules already includes a rule of length 14, but as per the above, the absolute rule length limit is actually $14+12=26$, and completion finishes successfully:
\VerbInput{completion/Z14.swift}
\end{example}

If completion fails to build a rewriting system for a \index{requirement minimization}minimization machine, we have a source location associated with some protocol or generic declaration. We diagnose an error at this source location, and minimization outputs an empty list of requirements, allowing the remainder of compilation to proceed and possibly produce more errors. However, if completion fails with a query machine, we don't have a good source location for diagnostics, so the compiler dumps the entire rewriting system so far, and aborts with a fatal error. The latter scenario is unusual; if we successfully constructed a rewriting system from user-written requirements, we should be able construct a rewriting system from those requirements again when we build a query machine.

\paragraph{Debugging flags.}
A pair of debugging options can help us understand the operation of the completion procedure; both can be set together\footnote{With a single \texttt{-debug-requirement-machine=} flag, separating the subflags with commas.}, but be warned that they produce a large volume of output:
\begin{itemize}
\item \IndexTwoFlag{debug-requirement-machine}{completion} \texttt{-debug-requirement-machine=completion} will dump all overlapping rules and critical pairs.
\item \IndexTwoFlag{debug-requirement-machine}{add} \texttt{-debug-requirement-machine=add} will dump rewrite rules that are recorded as a result of resolving critical pairs.
\end{itemize}

\section{Rule Simplification}\label{sec:rule simplification}

We impose two further conditions on our \index{complete presentation}\index{finite complete presentation}finite complete rewriting system:
\begin{enumerate}
\item No rule has a left-hand side that can be reduced by any other rule.
\item No rule has a right-hand side that can be reduced by any other rule.
\end{enumerate}
Sometimes, such rewriting system are referred to as ``normalized,'' ``canonical,'' or ``inter-reduced'' in the literature. Any finite complete rewriting system can be transformed into one that satisfies these conditions by applying a pair of simplification passes, which we will now describe.

\paragraph{Left-hand side simplification.} If the left-hand side of a rewrite rule $(u, v)$ contains the left-hand side of another rule $(u', v')$, so that $u=xu'y$ for some $x$, $z \in A^*$, we have an \index{overlapping rules}overlap of the first kind in the sense of \DefRef{overlappingrules}. Once we resolve this critical pair, both ways of reducing the overlap word $u:=xu'z$ must ultimately yield the same result, say $t \in A^*$, and so we don't need the first rule $(u, v)$ at all:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
&xu'z
\arrow[ld, Rightarrow, "(u\Red v)"']
\arrow[rd, Rightarrow, "x(u'\Red v')z"]
\\
v\arrow[d, Rightarrow]&&xv'z\arrow[d, Rightarrow]\\
\ldots\arrow[rd, Rightarrow]&&\ldots\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\\
&t&
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\end{ceqn}

The \IndexDefinition{left-hand side simplification}left-hand side simplification algorithm looks at each rule, and obsoletes the rule if it's left-hand side contains the left-hand side of some other rule. Our implementation of \index{proof logging}proof logging uses the indices of rules as stable indices elsewhere, so we don't actually delete rules from the rule array. Instead, we set the \index{left-simplified rule}\textbf{left-simplified} flag, and only remove the rule from the \index{rule trie}\index{trie}rule trie. This algorithm only needs to consider local rules, because imported rules were already simplified when we constructed the rewriting system for their original protocol components.
\begin{algorithm}[Simplify left-hand sides]\label{alg:simplify left}
Takes the list of local rules as input. Has side effects.
\begin{enumerate}
\item (Initialize) Let $n$ be the total number of local rules, and set $i \leftarrow 0$.
\item (Outer check) If $i=n$, return. Otherwise, let $(u, v)$ be the $i$th local rule.
\item (Skip) If the rule was previously marked \textbf{left-simplified} or \textbf{right-simplified}, go to Step~9. Otherwise, set $j \leftarrow 0$.
\item (Inner check) If $j=|u|$, go to Step~9.
\item (Search) Look up $u[j:]$ in the \index{rule trie}rule \index{trie}trie using \AlgRef{trie lookup algo}, where $u[j:]$ is the suffix of $u$ with length $|u|-j$.
\item (Decide) If the trie lookup returns no results, or if it returns $(u, v)$ itself (which can only happen if $j=0$), go to Step~9.
\item (Mark) Otherwise, mark $(u, v)$ as \textbf{left-simplified} and go to Step~9.
\item (Inner loop) Set $j\leftarrow j+1$ and go back to Step~4.
\item (Outer loop) Set $i\leftarrow i+1$ and go back to Step~2.
\end{enumerate}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Right-hand side simplification.} \IndexDefinition{right-hand side simplification}We may also have a rule $(u, v)$ whose right-hand side $v$ can be reduced by some other rule, so $v\Red \Lambda(v)$ via some \index{positive rewrite path}positive rewrite path~$p$. In this case, we can replace $(u, v)$ with $(u, \Lambda(v))$, essentially as a ``shortcut.'' As with the previous algorithm, we only need to consider \index{local rule}local rules, because \index{imported rule}imported rules have already been simplified in this manner. Once again, we cannot actually delete or modify existing rules, so instead we mark the rule as \index{right-simplified rule}\textbf{right-simplified}, remove it from the trie, add a new rule, and finally, record a \index{rewrite loop}rewrite loop relating the two.

\begin{algorithm}[Simplify right-hand sides]\label{alg:simplify right}
Takes the list of local rules as input. Has side effects.
\begin{enumerate}
\item (Initialize) Let $n$ be the total number of local rules, and set $i:=0$.
\item (Check) If $i=n$, return. Otherwise, let $(u, v)$ be the $i$th local rule.
\item (Skip) If this rule was previously marked \textbf{left-simplified} or \textbf{right-simplified}, go to Step~7.
\item (Reduce) Otherwise, apply \AlgRef{alg:trie normal form} to $v$ to get a word $\Lambda(v)$ and a rewrite path~$p$ with $\Src(p)=v$ and $\Dst(p)=\Lambda(v)$. If $v=\Lambda(v)$, go to Step~7.
\item (Mark) Otherwise, mark $(u, v)$ as \textbf{right-simplified} and remove it from the trie.
\item (Record) Invoke \AlgRef{add rule derived algo} to add a new rewrite rule $(u, \Lambda(v))$ with rewrite path $(u \Red v) \circ p$.
\item (Loop) Set $i \leftarrow i + 1$, and go back to Step~2.
\end{enumerate}
\end{algorithm}

Recall that \AlgRef{alg:knuthbendix} repeatedly runs both passes during completion, once per round. This is advantageous for performance. To ensure correctness, we must perform left simplification after we collect overlaps, but \emph{before} we resolve the corresponding critical pairs. This ensures that for any rule we delete, the corresponding overlap of the first kind is still considered. Right simplification is fine to run at any time, so we run it after \index{resolve critical pair}resolving critical pairs. Recall that the Requirement Machine also performs a third \emph{substitution simplification} pass. Substitution simplification reduces the terms that appear in superclass, concrete type, and concrete conformance symbols. We will discuss it in \ChapRef{chap:property map}.

\section{Associated Types}\label{sec:associated types}

Recall the discussion of bound and unbound type parameters from \SecRef{bound type params}. A conformance rule $\ProtoConf{t}{P}$ always overlaps with an associated type rule $\AssocIntro{P}{A}$, and resolving this critical pair defines a rule $t\cdot\nA \Red t\cdot\aPA$ (unless the right-hand side reduces further). We will see that these rewrite rules reduce terms that represent \index{unbound type parameter!in requirement machine}unbound type parameters, to terms that represent \index{bound type parameter!in requirement machine}bound type parameters.

\begin{example}\label{assoc type completion example}
We're going to look at this pair of protocol declarations, and the \index{protocol generic signature}protocol generic signature $\GP$:
\VerbInput{completion/assoc1.swift}

Protocol \tP\ has an \index{associated conformance requirement}associated conformance requirement $\AssocConfReq{Self.[P]A}{Q}{P}$, and $\GP$ has a conformance requirement $\ConfReq{\rT}{P}$, so the \index{protocol dependency graph}protocol dependency graph has the two edges $\GP\prec\tP$ and $\tP\prec\tQ$. We first build a rewriting system for \tQ, then a rewriting system for \tP, which imports the rules for \tQ. Finally, we build the rewriting system for $\GP$, which imports rules from \tP\ and \tQ.

At the end of this process, we get the following requirement machine for~$\GP$:
\begin{gather*}
\AssocIntro{Q}{B}\tag{1}\\
\midrule
\AssocIntro{P}{A}\tag{2}\\
\ProtoConf{\aPA}{Q}\tag{3}\\
\aPA\cdot\nB\Red\aPA\cdot\aQB\tag{$\CRule{4}$}\\
\midrule
\ProtoConf{\rT}{P}\tag{5}\\
\rT\cdot\nA\Red\rT\cdot\aPA\tag{$\CRule{6}$}
\end{gather*}
We can categorize the above rules as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item Associated type rules: 1 and 2, added by \AlgRef{rules for protocol algo}.
\item Conformance requirements: 3 and 5, added by \AlgRef{build rule protocol}.
\item Rules added by completion, marked with a diamond: $\CRule{4}$ and $\CRule{6}$.
\end{itemize}
Let's go through the construction of each requirement machine step by step. The protocol machine for \tQ\ is just rule 1. The protocol machine for \tP\ imports rule 1 from \tQ, and adds rules 2 and 3 as local rules. Completion scans the left-hand sides of rules 2 and 3 for overlaps. Rule 2 does not overlap with any other rules. Rule 3 overlaps with rule 1 on the term $\aPA\cdot\pQ\cdot\nB$:
\begin{align*}
\aPA\cdot{}&\pQ\\
&\pQ\cdot\nB
\end{align*}
The two sides of the critical pair reduce to $\aPA\cdot\nB$ and $\aPA\cdot\aQB$, respectively. Resolving this critical pair introduces rule $\CRule{4}$. Note the direction of the rule; associated type symbols precede name symbols in our reduction order (\AlgRef{symbol reduction order}):
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=-1em]
& \aPA \cdot \pQ \cdot \nB
\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]
\arrow[rd, Rightarrow] \\
\aPA \cdot \nB\arrow[rr, Rightarrow, dashed] && \aPA \cdot \aQB
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\end{ceqn}
We enter the second round, and scan the left-hand sides of 2, 3, and $\CRule{4}$ for overlaps. This yields nothing new, so we have a finite complete rewriting system for~\tP.

Finally, we build the requirement machine for $\GP$. We import all rules from \tQ\ and \tP, and add rule 5, which corresponds to the conformance requirement $\ConfReq{\rT}{P}$. We scan the left-hand side of 5 for overlaps. Rule 5 overlaps with rule 2 on the term
$\rT\cdot\pP\cdot\nA$:
\begin{align*}
\rT\cdot{}&\pP\\
&\pP\cdot\nA
\end{align*}
Resolving this critical pair introduces rule $\CRule{6}$:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=-1em]
& \rT \cdot \pP \cdot \nA
\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]
\arrow[rd, Rightarrow] \\
\rT \cdot \nA\arrow[rr, Rightarrow, dashed] && \rT \cdot \aPA
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\end{ceqn}
We scan the left-hand sides of rules 5 and $\CRule{6}$ again. There are no new overlaps there, so we're done.

Now, we get to the interesting part. Consider the unbound type parameter \texttt{\rT.A.B}, and the bound type parameter \texttt{\rT.[P]A.[Q]B}. These two type parameters are equivalent. The two corresponding terms are $\rT\cdot\nA\cdot\nB$, and $\rT\cdot\aPA\cdot\aQB$, and they must be equivalent in our rewriting system as well. If we apply the normal form algorithm to each term, we see that the second term is already reduced, since it does not contain the left-hand side of any rule. The first term then reduces to the second by a \index{positive rewrite path}positive rewrite path involving rules $\CRule{4}$ and $\CRule{6}$ that were added by completion above:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=huge]
\rT\cdot\nA\cdot\nB\arrow[r, Rightarrow, "(\CRule{4}) \WR \nB"]&
\rT\cdot\aPA\cdot\nB\arrow[r, Rightarrow, "\rT \WL (\CRule{6})"]&
\rT\cdot\aPA\cdot\aQB
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\end{ceqn}
Note the ``telescoping'' effect, as the rewrite path transforms the name symbols from left to right, replacing them with associated type symbols.

Now, let's see what happens if we start with an \emph{invalid} type parameter, such as \texttt{\rT.A.A}. The corresponding term is $\rT \cdot \nA \cdot \nA$. The normal form algorithm applies rule $\CRule{4}$ to yield the term $\rT\cdot\aPA\cdot\nA$, but no further reductions can be performed:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=huge]
\rT\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\arrow[r, Rightarrow, "(\CRule{4}) \WR \nA"]&
\rT\cdot\aPA\cdot\nA
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\end{ceqn}
The final name symbol \nA\ remains ``unresolved'' because the type parameter \texttt{\rT.[P]A} does not conform to any protocols that state an associated type named \nA.
\end{example}

We now know enough to reveal the implementation of the \IndexQuery{isValidTypeParameter}$\Query{isValidTypeParameter}{}$ generic signature query, which operates on both bound and unbound type parameters:

\begin{algorithm}[$\Query{isValidTypeParameter}{}$ generic signature query]\label{alg:isValidTypeParameter}
Takes a generic signature $G$ and a type parameter \tT\ as input. Outputs true if $G\vdash\tT$, false otherwise.
\begin{enumerate}
\item (Bound) Apply \AlgRef{build term generic} to \tT\ to construct the term $t = \Term(\tT)$.
\item (Root) Check if $t$ starts with a \index{generic parameter symbol}generic parameter listed in $G$. If not, return false.
\item (Erase) Build a term $t'$ by replacing every associated type symbol $\aPA$ in $t$ with the corresponding name symbol $\nA$.
\item (Reduce) Use \AlgRef{alg:trie normal form} to compute $\Lambda(t)$ and $\Lambda(t')$.
\item (Scan) If any \index{name symbol}name symbols remain in $\Lambda(t')$, return false.
\item (Check) If $\Lambda(t)$ and $\Lambda(t')$ are identical, return true. Otherwise, return false.
\end{enumerate}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{example}\label{overlap of first kind example}
Let's build on our previous example and look at how unbound type parameters reduce when they appear in requirements. We add a new protocol \tR, and declare a \index{constrained extension}constrained \index{protocol extension}protocol extension of \tP\ where \texttt{Self.A.B} conforms to \tR:
\VerbInput{completion/assoc2.swift}
To build a rewriting system for the generic signature of the above \index{protocol extension}protocol extension, we take the 6 rules from the previous example, and add rule 7 corresponding to the user-written requirement $\ConfReq{\rT.A.B}{R}$:
\begin{gather*}
\ProtoConf{\rT\cdot\nA\cdot\nB}{R}\tag{7}
\end{gather*}
The left-hand side of rule 7 overlaps with the left-hand side of rule 6 on the term $\rT\cdot\nA\cdot\nB\cdot\pR$:
\begin{align*}
&\rT\cdot\nA\cdot\nB\cdot\pR\\
&\rT\cdot\nA
\end{align*}
Reducing the overlap term with each rule proceeds as follows:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=-1em]
& \rT \cdot \nA \cdot \nB \cdot \pR\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow] \\
\rT \cdot \nA \cdot \nB\arrow[d, Rightarrow] && \rT \cdot \aPA \cdot \nB \cdot \pR\arrow[dd, Rightarrow] \\
\rT \cdot \aPA \cdot \nB\arrow[d, Rightarrow] \\
\rT \cdot \aPA \cdot \aQB && \rT \cdot \aPA \cdot \aQB \cdot \pR\arrow[ll, dashed, Rightarrow]
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\end{ceqn}
The first term reduces to $\rT \cdot \aPA \cdot \aQB$ via rules $\CRule{4}$ and $\CRule{6}$ as in the previous example. The second term reduces to $\rT \cdot \aPA \cdot \aQB \cdot \pR$ by rule $\CRule{6}$. Neither side can be reduced further at this point, so we have a non-trivial critical pair. After orienting the two sides, we add the new rule $\CRule{8}$:
\begin{gather*}
\ProtoConf{\rT\cdot\aPA\cdot\aQB}{R}\tag{$\CRule{8}$}
\end{gather*}
Since this is an overlap of the first kind, we mark rule 7 as \index{left-simplified rule}\textbf{left-simplified} when we run \AlgRef{alg:simplify left}, so rule $\CRule{8}$ completely supersedes rule 7. Rule $\CRule{8}$ survives minimization and is then translated to the requirement $\ConfReq{\rT.[P]A.[Q]B}{R}$, which appears in the protocol extension's generic signature:
\begin{quote}
\begin{verbatim}
<τ_0_0 where τ_0_0: P, τ_0_0.[P]A.[Q]B: R>
\end{verbatim}
\end{quote}

Now, let's say we emit a \index{serialized module}serialized module. We serialize the \index{protocol extension}protocol extension's generic signature, so we do not need to re-construct it again if this module is imported by another module in a subsequent compiler invocation. If we construct a query machine for this generic signature, we build a ``second-generation'' rewriting system from the generic signature's minimal requirements. What was formerly rule 7 above is not present in this rewriting system, and what was rule $\CRule{8}$ is now one of our initial rules, produced as a result of lowering the minimal requirement $\ConfReq{\rT.[P]A.[Q]B}{R}$, whose subject type is a bound type parameter.

That is, if we ignore \textbf{left-simplified} and \textbf{right-simplified} rules, the rewriting system we obtain from user-written requirements is identical, up to a permutation of the rules, to one we get from minimal requirements.
\end{example}

Rules 1 and 2 are the associated type rules for the associated types named \nA\ in each protocol, and rules 3 and 4 correspond to the conformance requirements $\ConfReq{\rT}{P1}$ and $\ConfReq{\rT}{P2}$ of our generic signature.

Completion then adds two more rules. Rule $\CRule{5}$ is added when resolving the overlap between rule 3 and rule 1, exactly as in \ExRef{assoc type completion example}. This rule encodes the fact that the unbound type parameter \texttt{\rT.A} is equivalent to \texttt{\rT.[P1]A}.

Rule $\CRule{6}$ takes on a new form we haven't seen before. It arises in the following way. Rule~4 overlaps with rule~2 on the term $\rT\cdot\protosym{P2}\cdot\nA$:
\begin{align*}
\rT.&\protosym{P2}\\
&\protosym{P2}\cdot\nA
\end{align*}
The first term in the critical pair reduces to $\rT\cdot\assocsym{P1}{A}$ via rule 4 and rule $\CRule{5}$, and the other reduces to $\rT\cdot\assocsym{P2}{A}$ via rule 2:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=-1em]
& \rT \cdot \protosym{P2} \cdot \nA
\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow] \\
\rT \cdot \nA\arrow[rd, Rightarrow] && \rT \cdot \assocsym{P2}{A}\arrow[ld, dashed, Rightarrow] \\
& \rT \cdot \assocsym{P1}{A}
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\end{ceqn}
Rule $\CRule{6}$ encodes the equivalence between the bound type parameters \texttt{\rT.[P1]A} and \texttt{\rT.[P2]A}. Here are the three type parameters in this equivalence class, and the corresponding terms, which all reduce to the term $\rT \cdot \assocsym{P1}{A}$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\textbf{Type:}&\textbf{Term:}\\
\toprule
\texttt{\rT.[P1]A}&$\rT\cdot\assocsym{P1}{A}$\\
\texttt{\rT.[P2]A}&$\rT\cdot\assocsym{P2}{A}$\\
\texttt{\rT.A}&$\rT\cdot\nA$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{example}

\section{More Critical Pairs}\label{sec:more critical pairs}

We saw how associated type symbols relate bound and unbound type parameters in a few simple examples. The next two examples will show how \index{protocol inheritance!in requirement machine}protocol inheritance interacts with completion. The first example does not involve associated types at all.

\begin{example}\label{ex:protocol inheritance completion}
Here is a simple protocol inheritance hierarchy:
\VerbInput{completion/inheritance1.swift}
Here is the requirement machine for the protocol generic signature $G_{\texttt{Bot}}$:
\begin{gather*}
\ProtoInherit{Mid}{Top}\tag{1}\\
\midrule
\ProtoInherit{Bot}{Mid}\tag{2}\\
\ProtoInherit{Bot}{Top}\tag{$\CRule{3}$}\\
\midrule
\ProtoConf{\rT}{Bot}\tag{4}\\
\ProtoConf{\rT}{Mid}\tag{$\CRule{5}$}\\
\ProtoConf{\rT}{Top}\tag{$\CRule{6}$}
\end{gather*}

Let's go through its construction step by step. First up, \texttt{Top} is trivial, so the protocol machine for \texttt{Top} does not have any rules in it at all. The protocol machine for \texttt{Mid} has one rule, corresponding to the associated requirement $\AssocConfReq{Self}{Top}{Bot}$. This rule's left-hand side does not overlap with itself, so the rewriting system for \texttt{Mid} is already complete.

The protocol machine for \texttt{Bot} is more interesting. We import rule 1 from \texttt{Mid}, and add rule~2, corresponding to the associated requirement $\AssocConfReq{Self}{Mid}{Bot}$. Rule~2 overlaps with rule~1 on the term $\protosym{Bot} \cdot \protosym{Mid} \cdot \protosym{Top}$:
\begin{align*}
\protosym{Bot}\cdot{}&\protosym{Mid}\\
&\protosym{Mid}\cdot\protosym{Top}
\end{align*}
Resolving this critical pair gives us rule $\CRule{3}$, representing the fact that \texttt{Bot} indirectly inherits from \texttt{Top}:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=-3em]
& \protosym{Bot} \cdot \protosym{Mid} \cdot \protosym{Top}
\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow] \\
\protosym{Bot} \cdot \protosym{Top} \arrow[rd, Rightarrow, dashed] && \protosym{Bot} \cdot \protosym{Mid} \arrow[ld, Rightarrow] \\
& \protosym{Bot}
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\end{ceqn}

In general, after completion, rewrite rules of the form $\pP\cdot\pQ\Red\pP$ encode the \index{transitive closure}transitive closure of the \index{protocol inheritance!in requirement machine}protocol inheritance relation.

Finally, we get to the requirement machine for the generic signature $G_\texttt{Bot}$. It consists of a single conformance requirement $\ConfReq{\rT}{Bot}$, which gives us rule~4. This rule's left-hand side then overlaps with the left-hand side of rules~2 and $\CRule{3}$. Resolving these critical pairs yields rules~$\CRule{5}$ and $\CRule{6}$:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=-3em]
& \rT \cdot \protosym{Bot} \cdot \protosym{Mid}
\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow] \\
\rT \cdot \protosym{Mid} \arrow[rd, Rightarrow, dashed] && \rT \cdot \protosym{Bot} \arrow[ld, Rightarrow] \\
& \rT
\end{tikzcd}
\qquad
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=-3em]
& \rT \cdot \protosym{Bot} \cdot \protosym{Top}
\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow] \\
\rT \cdot \protosym{Top} \arrow[rd, Rightarrow, dashed] && \rT \cdot \protosym{Bot} \arrow[ld, Rightarrow] \\
& \rT
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\end{ceqn}
Observe that the three conformance rules 4, $\CRule{5}$, and $\CRule{6}$ correspond to the fact that \rT\ conforms to \texttt{Bot}, \texttt{Mid}, and \texttt{Top}. The first is via an explicit requirement, and the second two via derived requirements.
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{ex:associated type inheritance completion}
Next, we will look at \index{inherited associated type}inheritance from a protocol with an associated type. In the below snippet, the \texttt{Top} protocol declares an associated type \nA, and \texttt{Bot} inherits directly from \texttt{Top}:
\VerbInput{completion/inheritance2.swift}
We're going to examine the requirement machine for the generic signature $G_\texttt{Bot}$, which is built up from rules imported from \texttt{Mid} and \texttt{Bot}, followed by the generic signature's local rules:
\begin{gather*}
\AssocIntro{Top}{A}\tag{1}\\
\midrule
\AssocIntro{Bot}{A}\tag{2}\\
\ProtoInherit{Bot}{Top}\tag{3}\\
\protosym{Bot}\cdot\assocsym{Top}{A}\Red\assocsym{Bot}{A}\tag{$\CRule{4}$}\\
\midrule
\ProtoConf{\rT}{Bot}\tag{5}\\
\ProtoConf{\rT}{Top}\tag{$\CRule{6}$}\\
\rT\cdot\nA \Red \rT\cdot\assocsym{Bot}{A}\tag{$\CRule{7}$}\\
\rT\cdot\assocsym{Top}{A} \Red \rT\cdot\assocsym{Bot}{A}\tag{$\CRule{8}$}
\end{gather*}
The structure of some of the rules above should be familiar by now. We're going to focus on the rules that relate to our inherited associated type---rules 2, $\CRule{4}$, $\CRule{7}$, and $\CRule{8}$.

Recall that \AlgRef{rules for protocol algo} introduces associated type rules for each inherited associated type as well as those directly stated in the protocol. This explains rule~2.

Rule~3 overlaps with rule~1 on the term $\protosym{Top} \cdot \protosym{Bot} \cdot \nA$. When we resolve this critical pair, we get the funny-looking rule $\CRule{4}$:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=-2em]
& \protosym{Top} \cdot \protosym{Bot} \cdot \nA
\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow] \\
\protosym{Top} \cdot \nA \arrow[rd, Rightarrow] && \protosym{Top} \cdot \assocsym{Bot}{A} \arrow[ld, Rightarrow, dashed] \\
& \assocsym{Top}{A}
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\end{ceqn}

In the requirement machine for the generic signature itself, rule~5 overlaps with rule~2 on the term $\rT \cdot \protosym{Bot} \cdot \nA$. Resolving this critical pair yields rule $\CRule{7}$:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=-2em]
& \rT \cdot \protosym{Bot} \cdot \nA
\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow] \\
\rT \cdot \nA \arrow[rr, Rightarrow, dashed] && \rT \cdot \assocsym{Bot}{A}
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\end{ceqn}
Rule~5 also overlaps with~$\CRule{4}$, on the term $\rT \cdot \protosym{Bot} \cdot \assocsym{Top}{A}$. Resolving this critical pair yields rule $\CRule{8}$. Notice the orientation of the rule. Recall that the \index{reduction order!in requirement machine}reduction order on \index{associated type symbol}associated type symbols (\AlgRef{associated type reduction order}) prefers inherited associated types, which is why we have $\assocsym{Bot}{A} < \assocsym{Top}{A}$:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=-2.5em]
& \rT \cdot \protosym{Bot} \cdot \assocsym{Top}{A}
\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow] \\
\rT \cdot \assocsym{Top}{A} \arrow[rr, Rightarrow, dashed] && \rT \cdot \assocsym{Bot}{A}
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\end{ceqn}

Finally, rule~$\CRule{6}$ overlaps with rule~1 on the term $\rT \cdot \protosym{Top} \cdot \nA$. This critical pair is now trivial, thanks to the rules we derived above:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=-2em]
& \rT \cdot \protosym{Top} \cdot \nA
\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow] \\
\rT \cdot \nA \arrow[rd, Rightarrow] && \rT \cdot \assocsym{Top}{A}\arrow[ld, Rightarrow] \\
& \rT \cdot \assocsym{Bot}{A}
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\end{ceqn}

In this example, the relationship between \index{type parameter}type parameters and \index{term!in requirement machine}terms is more subtle than we've previously seen. In the derived requirements theory, the \index{equivalence class!of terms}equivalence class of \texttt{\rT.A} contains only one other type parameter, \texttt{\rT.[Top]A}, which is the \index{reduced type parameter!versus reduced term}reduced type parameter in this equivalence class. On the other hand, in the rewriting system, we have \emph{three} equivalent terms: $\rT\cdot\assocsym{Top}{A}$, $\rT\cdot\assocsym{Bot}{A}$, and $\rT\cdot\nA$; we have two distinct \index{associated type symbol}associated type symbols, $\assocsym{Top}{A}$ and $\assocsym{Bot}{A}$, which both correspond to the same associated type \emph{declaration} in the source language. Applying the normal form algorithm to either of these three terms outputs the reduced term $\rT \cdot \assocsym{Bot}{A}$.

Observe that applying \AlgRef{build term generic} to the \index{reduced type parameter}reduced type parameter \texttt{\rT.[Top]A} outputs $\rT\cdot\assocsym{Top}{A}$, so the image of a reduced type parameter under the $\Term$ mapping is not necessarily itself a reduced term:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\textbf{Type:}&\textbf{Term:}\\
\toprule
\texttt{\rT.[Top]A} (reduced)&$\rT\cdot\assocsym{Top}{A}$\\
&$\rT\cdot\assocsym{Bot}{A}$ (reduced)\\
\texttt{\rT.A}&$\rT\cdot\nA$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
For this reason, the implementation of the \IndexQuery{getReducedType}$\Query{getReducedType}{}$ generic signature query has to do more work than simply translating associated type symbols back into bound type parameters. The exact procedure will be described in \SecRef{implqueries}.

Finally, let's change the declaration of \texttt{Bot} to \index{restated associated type}\emph{restate} the associated type:
\begin{Verbatim}
protocol Bot: Top {
  associatedtype A
}
\end{Verbatim}
We get the same rewriting system in this case, while the equivalence class of \texttt{\rT.A} now contains three type parameters:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\textbf{Type:}&\textbf{Term:}\\
\toprule
\texttt{\rT.[Top]A} (reduced)&$\rT\cdot\assocsym{Top}{A}$\\
\texttt{\rT.[Bot]A}&$\rT\cdot\assocsym{Bot}{A}$ (reduced)\\
\texttt{\rT.A}&$\rT\cdot\nA$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
In particular, the reduced type parameter is still \texttt{\rT.[Top]A}, because in the \index{type parameter order}type parameter order, a \index{root associated type}root associated type declaration always precedes a restated associated type declaration (\AlgRef{associated type order}).

The end result is that restating an associated type (or removing a restated associated type) does not change either the rewriting system or reduced type relation, and it has no effect on calling convention, witness table layout, or any other aspect of the Swift \index{ABI}ABI. This is important, since associated type declarations sometimes need to be restated, as we previously saw in \ExRef{ex:restate assoc type default} and \ExRef{ex:restate assoc type infer}. (Also, recall \ExRef{rqm assoc bind example}.)

The reason why the reduction order has to differ from the type parameter order when inherited associated types are involved will become clear in \ExRef{ex:protocol o fcrs}.
\end{example}

All of our examples so far had a \index{theory!finite}finite set of type parameters, which we recall is the simplest class of generic signatures, from \SecRef{sec:archetype builder}. Our next example illustrates a generic signature with an \index{theory!infinite}infinite set of type parameters, but a finite set of equivalence classes, which is the next level of complexity.

\begin{example}\label{proto assoc rule}
We now ask if \texttt{f()} type checks. That is, is \texttt{T.C.B} equivalent to \texttt{T.A} in the generic signature of \texttt{f()}?
\VerbInput{completion/twist.swift}
We're going to be working with the generic signature of \texttt{f()}, which is identical to the protocol generic signature $G_{\texttt{S}}$. This generic signature is somewhat non-trivial, so let's take a closer look before we jump into the rewriting system.

The protocol \texttt{S} states three associated requirements:
\begin{gather*}
\AssocConfReq{Self.C}{S}{S}\\
\AssocSameReq{Self}{Self.C.C}{S}\\
\AssocSameReq{Self.B}{Self.C.A}{S}
\end{gather*}
The third requirement immediately gives us $G \vdash \SameReq{\rT.B}{\rT.C.A}$. What is less apparent is that also, $G \vdash \SameReq{\rT.C.B}{\rT.A}$:
\begin{gather*}
\ConfStep{\rT}{S}{1}\\
\AssocConfStep{1}{\rT.C}{S}{2}\\
\AssocSameStep{2}{\rT.C.B}{\rT.C.C.A}{3}\\
\AssocSameStep{1}{\rT}{\rT.C.C}{4}\\
\SymStep{4}{\rT.C.C}{\rT}{5}\\
\SameNameStep{1}{5}{\rT.C.C.A}{\rT.A}{6}\\
\TransStep{3}{6}{\rT.C.B}{\rT.A}{7}
\end{gather*}
This implies that each of the infinitely many valid type parameters of $G_{\texttt{S}}$ is equivalent to exactly one of \rT, \texttt{\rT.A}, \texttt{\rT.B}, or \texttt{\rT.C}:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|l|l|l}
\toprule
\texttt{\rT}&\texttt{\rT.A}&\texttt{\rT.B}&\texttt{\rT.C}\\
\texttt{\rT.C.C}&\texttt{\rT.C.B}&\texttt{\rT.C.A}&\texttt{\rT.C.C.C}\\
\texttt{\rT.C.C.C.C}&\texttt{\rT.C.C.A}&\texttt{\rT.C.C.B}&\texttt{\rT.C.C.C.C.C}\\
\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
For a visual perspective, here are the \index{type parameter graph}type parameter and \index{conformance graph}conformance graphs of $G_\texttt{S}$:

\end{center}
Finally, here are two possible inter-dependent conforming types:

\medskip

\noindent
\begin{minipage}{18.4em}
\begin{Verbatim}

  typealias A = Int
  typealias B = String
  typealias C = Y
}
\end{Verbatim}
\end{minipage}
\quad
\begin{minipage}{18.4em}
\begin{Verbatim}
struct Y: S {
  typealias A = String
  typealias B = Int
  typealias C = X
}
\end{Verbatim}
\end{minipage}

\medskip

Now, let's open up the requirement machine for $G_{\texttt{S}}$. We start with three associated type rules, and three rules for each associated requirement. To avoid various derivations that would be the same as in previous examples, this time we will start with the bound type parameter form of each associated requirement, which would be the case if we constructed this protocol machine from a serialized requirement signature:
\begin{gather*}
\AssocIntro{S}{A}\tag{1}\\
\AssocIntro{S}{B}\tag{2}\\
\AssocIntro{S}{C}\tag{3}\\
\ProtoConf{\assocsym{S}{C}}{S}\tag{4}\\
\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\assocsym{S}{C}\Red\protosym{S}\tag{5}\\
\assocsym{S}{C}\cdot\assocsym{S}{A}\Red\assocsym{S}{B}\tag{6}
\end{gather*}
(To help clarify things, $\AssocConfReq{Self.[S]C}{S}{S}$ is rule~4, $\AssocSameReq{Self}{Self.[S]C.[S]C}{S}$ is rule~5, and $\AssocSameReq{Self.[S]B}{Self.[S]C.[S]A}{S}$ is rule~6.)

Completion then adds \emph{eight} new rules; we will study them a few at a time.

The first set of critical pairs involve the conformance rule~4, which overlaps with rules 1, 2, and 3. Here is each critical pair:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=-2em]
& \assocsym{S}{C} \cdot \protosym{S} \cdot \nA
\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow] \\
\assocsym{S}{C} \cdot \nA \arrow[rr, Rightarrow, dashed] && \assocsym{S}{C} \cdot \assocsym{S}{A}
\end{tikzcd}
\quad
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=-2em]
& \assocsym{S}{C} \cdot \protosym{S} \cdot \nB
\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow] \\
\assocsym{S}{C} \cdot \nB \arrow[rd, Rightarrow, dashed] && \assocsym{S}{C} \cdot \assocsym{S}{B} \arrow[ld, Rightarrow] \\
& \assocsym{S}{A}
\end{tikzcd}
\quad
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=-2em]
& \assocsym{S}{C} \cdot \protosym{S} \cdot \nC
\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow] \\
\assocsym{S}{C} \cdot \nC \arrow[rd, Rightarrow, dashed] && \assocsym{S}{C} \cdot \assocsym{S}{C} \arrow[ld, Rightarrow] \\
& \protosym{S}
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\end{ceqn}
So far, we get three new rules:
\begin{gather*}
\assocsym{S}{C} \cdot \nA \Red \assocsym{S}{C} \cdot \assocsym{S}{A} \tag{$\CRule{7}$} \\
\assocsym{S}{C} \cdot \nB \Red \assocsym{S}{A} \tag{$\CRule{8}$} \\
\assocsym{S}{C} \cdot \nC \Red \protosym{S} \tag{$\CRule{9}$}
\end{gather*}
Next, Rule~5 overlaps with rule 4:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=-2em]
& \assocsym{S}{C} \cdot \assocsym{S}{C} \cdot \protosym{S}
\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow] \\
\protosym{S} \cdot \protosym{S} \arrow[rd, Rightarrow, dashed] && \assocsym{S}{C} \cdot \assocsym{S}{C} \arrow[ld, Rightarrow] \\
& \protosym{S}
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\end{ceqn}
Resolving the above critical pair generates a rule which is key to what comes next:
\begin{gather*}
\ProtoConf{\protosym{S}}{S}\tag{$\CRule{10}$}
\end{gather*}
From looking at our previous examples, one might think this indicates that protocol~\texttt{S} inherits from itself, which would be an error. However, this is not the case. Instead, this rule indicates that \index{conformance path}\textsl{there are two distinct conformance paths} witnessing the $\ConfReq{\rT}{S}$ conformance in the protocol generic signature $G_{\texttt{S}}$. The first conformance path is the ``obvious'' one consisting of a single step:
\[
\ConfReq{\rT}{S}
\]
The second conformance path gives us another way to derive the same conformance via a more circuitous route, by applying two associated conformance projections to $\ConfReq{\rT}{S}$, which we know must give us the same conformance, due to the same-type requirement $\AssocSameReq{Self}{Self.C.C}{S}$:
\[
\AssocConf{Self.C}{S} \otimes \AssocConf{Self.C}{S} \otimes \ConfReq{\rT}{S}
\]

Rule $\CRule{10}$ is called an \IndexDefinition{identity conformance rule}\emph{identity conformance rule}. Unlike other conformance rules, the identity conformance rule does not appear in the protocol's \index{requirement signature!identity conformance rule}requirement signature. To avoid redundant work in the requirement minimization algorithm, we always mark the identity conformance rule as \index{permanent rule}\textbf{permanent}, if we happen to discover it during completion.

Returning to our example, we have to consider some critical pairs involving the identity conformance rule $\CRule{10}$, and the associated type introduction rules 1, 2, and 3. They resolve as follows:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=-1em]
& \protosym{S} \cdot \protosym{S} \cdot \nA
\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow] \\
\protosym{S} \cdot \nA && \protosym{S} \cdot \assocsym{S}{A} \arrow[ll, Rightarrow, dashed]
\end{tikzcd}
\qquad
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=-1em]
& \protosym{S} \cdot \protosym{S} \cdot \nB
\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow] \\
\protosym{S} \cdot \nB && \protosym{S} \cdot \assocsym{S}{B} \arrow[ll, Rightarrow, dashed]
\end{tikzcd}
\qquad
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=-1em]
& \protosym{S} \cdot \protosym{S} \cdot \nC
\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow] \\
\protosym{S} \cdot \nC && \protosym{S} \cdot \assocsym{S}{C} \arrow[ll, Rightarrow, dashed]
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\end{ceqn}
We get three more rules from the above:
\begin{gather*}
\protosym{S}\cdot\assocsym{S}{A}\Red\assocsym{S}{A}\tag{$\CRule{11}$}\\
\protosym{S}\cdot\assocsym{S}{B}\Red\assocsym{S}{B}\tag{$\CRule{12}$}\\
\protosym{S}\cdot\assocsym{S}{C}\Red\assocsym{S}{C}\tag{$\CRule{13}$}
\end{gather*}

Now, the main event. Rule~5 also overlaps with rule~6. We resolve this critical pair like so, using rule $\CRule{11}$, which was a consequence of the identity conformance rule:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=-2em]
& \assocsym{S}{C} \cdot \assocsym{S}{C} \cdot \assocsym{S}{A}
\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow] \\
\protosym{S} \cdot \assocsym{S}{A} \arrow[rd, Rightarrow] && \assocsym{S}{C} \cdot \assocsym{S}{B} \arrow[ld, Rightarrow, dashed] \\
& \assocsym{S}{A}
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\end{ceqn}
This is how we get a rewrite rule representing the derived same-type requirement we mentioned at the start of this example, $\SameReq{\rT.A}{\rT.C.B}$:
\begin{gather*}
\assocsym{S}{C} \cdot \assocsym{S}{B} \Red \assocsym{S}{A} \tag{$\CRule{14}$}
\end{gather*}

An identity conformance rule also overlaps with itself, and the resulting critical pair is always trivial:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
\protosym{S}\cdot\protosym{S}\cdot\protosym{S}\arrow[d, Rightarrow, xshift=-10pt]\arrow[d, Rightarrow, xshift=10pt]\\
\protosym{S}\cdot\protosym{S}
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\end{ceqn}
There are a few remaining critical pairs, but they are also trivial, so we have a finite complete rewriting system for the protocol machine of \texttt{S}.

Finally, the requirement machine for the generic signature $G_{\texttt{S}}$ adds four more rules:
\begin{gather*}
\rT \cdot \protosym{S} \Red \rT \tag{15} \\
\rT \cdot \nA \Red \rT \cdot \assocsym{S}{A} \tag{$\CRule{16}$} \\
\rT \cdot \nB \Red \rT \cdot \assocsym{S}{B} \tag{$\CRule{17}$} \\
\rT \cdot \nC \Red \rT \cdot \assocsym{S}{C} \tag{$\CRule{18}$}
\end{gather*}
We don't need to discuss how $\CRule{16}$, $\CRule{17}$, and $\CRule{18}$ are derived since they are entirely analogous to previous examples. Now, it is easy to see that for example, both $\rT \cdot \nC \cdot \nB$ and $\rT \cdot \nA$ reduce to the same term, $\rT \cdot \assocsym{S}{A}$, proving the equality between the two type parameters.

Completion of protocol \texttt{S} demonstrates the Requirement Machine's ability to discover complex equivalences between type parameters. This example originated in a developer's bug report from 2020 \cite{sr12120}; \index{history}at the time, it broke the older \Index{GenericSignatureBuilder@\texttt{GenericSignatureBuilder}}\texttt{GenericSignatureBuilder} implementation of generics in the Swift compiler.

This example also illustrates a difference between Swift and \index{Rust}Rust generics. If we attempt to express this design using trait bounds in Rust, the Rust compiler's generics implementation is unable to prove the derived requirement:
\begin{Verbatim}
trait S {
  type A;
  type B;
  type C: S<A = Self::B, C = Self>;
}

fn f<T: S>(val: <T::C as S>::B) {
  let val2: T::A = val;
  // note: expected associated type `<T as S>::A'
  // found associated type `<<T as S>::C as S>::B'
}
\end{Verbatim}
\end{example}

\section{Recursive Conformances}\label{sec:recursive conformances redux}

Having seen some examples where completion is applied to a generic signature with a finite set of type parameters, and another example with an infinite set of type parameters but a finite set of equivalence classes, we move on to generic signatures with an \index{generic signature!infinite}\index{infinite generic signature}infinite set of equivalence classes. This will demonstrate the true purpose of the still mysterious associated type symbols, as well some details about our reduction order. Indeed, with each prior example from this chapter, completion would have still finished successfully without adding symbols and with any reduction order, but as we will see shortly, this is no longer the case once we have an infinite set of equivalence classes.

\paragraph{Tietze transformations.} First, we must address an even more fundamental issue. We add these \index{associated type symbol!Tietze transformation}associated type symbols paired with \index{associated type rule!Tietze transformation}associated type rules, then the \index{Knuth-Bendix algorithm!Tietze transformation}Knuth-Bendix algorithm adds new rules, and the simplification passes from \SecRef{sec:rule simplification} remove rules. How can we be sure that our end result even presents a \index{monoid isomorphism}monoid isomorphic to the one we started with? As it turns out, if two finite monoid presentations define isomorphic monoids, we can obtain one from the other by something akin to a rewriting process on monoid presentations.

\begin{definition}
Let $\AR$ be a \index{monoid presentation!Tietze transformation}monoid presentation. A \IndexDefinition{Tietze transformation}\emph{Tietze transformation} is one of the following:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Adding a rule.} If a pair of words $u$, $v\in A^*$ are already joined by a rewrite path over the rules of $R$---that is, if $u\Eq_R v$---we can add $(u,v)$ to $R$ to obtain a new presentation of the same monoid:
\[\Pres{A}{R\cup\{(u,v)\}}\]
\item \textbf{Removing a rule.} If $(u,v)\in R$, and we have $u \Eq_R v$ by some rewrite path that does not involve $(u,v)$, we can remove $(u,v)$ from $R$:
\[\Pres{A}{R\setminus\{(u,v)\}}\]
\item \textbf{Adding a generator.} If $a$ is a symbol distinct from every other element of $A$, and $w\in A^*$ is any word, we can introduce $a$ together with a rule $(w,a)$:
\[\Pres{A\cup\{a\}}{R\cup\{(w,a)\}}\]
\item \textbf{Removing a generator.} If $a\in A$, $(w,a)\in R$ for some word $w$ not involving $a$, and no other $(u,v)\in R$ has any occurrences of $a$ on either side, we can simultaneously eliminate $a$ and $(w,a)$:
\[\Pres{A\setminus\{a\}}{R\setminus\{(w,a)\}}\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Tietze transformations play a fundamental role in the study of \emph{combinatorial group theory}; for example, see~\cite{combinatorialgroup}. The more general case of monoid presentations is described in \cite{book2012string}~and~\cite{henry2021tietze}. The key result is the following, which we state without proof:

\begin{theorem}
Suppose that $\AR$ and $\Pres{C}{S}$ are two monoid presentations, with $A$, $C$, $R$, and $S$ all finite. Then $\AR$ and $\Pres{C}{S}$ present isomorphic monoids if and only if $\Pres{C}{S}$ can be obtained from $\AR$ by the application of a finite sequence of Tietze transformations.
\end{theorem}

In short, while our process of introducing new symbols followed by completion certainly changes the reduction relation $\Red$, it does not change the monoid congruence $\Eq$.

\begin{example}\label{ex:protocol n fcrs}
The first of the two examples in this section is \IndexProto{N}protocol \tN, previously discussed in \ExRef{protocol n example}, \ExRef{protocol n graph}, and \ExRef{ex:protocol n fcrs}:
\VerbInput{generic-signatures/N.swift}
Recall that in the protocol generic signature $G_\tN$, the type parameters \rT, \texttt{\rT.A}, \texttt{\rT.A.A}, \texttt{\rT.A.A.A}, \ldots{} are all distinct, and they all again conform to \tN.

\smallskip

(\emph{First attempt.}) We begin by looking at the protocol machine for \tN, except we will omit the associated type symbol $\aNA$ and the associated type rule $\pN \cdot \nA \Red \aNA$ that would otherwise be present. That is, we pretend our protocol machine has just one initial rule, for the associated conformance requirement $\AssocConfReq{Self.A}{N}{N}$:
\[
\ProtoConf{\pN\cdot\nA}{N}
\]
We immediately see that this rule overlaps with itself on the term $\pN \cdot \nA \cdot \pN \cdot \nA \cdot \pN$:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=-2em]
&\pN \cdot \nA \cdot \pN \cdot \nA \cdot \pN \arrow[ld, Rightarrow] \arrow[rd, Rightarrow]\\
\pN \cdot \nA \cdot \nA \cdot \pN \arrow[rd, Rightarrow, dashed] && \pN \cdot \nA \cdot \pN \cdot \nA \arrow[ld, Rightarrow] \\
& \pN \cdot \nA \cdot \nA
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\end{ceqn}
We get a new rule out of this:
\[
\ProtoConf{\pN \cdot \nA \cdot \nA}{N}
\]
The new rule overlaps with the initial rule, and with itself, in various ways. Let's consider one of those overlaps:
\begin{align*}
\pN \cdot \nA \cdot \nA \cdot {} &\pN \\
&\pN \cdot \nA \cdot \pN
\end{align*}
Resolving this overlap introduces an even longer rule with the same form:
\[
\ProtoConf{\pN \cdot \nA \cdot \nA \cdot \nA}{N}
\]
This rule again overlaps with itself, and with the previous two rules. No matter what critical pair we choose to resolve next, we discover there are more unresolved critical pairs, and we find ourselves enumerating an infinite sequence of rules:
\[
\ProtoConf{\pN \cdot \nA^n}{N} \tag{for all $n \geq 1$}\\
\]
Indeed, if we take any two rules in this sequence, say with left-hand sides $\pN \cdot \nA^m \cdot \pN$ and $\pN \cdot \nA^n \cdot \pN$, we see that they overlap, and resolving the critical pair yields another rule with an even longer left-hand side $\pN \cdot \nA^{m+n} \cdot \pN$. With these input parameters, completion does not converge! The Requirement Machine would be useless if it did not accept this simple protocol though, so let's think about it some more.

\smallskip

(\emph{Second attempt.})
Now, we add the associated type symbol $\aNA$ and associated type rule $\pN \cdot \nA \Red \aNA$, as in the real implementation, but we're going to play with the \index{reduction order!in requirement machine}reduction order:
\begin{gather*}
\AssocIntro{N}{A}\tag{1}\\
\ProtoConf{\pN\cdot\nA}{N}\tag{2}
\end{gather*}
Recall that \AlgRef{symbol reduction order} puts \index{associated type symbol!reduction order}associated type symbols before name symbols, so $\aNA < \nA$. We pretend that $\nA < \aNA$, instead. The second rule has an overlap of the first kind with the first rule, and resolving this critical pair yields the following rule:
\[
\ProtoConf{\aNA}{N}\tag{$\CRule{3}$}
\]
Rule 2 is then marked \textbf{left-simplified}. Now, rule $\CRule{3}$ overlaps with rule 1:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=-2em]
&\aNA\cdot\pN\cdot\nA\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow]\\
\aNA\cdot\nA && \aNA\cdot\aNA\arrow[ll, Rightarrow, dashed]
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\end{ceqn}
Resolving the above with our broken reduction order yields the rule:
\[
\aNA \cdot \aNA \Red \aNA \cdot \nA
\]
The above rule overlaps with rule $\CRule{3}$, and also with itself:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=-2em]
&\aNA\cdot\aNA\cdot\pN\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow]\\
\aNA\cdot\nA\cdot\pN\arrow[rd, Rightarrow, dashed] && \aNA\cdot\aNA\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\\
&\aNA\cdot\nA
\end{tikzcd}
\qquad
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=-2em]
&\aNA\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow]\\
\aNA\cdot\nA\cdot\aNA\arrow[rd, Rightarrow, dashed] && \aNA\cdot\aNA\cdot\nA\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\\
&\aNA\cdot\nA\cdot\nA
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\end{ceqn}
We record two new rules:
\begin{gather*}
\aNA\cdot\nA\cdot\pN \Red \aNA\cdot\nA \\
\aNA\cdot\nA\cdot\aNA \Red \aNA\cdot\nA\cdot\nA
\end{gather*}
However, the second of the two rules above again overlaps with $\aNA \cdot \pN \Red \aNA$, and also with $\aNA \cdot \aNA \Red \aNA \cdot \nA$. We have another non-terminating completion, even worse than our first attempt, because we're enumerating \emph{two} infinite families of rules:
\begin{gather*}
\ProtoConf{\aNA \cdot \nA^n}{N} \qquad \tag{for all $n \geq 1$} \\
\aNA \cdot \nA^n \cdot \aNA \Red \aNA \cdot \nA^{n+1} \qquad \tag{for all $n \geq 1$}
\end{gather*}
We're not having much luck so far, either with the original alphabet $\{\pN,\nA\}$, or with the extended alphabet $\{\pN,\nA,\aNA\}$ and \index{shortlex order}shortlex order $\pN < \nA < \aNA$.

\smallskip

(\emph{Third attempt.})
Finally, let's use the actual Requirement Machine reduction order as specified in \AlgRef{symbol reduction order}, where $\pN < \aNA < \nA$. Like the second attempt, we have two initial rules, and we add a third rule, allowing us to left-simplify the second rule:
\begin{gather*}
\AssocIntro{N}{A}\tag{1}\\
\ProtoConf{\pN\cdot\nA}{N}\tag{2}\\
\ProtoConf{\aNA}{N}\tag{$\CRule{3}$}
\end{gather*}
This time, when rule $\CRule{3}$ overlaps with rule 1, the critical pair resolves differently:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=-2em]
&\aNA\cdot\pN\cdot\nA\arrow[ld, Rightarrow]\arrow[rd, Rightarrow]\\
\aNA\cdot\nA\arrow[rr, Rightarrow, dashed] && \aNA\cdot\aNA
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\end{ceqn}
We get the following rule, with the opposite orientation than the previous attempt:
\[
\aNA\cdot\nA\Red\aNA\cdot\aNA\tag{$\CRule{4}$}
\]
Remarkably, no more critical pairs remain! We get a finite complete rewriting system for the protocol machine of \tN, consisting of only three rules after simplification.

Finally, the requirement machine for the generic signature~$G_\tN$ has two more rules:
\begin{gather*}
\ProtoConf{\rT}{N}\tag{5}\\
\rT\cdot\nA\Red\rT\cdot\aNA\tag{$\CRule{6}$}
\end{gather*}
Let's spot check that $G_\tN \vdash \ConfReq{\rT.A.A.A}{N}$. By \ThmRef{derivation to path swift}, this should imply that the two terms $\rT\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\pN$ and $\rT\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\nA$ are equivalent. We reduce the right-hand side by an application of rule $\CRule{6}$ followed by two applications of rule $\CRule{4}$:
\begin{align*}
\rT\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\nA & \Red \rT\cdot\aNA\cdot\nA\cdot\nA \\
& \Red \rT\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA\cdot\nA \\
& \Red \rT\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA
\end{align*}
We then reduce the left-hand side with the same sequence of rules, followed by rule~$\CRule{3}$ to delete the protocol symbol $\pN$ from the end:
\begin{align*}
\rT\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\pN & \Red \rT\cdot\aNA\cdot\nA\cdot\nA\cdot\pN \\
& \Red \rT\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA\cdot\nA\cdot\pN \\
& \Red \rT\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA\cdot\pN \\
& \Red \rT\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA
\end{align*}
Both sides reduce to $\rT\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA\cdot\aNA$, so they are equivalent with respect to our initial rules, just as we would expect.

\smallskip

(\emph{Summary.})
Let's restate this example using \index{monoid presentation}monoid presentations. We write $n$, $a$, $c$ instead of $\pN$, \nA, $\aNA$. The protocol machine of \tN\ has a \index{one-relation monoid}one-relation monoid presentation:
\[
\Pres{n, a}{nan \Eq na}
\]
However, simply feeding this presentation into the Knuth-Bendix algorithm was fruitless, because the process does not converge. Next, we added a symbol $c$ equivalent to $na$:
\[
\Pres{n, a, c}{na \Eq c,\, nan \Eq na}
\]
Our second attempt took this presentation with the shortlex order $n < a < c$, but that did not work either. Finally, the shortlex order $n < c < a$ produced a finite complete presentation of our one-relation monoid:
\[
\Pres{n, a, c}{na \Eq c,\, cn \Eq c,\, ca \Eq cc}
\]
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{ex:protocol o fcrs}
In our second example, we consider the following relatively common setup. The standard library \IndexProto{Collection}\tCollection\ protocol declares an \texttt{Indices} associated type that conforms to \tCollection. The \texttt{BidirectionalCollection} protocol then inherits from \tCollection, and further states that its inherited \texttt{Indices} type also conforms to \texttt{BidirectionalCollection}:
\begin{Verbatim}
protocol Collection {
  ...
  associatedtype Indices: Collection
  ...
}

protocol BidirectionalCollection: Collection
    where Indices: BidirectionalCollection {
  ...
}
\end{Verbatim}
This all boils down to protocol \texttt{O} below, which inherits from our favorite protocol \tN:
\begin{Verbatim}
protocol O: N where A: O {}
\end{Verbatim}
Two properties of the implementation will play an important role here:
\begin{enumerate}
\item While protocol \texttt{O} does not directly declare an associated type named~\nA, it inherits~\nA\ from~\tN, and \AlgRef{rules for protocol algo} introduces the associated type symbol $\aOA$ and associated type rule $\AssocIntro{O}{A}$.

\item Instead of \index{protocol symbol!reduction order}\index{reduction order!in requirement machine}comparing protocol names lexicographically, \AlgRef{protocol reduction order} compares inheritance depth first.
\end{enumerate}
We will see that successful completion requires both of the above. As before, we will walk through three ``completion attempts'' where the first two fail to terminate, but we omit some details this time around because they will look quite similar to the previous example.

\smallskip

(\emph{First attempt.}) Let's keep the associated type symbol $\aNA$ with associated type rule $\AssocIntro{A}{N}$, but skip introducing the second symbol $\aOA$ and its associated type rule $\AssocIntro{A}{O}$. We take the finite complete rewriting system for protocol \tN\ from the previous example, and add two rules, corresponding to $\AssocConfReq{Self}{N}{O}$ and $\AssocConfReq{Self.A}{O}{O}$:
\begin{gather*}
\AssocIntro{N}{A}\tag{1}\\
\ProtoConf{\aNA}{N}\tag{2}\\
\aNA\cdot\nA\Red\aNA\cdot\aNA\tag{$\CRule{3}$}\\
\midrule
\ProtoConf{\pO}{N}\tag{4}\\
\ProtoConf{\pO\cdot\nA}{O}\tag{5}
\end{gather*}
Rule 4 overlaps with rule 1, and resolving this critical pair adds the following rule:
\[
\pO\cdot\nA \Red \pO\cdot\aNA
\]
Rule 5 then overlaps with the above rule, and we get:
\[
\pO\cdot\aNA\cdot\pO \Red \pO\cdot\aNA
\]
At this point, we face the same problem as the first attempt in the previous example, where we had a rule $\pN\cdot\nA\cdot\pN$ that overlaps with itself. As before, we see that completion runs away, generating this infinite sequence of rules:
\[
\pO\cdot\aNA^n\cdot\pO \Red \pO\cdot\aNA^n \tag{for all $n \geq 1$}
\]
This form of recursion thus demonstrates why we must introduce associated type symbols for inherited associated types as well as those directly stated in the protocol.

\smallskip

(\emph{Second attempt.}) We now add both $\aNA$ and $\aOA$, but we use the wrong reduction order that only compares protocol names lexicographically, instead of checking their inheritance depth first. Under this order, we have $\pN < \pO$ and $\aNA < \aOA$. (This is why we named our protocol ``\tO''; if it was named ``\texttt{M}'' for example, the example would work on accident even with the wrong order.)

We start with the following:
\begin{gather*}
\AssocIntro{N}{A}\tag{1}\\
\ProtoConf{\aNA}{N}\tag{2}\\
\aNA\cdot\nA\Red\aNA\cdot\aNA\tag{$\CRule{3}$}\\
\midrule
\AssocIntro{O}{A}\tag{4}\\
\ProtoConf{\pO}{N}\tag{5}\\
\ProtoConf{\pO\cdot\nA}{O}\tag{6}
\end{gather*}
Rule 5 overlaps with rule 4, and we get:
\[
\ProtoConf{\aOA}{O}
\]
So far so good. However, after resolving a couple more critical pairs, we get two rules that look like this:
\begin{gather*}
\aOA \cdot \nA \Red \aOA \cdot \aNA \\
\aOA \cdot \aOA \Red \aOA \cdot \aNA
\end{gather*}
The second rule's left-hand side overlaps with our conformance rule $\ProtoConf{\aOA}{O}$, and this time, we get stuck enumerating two infinite rule families:
\begin{gather*}
\ProtoConf{\aOA\cdot\aNA^n}{O} \tag{for all $n \geq 1$}\\
\aOA\cdot\aNA^n\cdot\aOA \Red \aOA\cdot\aNA^{n+1} \tag{for all $n \geq 1$}
\end{gather*}

\smallskip

(\emph{Third attempt.}) With both associated type symbols $\aNA$ and $\aOA$, and the correct reduction order where $\aOA < \aNA$, we finally obtain a finite complete rewriting system for \texttt{O}, consisting of the 6 rules above, together with 5 more rules, listed below:
\begin{gather*}
\ProtoConf{\aOA}{O} \tag{$\CRule{7}$}\\
\ProtoConf{\aOA}{N} \tag{$\CRule{8}$}\\
\pO\cdot\aNA \Red \aOA \tag{$\CRule{9}$}\\
\aOA\cdot\aNA \Red \aOA \cdot \aOA \tag{$\CRule{10}$}\\
\aOA\cdot\nA \Red \aOA \cdot \aOA \tag{$\CRule{11}$}
\end{gather*}
Note that the original rule 6 becomes \textbf{left-simplified}.

(\emph{Summary.}) Let's write $n$, $o$, $a$, $c$, $d$ instead of $\pN$, $\pO$, $\nA$, $\aNA$, $\aOA$. The protocol machine for \tO\ is a three-relation monoid:
\[
\Pres{n, o, a}{nan \Eq na,\, on \Eq o,\, oao \Eq oa}
\]
To obtain a finite complete presentation, we had to first add two symbols $c$ and $d$, equivalent to $na$ and $oa$, respectively. Then using the shortlex order $o < n < d < c < a$, we got this finite complete presentation of our original monoid:
\begin{align*}
\Pres{n, o, a, c, d}{{}&na \Eq c,\, cn \Eq c,\, ca \Eq cc,\, \\
&oa \Eq d,\, on \Eq o,\, do \Eq d,\, dn \Eq d,\, oc \Eq d,\, dc \Eq dd, da \Eq dd}
\end{align*}

This example shows another difference between Swift and \index{Rust}Rust generics. Rust rejects the ``obvious'' translation of protocol \tO:
\VerbInput{completion/O1.rs}
However, this somewhat indirect formulation works:
\VerbInput{completion/O2.rs}
\end{example}

\paragraph{Morphocompletion.}
The Requirement Machine uses a simple strategy to introduce generators in a fixed manner, and then it runs completion with a fixed reduction order; our multiple ``attempts'' above were just illustrative. However, it is possible to construct a more advanced completion implementation which really does make multiple completion attempts, applying different heuristics for choosing generators and reduction orders on each attempt. This is known as \emph{morphocompletion}~\cite{morph}. Searching for a finite complete presentation is an extremely hard problem in general. At the end of \SecRef{sec:normal form algorithm}, we mentioned that it is unknown if every one-relation monoid admits a finite complete presentation. To drive this point home, here is a concrete instance which is unsolved at the time of writing:
\begin{quote}
Does the monoid $\Pres{a,b}{abbaab \Eq ba}$ admit a finite complete presentation over any alphabet?
\end{quote}

\section{Regular Languages}\label{sec:regular languages}

The proof in the next section relies on the theory of \emph{regular languages}. A \emph{language} in this sense is a subset of a \index{free monoid}free monoid over some alphabet, and regular languages are sometimes known as \emph{regular sets}. Regular languages appear in programming in the form of \index{regular expression|see{regular language}}\emph{regular expressions}. Here is a typical regular expression, written in Unix style:
\begin{quote}
\texttt{t(a*)(p|q)}
\end{quote}
This regular expression will match various strings such as \texttt{tp} and \texttt{taaaq}, but not \texttt{pat}.

We only need a few definitions, together with the statement of the pumping lemma. A complete treatment of this subject can be found in \cite{transductions}.

\begin{definition}
Let $A^*$ be the \index{free monoid}free monoid over some set $A$, and let $X$, $Y \subseteq A^*$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The \index{concatenation}\emph{concatenation} of $X$ and $Y$, denoted $XY$ or $X \cdot Y$, is defined as the set of all possible words $xy$ formed by taking some $x \in X$ and $y \in Y$.
\item If $n \geq 0$, then $X^n$ is the concatenation of $X$ with itself $n$ times, which is the set of all words formed by concatenating any $n$ elements taken from $X$, with repetitions allowed. In particular, $X^0=\{\varepsilon\}$ is the singleton set consisting of the \index{empty word}empty word, while $X^1=X$.
\item The \IndexDefinition{Kleene star}\emph{Kleene star} of $X$, denoted by $X^*$, is the set of all words that can be formed by concatenating elements of $X$ any number of times:
\[
X^* := X^0 \cup X^1 \cup X^2 \cdots \cup X^n \cup \cdots
\]
\item We also define $X^+ := XX^* = X^* \setminus \{\varepsilon\}$; this is the set of all words formed by concatenating elements of $X$ one or more times.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

A regular language, or a regular subset of $A^*$, is then one that can be constructed by a finite combination of the operations below:

\begin{definition}
Let $A^*$ be the free monoid over some set $A$. The family of \IndexDefinition{regular language}\emph{regular languages} of $A^*$ is defined inductively as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $X \subset A^*$ is a finite set of words, then $X$ is a regular language.
\item If $X$, $Y \subseteq A^*$ are regular languages, their \index{union}set union $X \cup Y$ is a regular language.
\item If $X$, $Y \subseteq A^*$ are regular languages, their \index{concatenation}concatenation $XY$ is a regular language.
\item If $X \subseteq A^*$ is a regular language, the Kleene star $X^*$ is a regular language.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

To minimize clutter when specifying a regular language, we say that a word actually stands for the singleton set containing that word, so for example $a^*$ and $taq$ will denote $\{a\}^*$ and $\{taq\}$, respectively.

\begin{example}
In our notation, the regular expression from the start of this section, when viewed as a subset of $\{t,p,q,a\}^*$, can be written like this:
\[
t \cdot a^* \cdot \{p,q\}
\]
\end{example}

A standard result is that regular languages are precisely those that can be recognized by a \index{finite state machine}finite state machine. This can be used to show that regular languages satisfy some closure properties, by explicitly constructing a finite state machine for each case.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:regular language closure}
Let $X$, $Y \subseteq A^*$ be any two regular languages.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The intersection $X \cap Y$ is a regular language.
\item The \index{complement}complement $A^* \setminus X$ is a regular language.
\item The set formed by reversing each word in $X$ is a regular language.
\end{enumerate}
Also, the empty set $\varnothing$ and the entire set $A^*$ are both regular.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Pumping lemma]\label{pumping lemma}
If $X \subseteq A^*$ is a regular language, there exists a natural number $\Ell > 0$ such that any word $u \in X$ with $|u| \geq \Ell$ has a factorization:
\[
u = xyz
\]
with the property that $|y| > 0$, $|xy| \leq \Ell$, and for all $n \geq 0$:
\[
xy^nz \in X
\]
\end{lemma}

The proof can be found in \cite{transductions}, but the general idea is the following. When $X$ is finite, we set $\Ell$ to be longer than the longest word in $X$, and the conclusion is vacuously true. Otherwise, if $X$ is infinite, we can always find a word $u \in X$ whose length exceeds the number of states in the finite state machine for $X$. This finite state machine must visit at least one state more than once while reading~$u$, but it cannot ``remember'' the fact that it visited the same state more than once, so we can repeat the sequence of symbols visited along such a path any number of times to obtain longer elements of $X$. This gives us the factor $y$ in the statement of the lemma.

\begin{remark}
Our statement of the pumping lemma yields a factorization ``from the start'' with $|xy| \leq \Ell$. However, by part~3 of \LemmaRef{lemma:regular language closure}, regular languages are closed under reversal, which implies a symmetric form of the pumping lemma where the factorization is ``from the end'' instead, so $|yz| \leq \Ell$. In the next section, we will refer to this as applying the pumping lemma ``from the start'' or ``from the end.''
\end{remark}

Now, we will relate all of this to complete rewriting systems. Recall the definition of an \index{irreducible word}irreducible word from \SecRef{sec:normal form algorithm}, and consider the set of all irreducible words in some monoid presentation $\AR$, that is, all words from $A^*$ that avoid the left-hand sides of $R$. We will denote this set by \index{$\Irr(R)$}\index{$\Irr(R)$!z@\igobble|seealso{irreducible word}}$\Irr(R)$ in what follows.

\begin{remark}
The notation $\Irr(R)$ actually depends on the choice of alphabet $A$, so if $R=\{(a,\varepsilon)\}$ and $A=\{a\}$ for example, then $\Irr(R) = \{ \varepsilon \}$, but if we take the same $R$ with $A=\{a,b\}$, then $\Irr(R)=b^*$. On the other hand, the definition of $\Irr(R)$ does not depend on the \emph{right-hand} sides of~$R$ at all.
\end{remark}

The following properties of $\Irr(R)$ will be useful to us.

\begin{lemma}\label{irr r facts}
Let $\AR$ be any monoid presentation, with $R$ not necessarily finite.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $x$, $y$, $z \in A^*$ and $xyz \in \Irr(R)$, then $y \in \Irr(R)$, so any factor of an irreducible word is itself irreducible.
\item If $R$ is finite, then $\Irr(R)$ is a regular set.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For the first part, we prove the contrapositive statement. If $y \not\in \Irr(R)$, then $y=y'uy''$ for some $(u, v) \in R$, in which case $xyz=xy'uy''z$ also certainly contains this left-hand side from $R$, and thus $xyz \not\in \Irr(R)$.

For the second part, let $R = \{ (u_1, v_1), \, \ldots, \, (u_n, v_n) \}$. we can express the complement of $\Irr(R)$ in $A^*$ as follows:
\[
A^* \setminus \Irr(R) = (A^* \cdot u_1 \cdot A^*) \cup \cdots \cup (A^* \cdot u_n \cdot A^*)
\]
This is a regular set, and by \LemmaRef{lemma:regular language closure}, so is its complement, which is just $\Irr(R)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
In \ExRef{ex:protocol n fcrs}, we saw this finite complete presentation:
\[
\Pres{n,a,c}{na \Eq c, cn \Eq c, ca \Eq cc}
\]
The \emph{reducible} words with respect to this presentation are those that contain at least one occurrence of $pa$, $cp$, or $ca$. This the irreducible words are those where $p$ does not follow $c$, and $a$ does not follow $p$ or $c$, which is precisely this regular set:
\[
a^* \cdot n^* \cdot c^*
\]
If we take a word from the above regular language having at least one non-empty block of $a$'s, $n$'s, or $c$'s, we can repeat that letter any number of times to obtain a longer irreducible word. Thus, $\Ell = 1$ in this case.
\end{example}

The pumping lemma allows us to construct long irreducible words, and the next lemma allows us to take two long irreducible words and overlap them to form an even longer irreducible word. Both of these lemmas will be important in the next section.

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:irreducible overlap}
Suppose that $xy\in \Irr(R)$ and $yz\in\Irr(R)$. Then, we either have $xyz\in\Irr(R)$, or there exists a rule $(u,v)\in R$ with $|u| > |y|$. In particular, if $y$ is at least as long as the longest left-hand side in $R$, we must have $xyz \in \Irr(R)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If $xyz \in \Irr(R)$, we're done, so assume that $xyz \not\in \Irr(R)$, with $xyz=x'uz'$ where $u$ is the left-hand side of a rule in $R$. Suppose that to the contrary, $|u| \leq |y|$. Either $|x'u| \leq |xy|$, or $|x'u| > |xy|$. If $|x'u| \leq |xy|$, then $x'u \not\in \Irr(R)$ is a prefix of $xy \in \Irr(R)$, which is impossible by \LemmaRef{irr r facts}. On the other hand, if $|x'u| > |xy|$, then $|z'| < |z|$, and since also $|u| \leq |y|$, now $uz'\not\in \Irr(R)$ is a suffix of $yz \in \Irr(R)$, which is again impossible. Therefore $|u| > |y|$.
\end{proof}

\section{The Event Horizon}\label{sec:event horizon}

In \SecRef{word problem}, we constructed a generic signature which inherently cannot be accepted by the Swift compiler because it has an undecidable theory. This section will discuss a more subtle kind of intractability, first mentioned in \SecRef{sec:normal form algorithm}. We will study a generic signature that is fully decidable, but one whose requirement machine nonetheless \index{limitation!no finite complete presentation}\index{generic signature!with no finite complete presentation}\textsl{does not admit a finite complete presentation}. The example is simple. We make two ``copies'' of \IndexProto{N}protocol \tN, and then we declare a generic parameter conforming to both:
\VerbInput{completion/tpqa.swift}
The Swift compiler quickly rejects the above with an error, pointing at struct \texttt{G}:
\begin{quote}
\begin{verbatim}
error: cannot build rewrite system for generic signature;
rule length limit exceeded
\end{verbatim}
\end{quote}
Here is the generic signature of the struct \texttt{G}:
\begin{quote}
\begin{verbatim}
<τ_0_0 where τ_0_0: P, τ_0_0: Q>
\end{verbatim}
\end{quote}
An interesting fact is that the \Index{GenericSignatureBuilder@\texttt{GenericSignatureBuilder}}\texttt{GenericSignatureBuilder} from \IndexSwift{5.5}Swift~5.5 and earlier accepted this generic signature, and this was perhaps the only regression in functionality that came with the introduction of the Requirement Machine. Let's take a closer look!

Much like the generic signatures of protocol \tN\ from \ExRef{ex:protocol n fcrs} and protocol \tO\ from \ExRef{ex:protocol o fcrs}, here we have an infinite set of distinct type parameters: \rT, \texttt{\rT.A}, \texttt{\rT.A.A}, \texttt{\rT.A.A.A}, and so on. However, unlike protocol \tN, every type parameter now conforms to two protocols, and unlike protocol \tO, these two protocols are not related by protocol inheritance. We have four requirements in total, one from each protocol and then two on the signature itself:
\begin{gather*}
\AssocConfReq{Self.A}{P}{P} \\
\AssocConfReq{Self.A}{Q}{Q} \\
\ConfReq{\rT}{P} \\
\ConfReq{\rT}{Q}
\end{gather*}
If we translate each of the above requirements to rewrite rules, we get the following:
\begin{gather*}
\ProtoConf{\pP \cdot \nA}{P} \\
\midrule
\ProtoConf{\pQ \cdot \nA}{Q} \\
\midrule
\ProtoConf{\rT}{P} \\
\ProtoConf{\rT}{Q}
\end{gather*}
We don't bother adding symbols $\aPA$ and $\aQA$ equivalent to $\pP \cdot \nA$ and $\pQ \cdot \nA$ here, since they are of no use. Also, we write $t$, $p$, $q$, $a$ instead of $\rT$, $\pP$, $\pQ$, $\nA$. We get a monoid with the following presentation:
\[
M := \Pres{t,p,q,a}{pap \Eq pa,\, qaq \Eq qa,\,tp \Eq t,\,tq \Eq t}
\]
We now ask, could we still obtain a finite complete presentation for $M$ if we added a different set of symbols first, or perhaps if we used some exotic reduction order? The answer is no, because:

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:monoid m is not fcrs}
The monoid $M$ has a decidable word problem, but no \index{finite complete presentation}finite complete presentation over any alphabet.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
This theorem makes two claims. We settle each one as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
\item To show that $M$ has a decidable word problem, we construct an \emph{infinite} complete presentation $\Pres{A}{\RInf}$ of $M$.
\item To show that $M$ has no \emph{finite} complete presentation, we assume that $\Pres{C}{S}$ is such a presentation, and derive a contradiction.
\end{enumerate}

From now on, $M$ will always denote the above monoid, and $A$~and~$R$ will refer to the alphabet and rules in our original presentation of $M$:
\begin{gather*}
A := \{ t, p, q, a \}\\
R := \{ (pap, pa), (qaq, qa), (tp, t), (tq, t) \}
\end{gather*}
The letters $t$, $p$, $q$, $a$ will only ever denote the elements of~$A$. Since the rules of $R$ are symmetric in $p$~and~$q$, all of our lemmas will hold when $p$ is replaced with $q$, or vice versa. To avoid needless repetition, we often say that $s \in \PAndQ$. We use the remaining lowercase letters to denote various words over $A^*$. The monoid congruence $\Eq_R$ generated by~$R$ will simply be denoted by~$\Eq$.

\paragraph{The word problem in $M$.}
Even though Knuth-Bendix fails to terminate on $\Pres{A}{R}$, we nonetheless get an infinite sequence of rules that follow a simple pattern, and in particular, their left-hand sides enumerate a regular set. This infinite set of rules, which we denote by $\RInf$, can be described in finite terms.

\begin{definition}\label{def:tpqa infinite complete}
We define $\RInf \subset A^* \times A^*$ to consist of the following:
\begin{itemize}
\item For all $n \geq 1$:
\begin{gather*}
pa^np \Red pa^n \tag{$1_n$} \\
qa^nq \Red qa^n \tag{$2_n$}
\end{gather*}
\item For all $n \geq 0$:
\begin{gather*}
ta^np \Red ta^n \tag{$3_n$} \\
ta^nq \Red ta^n \tag{$4_n$}
\end{gather*}
\end{itemize}
The \index{reduction relation}reduction relation $\Red_{\RInf}$ generated by $\RInf$ will be denoted by $\Red$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Complete presentation]\label{lemma:rinf complete}
The presentation $\Pres{A}{\RInf}$ given above is an \index{infinite complete presentation}infinite complete presentation of $M$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
First, we must show that $\Pres{A}{\RInf}$ is a presentation of $\AR$. It is clear that $R \subset \RInf$, so every rule in $R$ is trivially a consequence of $\RInf$. To establish that every rule in $\RInf$ is also a consequence of $R$, we claim the following:
\begin{itemize}
\item (Rule families $1_n$ and $2_n$.) For all $s \in \PAndQ$ and $n \geq 1$, we have $sa^ns \Eq sa^n$.
\item (Rule families $3_n$ and $4_n$.) For all $s \in \PAndQ$ and $n \geq 0$, we have $ta^ns \Eq ta^n$.
\end{itemize}

($1_n$ and $2_n$.) We proceed by induction on~$n$. If $n=1$, we have $(sas,sa) \in R$. If $n>1$, we have $sa^{n-1}s \Eq sa^{n-1}$ by the induction hypothesis, and then:
\begin{align*}
sa^ns & = (sa^{n-1})as \\
& \Eq (sa^{n-1}s)as \\
& = sa^{n-1}(sas) \\
& \Eq sa^{n-1}(sa) \\
& = (sa^{n-1}s)a \\
& \Eq sa^{n-1}a = sa^n
\end{align*}

\smallskip

($3_n$ and $4_n$.) If $n=0$, we have $(ts, t) \in R$. If $n \geq 1$, we make use of $(ts, t) \in R$, together with the first claim that $sa^ns \Eq sa^n$:
\begin{align*}
ta^ns & \Eq (ts)a^ns \\
& = t(sa^ns) \\
& \Eq t(sa^n) \\
& = (ts)a^n \\
& \Eq ta^n
\end{align*}

(\emph{Termination.})
We turn our attention to the reduction relation $\Red$. The right-hand side of each rule in $\RInf$ is strictly shorter than the left-hand side, so any sequence of such reductions must eventually terminate in a finite number of steps.

\smallskip

(\emph{Confluence.})
Here is a list of rule families with overlapping left-hand sides:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
$1_m$ vs.~$1_n$: &
$\begin{aligned}
pa^m&p \\
    &pa^np
\end{aligned}$ &
$m \geq 1$, $n \geq 1$. \\
\midrule
$2_m$ vs.~$2_n$: &
$\begin{aligned}
qa^m&q \\
    &qa^nq
\end{aligned}$ &
$m \geq 1$, $n \geq 1$. \\
\midrule
$3_m$ vs.~$1_n$: &
$\begin{aligned}
ta^m&p \\
    &pa^np
\end{aligned}$ &
$m \geq 0$, $n \geq 1$. \\
\midrule
$4_m$ vs.~$2_n$: &
$\begin{aligned}
ta^m&q \\
    &qa^nq
\end{aligned}$ &
$m \geq 0$, $n \geq 1$. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\FigRef{fig:tpqa critical pairs} shows that all critical pairs we get from the above are trivial. Thus, $\Red$ is terminating and confluent, and $\Pres{A}{\RInf}$ is an infinite complete presentation of $M$.
\end{proof}

We've settled that $M$ has a decidable word problem, which was the first part of \ThmRef{thm:monoid m is not fcrs}. We no longer need to talk about our infinite complete presentation $\Pres{A}{\RInf}$ or the reduction relation $\Red$. However, before we can move on to the second part of the theorem, we must take a closer look at the monoid congruence $\Eq$.

\begin{definition}[Tail monoid]\label{def:tpqa oslash}
We define a binary operation $\oslash\colon A \times A \rightarrow A$:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{array}{c|cccc}
\oslash&t&p&q&a \\
\hline
t&t&t&t&t\\
p&t&p&q&p \\
q&t&p&q&q \\
a&t&p&q&a
\end{array}
\]
\end{ceqn}
This operation is associative with identity element $a$, so $(A,\, \oslash,\, a)$ is a finite monoid with 4 elements. We refer to this structure as $E$, to distinguish it from the set~$A$ unadorned with a monoid operation. A few observations about the \index{Cayley table}Cayley table above:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The subset $\PQA \subset E$ is a \index{submonoid}submonoid of $E$.
\item If $x$, $y \in E$ and $x \oslash y = a$, then both $x=y=a$.
\item The element $t \in E$ is the \index{zero element}\emph{zero element}, because $x \oslash t = t \oslash x = t$ for all $x \in E$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Degree, rank, and tail]\label{def:deg rank tail}
We define three monoid homomorphisms on the letters of $A$ as shown below, then extend them to all of $A^*$ in the usual way:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The ``degree mapping'' $\Deg\colon A^* \rightarrow \NN$ counts occurrences of $t$:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\Deg(t)=1$, $\Deg(p)=\Deg(q)=\Deg(a)=0$,
\item $\Deg(\varepsilon)=0$, $\Deg(xy)=\Deg(x)+\Deg(y)$.
\end{itemize}
\item The ``rank mapping'' $\Rank\colon A^* \rightarrow \NN$ counts occurrences of $a$:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\Rank(a)=1$, $\Rank(t)=\Rank(p)=\Rank(q)=0$,
\item $\Rank(\varepsilon)=0$, $\Rank(xy)=\Rank(x)+\Rank(y)$.
\end{itemize}
\item The ``tail mapping'' $\Tail\colon A^* \rightarrow E$ describes how words behave ``at the end'':
\begin{itemize}
\item $\Tail(t)=t$, $\Tail(p)=p$, $\Tail(q)=q$, $\Tail(a)=a$.
\item $\Tail(\varepsilon)=a$, $\Tail(xy)=\Tail(x) \oslash \Tail(y)$.
\end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Not only are they homomorphisms from $A^*$, they are homomorphisms from $M$.

\begin{lemma}[Well-defined]\label{lemma:tpqa well defined}
The $\Deg$, $\Rank$, and $\Tail$ mappings are \index{well-defined mapping}well-defined on the equivalence classes of $\Eq$, so for each such mapping $f$, $x \Eq y$ implies that $f(x)=f(y)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We apply each mapping to both sides of each $(u, v) \in R$, and see that we get the same result:
\[
\begin{array}{lll}
\Deg(tp) = \Deg(t) = 1 & \Rank(tp) = \Rank(t) = 0 & \Tail(tp) = \Tail(t) = t \\
\Deg(tq) = \Deg(t) = 1 & \Rank(tq) = \Rank(t) = 0 & \Tail(tq) = \Tail(t) = t \\
\Deg(pap) = \Deg(pa) = 0 & \Rank(pap) = \Rank(pa) = 1 & \Tail(pap) = \Tail(pa) = p \\
\Deg(qaq) = \Deg(qa) = 0 & \Rank(qaq) = \Rank(qa) = 1 & \Tail(qaq) = \Tail(qa) = q
\end{array}
\]
Furthermore, each $f \in \{\Deg,\, \Rank,\, \Tail\}$ is a monoid homomorphism, so for any rewrite step $x (u \Red v) y$ over~$R$, we have $f(xuy)=f(xvy)$. By induction on rewrite path length, we conclude that $x \Eq y$ implies $f(x) = f(y)$ for all $x$, $y \in A^*$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Degree mapping]\label{lemma:tpqa deg}
We relate the $\Deg$ mapping with $\Tail$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $\Deg(x)=0$, then $x \in \PQA^*$, and $\Tail(x) \in \PQA$.
\item If $\Deg(x)>0$, then $x = ytz$ for some $y \in A^*$, $z \in \PQA^*$, and $\Tail(x)=t$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We prove each claim in turn.

\smallskip

(1.) Suppose that $\Deg(x)=0$. There are no occurrences of $t$ in $x$ by definition of $\Deg$, so $x \in \PQA^*$. But $\Tail$ is the identity mapping on the letters of $A$, and since $\PQA$ is a submonoid of $E$, we can say that $\Tail(x) \in \PQA$.

\smallskip

(2.) Suppose that $\Deg(x)>0$. Then $x$ contains at least one $t$ by definition of $\Deg$. We pick the last $t$ and write $x=ytz$ for some $y \in A^*$ and $z \in \PQA^*$. Since $t$ is the zero element of $E$, we also get $\Tail(x)=\Tail(y) \oslash t \oslash \Tail(z) = t$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Tail mapping]\label{lemma:tpqa tail}
We describe the inverse image of each letter by $\Tail$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $\Tail(x)=a$, then $x = a^n$ for some $n \geq 0$.
\item If $\Tail(x)=t$, then $x = ytz$ for some $y \in A^*$, $z \in \PQA^*$.
\item If $\Tail(x)=s$ for some $s \in \PAndQ$, then $x=ysa^n$ for some $y \in \PQA^*$ and $n \geq 0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We prove each claim in turn.

(1.) A visual inspection of \DefRef{def:tpqa oslash} shows that when $\oslash$ is applied to two elements of $A$ where at least one is not $a$, the result cannot be $a$. Thus, $\Tail(x)=a$ implies that $x$ consists entirely of $a$'s.

\smallskip

(2.) Suppose that $\Tail(x)=t$. Since $\PQA$ is a submonoid of $E$, $x$ must contain at least one $t$, so $\Deg(x) > 0$, and $x=ytz$ for some $y \in A^*$, $z \in \PQA^*$ by \LemmaRef{lemma:tpqa deg}.

\smallskip

(3.) Suppose that $\Tail(x)=s$. Then $x$ contains at least one non-$a$ letter since otherwise we would have $\Tail(x)=a$, by definition. Also, $x$ cannot contain $t$ since then we'd have $\Tail(x)=t$. If we let $s'$ be the last non-$a$ letter in $x$, we can write $x=ys'a^n$ for some $y \in \PQA^*$, $s' \in \PAndQ$, and $n \geq 0$. Now, $\Tail(x)=\Tail(ys'a^n)=s'$, so $s'=s$ and $x$ has the claimed form.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
Let $x \in A^*$. We say that $x$ is a \emph{singleton equivalence class} of $\Eq$ if $x \Eq y$ actually implies that $x=y$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Monoid congruence]\label{lemma:tpqa word problem}
We use four properties of $\Eq$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item An $x \in A^*$ is a singleton equivalence class of $\Eq$ if and only if $x \in a^* \cdot \PAndQ^*$.
\item An $x \in A^*$ is equivalent to $ta^n$ if and only if $x=ty$ for some $y \in \PQA^*$ with $\Rank(y)=n$.
\item If $x \in A^*$ and $\Deg(x) = 0$, then $xs \NotEq x$ for at least one $s \in \PAndQ$.
\item If $x \in A^*$ and $\Deg(x) > 0$, then $xs \Eq x$ for both $s \in \PAndQ$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We prove each claim in turn.

\smallskip

(1.) An $x \in A^*$ is a singleton equivalence class of $\Eq$ if and only if $x$ does not contain either side of any rule in $R$, which is true if and only if $x$ has no occurrences of $t$ at all, and no occurrences of $a$ following $p$ or $q$. This is precisely the regular set above.

\smallskip

(2.) Suppose that $x \Eq ta^n$. No rule in $R$ changes the first letter of a word, so $x=ty$ for some $y \in A^*$. By \LemmaRef{lemma:tpqa well defined}, $\Deg(x)=\Deg(ty)=\Deg(ta^n)=1$, but $\Deg(t)=1$ also, so $\Deg(y)=0$, which is to say that $y \in \PQA^*$. Finally, $\Rank(x)=\Rank(ty)=\Rank(y)$, but also $\Rank(x)=\Rank(ta^n)=n$, so $\Rank(x)=\Rank(y)=n$.

Conversely, if $x=ty$ with $y \in \PQA^*$ and $\Rank(y)=n$, repeated application of $ta^is \Eq ta^i$ from \LemmaRef{lemma:rinf complete} allows us to erase all occurrences of $p$ and $q$ from $ty$. Only the initial $t$ followed by $a$'s remains, so $ty \Eq ta^n$.

\smallskip

(3.) If $\Deg(x) = 0$, then $x \in \PQA^*$ and $\Tail(x) \in \PQA$ by \LemmaRef{lemma:tpqa deg}. We pick an $s \in \PAndQ$ such that $\Tail(x) \neq s$. Then $\Tail(xs) = s$, and so $xs \NotEq x$ by \LemmaRef{lemma:tpqa well defined}.

\smallskip

(4.) If $\Deg(x) > 0$, then $x=ytz$ for some $y \in A^*$, $z \in \PQA^*$. By the second claim, $tz \Eq ta^n$ where $n=\Rank(z)$. Now let $s \in \PAndQ$. Making use of the identity $ta^ns \Eq ta^n$, we see that $xs = ytzs \Eq yta^ns \Eq yta^n \Eq ytz = x$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Finite complete presentations of $M$.}
Now, we assume we have a finite complete presentation $\Pres{C}{S}$ of $M$, and derive a contradiction. The contradiction will necessarily depend on the assumption that $S$ is \emph{both} finite and complete, because we already saw that $\Pres{A}{\RInf}$ is a perfectly reasonable \emph{infinite} complete presentation of $M$.

Roman letters besides $t$, $p$, $q$, $a$ will continue to denote words over~$A^*$, while lowercase Greek letters will denote words over $C^*$, with the exception of $\varepsilon$, which denotes the identity element of a monoid as before.

\begin{definition}[Encoding and decoding]\label{def:tpqa psi xi}
We fix a monoid isomorphism between $\Pres{C}{S}$ and $\AR$, and lift this isomorphism to a pair of homomorphisms between the \index{free monoid}free monoids $C^*$ and $A^*$. We get a ``decoding'' \index{monoid homomorphism}homomorphism $\Phi$, which maps each symbol of $C$ to a word over $A^*$:
\[
\Phi\colon C^* \rightarrow A^*
\]
We also get an ``encoding'' \index{monoid homomorphism}homomorphism $\Pi$ for the other direction:
\[
\Pi\colon A^* \rightarrow C^*
\]
These mappings satisfy various properties:
\begin{itemize}
\item For all $\alpha$, $\beta \in C^*$, $\alpha \Eq_{S} \beta$ if and only if $\Phi(\alpha) \Eq \Phi(\beta)$.
\item For all $x$, $y \in A^*$, $x \Eq y$ if and only if $\Pi(x) \Eq_{S} \Pi(y)$.
\item For all $\alpha \in C^*$, $\alpha \Eq_{S} \Pi(\Phi(\alpha))$.
\item For all $x \in A^*$, $x \Eq \Phi(\Pi(x))$.
\end{itemize}
Finally, since $\Deg$, $\Rank$, and $\Tail$ are well-defined on the equivalence classes of $\Eq$, we can simultaneously view them as mappings from our new alphabet, and they remain well-defined on the equivalence classes of $\Eq_S$:
\begin{gather*}
\Deg\colon C^* \rightarrow \NN \\
\Rank\colon C^* \rightarrow \NN \\
\Tail\colon C^* \rightarrow E
\end{gather*}
We define each one to first decode the input word to a word over $A^*$. That is, if $\lambda \in C^*$, we set $\Deg(\lambda) := \Deg(\Phi(\lambda))$, $\Rank(\lambda) := \Rank(\Phi(\lambda))$, and $\Tail(\lambda) := \Tail(\Phi(\lambda))$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[$S$-normal forms]
Since $\Pres{C}{S}$ is complete, $\Irr(S)$ contains one unique representative from each equivalence class of $\Eq_S$, and the \index{normal form algorithm}normal form algorithm assigns to each word in $C^*$ an irreducible word, equivalent to the original word via $\Eq_{S}$. We continue with the convention used in this book, and denote this mapping by $\Lambda$:
\[
\Lambda\colon C^* \rightarrow \Irr(S)
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Assumptions]\label{remark:c s assumptions}
Without loss of generality, we make three assumptions about $\Pres{C}{S}$ to simplify our proof.
\begin{enumerate}
\item
We assume that no rule in $S$ has a left-hand side that contains the left-hand side of any other rule. That is, we say that $S$ does not contain any overlaps of the first kind (\DefRef{overlappingrules}). For if any such rules were to appear in a complete presentation that is handed to us, we could apply \AlgRef{alg:simplify left} to remove them, without changing the reduction relation $\Red_S$.

\item
We similarly assume that the right-hand side of each rule in $S$ is irreducible by~$\Red_S$. Otherwise, we could apply \AlgRef{alg:simplify right} to replace the right-hand side with its $S$-normal form if necessary.

\item
The above further allows us to assume that $\Phi$ is \IndexDefinition{non-erasing homomorphism}\emph{non-erasing}, or that $|\Phi(\alpha)|>0$ for all symbols $\alpha \in C$. Indeed, suppose that $|\Phi(\alpha)| = 0$ for some $\alpha \in C$. Then $\alpha \Eq_S \varepsilon$, but since the empty word is always irreducible, $\alpha \Red_S \varepsilon$.

The first two assumptions then imply that $(\alpha, \varepsilon) \in S$, and $\alpha$ cannot appear on either side of any other rule in $S$. In this case, we can remove this rule from~$S$, and remove $\alpha$ from~$C$. Iterating this process must eventually stop, and leave us with a presentation $\Pres{C}{S}$ where $\Phi$ is non-erasing.
\end{enumerate}
\end{remark}

Next, we look at the $S$-normal forms of the words $ta^n$ for $n \geq 0$ and prove a couple of technical results that will be needed for the final stage of our proof.

\begin{definition}[Letters that start with $t$]
Let $T$ be the set of all $\tau \in C$ where $\Phi(\tau)) \Eq ta^n$ for some $n \geq 0$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Words that start with $t$]\label{lemma:t a decoding}
Suppose that $\lambda \in C^*$, and $\Phi(\lambda) \Eq ta^n$ for some $n \geq 0$. Then, $\lambda = \tau \sigma$ for some $\tau \in T$ and $\sigma \in C^*$ with $\Deg(\sigma)=0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By part 2 of \LemmaRef{lemma:tpqa word problem}, $\Phi(\lambda) = tx$ for some $x \in \PQA^*$. We write $\lambda = \tau \sigma$ where $\tau \in C$ and $\sigma \in C^*$. Since $\Phi$ is non-erasing by \RemarkRef{remark:c s assumptions}, $\Phi(\tau)=ty$ where $y$ is a (possibly empty) prefix of $x$. It follows that $y \in \PQA^*$, and $ty \Eq ta^m$ for some $0 \leq m \leq n$ by the same lemma, so $\tau \in T$ by definition of $T$. Finally, since $\Deg(ta^n)=\Deg(\tau\sigma)=1$ and $\Deg(\tau)=1$, we must have $\Deg(\sigma)=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Tail change]\label{lemma:tpqa tail change}
Let $\tau \in T$ and $\sigma \in C^*$ with $\Deg(\sigma)=0$. Let $s \in \PAndQ$ be chosen such that $\Tail(\sigma) \neq s$. Then any positive rewrite path over $S$ from $\tau\sigma \cdot \Pi(s)$ to $\tau\sigma$ contains a step of the form $( \tau' \zeta \Red \tau'' \eta ) \nu$, where $\tau'$, $\tau'' \in T$, $\zeta$, $\eta$, $\nu \in C^*$, and:
\begin{gather*}
\Tail(\zeta) = s \\
\Tail(\eta) \neq s \\
\Tail(\nu) = a
\end{gather*}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Every word visited by this path has the form described by \LemmaRef{lemma:t a decoding}, consisting of a symbol from $T$ followed by a degree-zero suffix from $C^*$. These words are all equivalent, so the $\Tail$ of every such word is~$t$. However, the $\Tail$ of their degree-zero suffixes varies. Indeed, the degree-zero suffix of the source of the first step is $\sigma \cdot \Pi(s)$, and $\Tail(\sigma \cdot \Pi(s))=s$ by definition. The degree-zero suffix of the destination of the final step is $\sigma$, and $\Tail(\sigma)\neq s$ by assumption.

Thus, our rewrite path must contain at least one step whose source and destination, say $\tau' \sigma'$ and $\tau'' \sigma''$, have degree-zero suffixes $\sigma'$ and $\sigma'' \in C^*$ with different tails:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\Tail(\sigma') = s$,
\item $\Tail(\sigma'') \neq s$.
\end{enumerate}
Now, a positive rewrite step over $S$ with source $\tau' \sigma'$ and destination $\tau'' \sigma''$ can take one of several forms:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The step might apply a rule $(\zeta, \eta) \in S$ somewhere in the degree-zero suffix $\sigma'$, while leaving $\tau'$ unchanged, so $\tau'' = \tau'$. In this case, our rewrite step has a non-empty left whisker of the form $\tau' \mu$ for some $\mu \in C^*$ with $\Deg(\mu)=0$, and a right whisker $\nu \in C^*$ also with $\Deg(\nu)=0$, so $\sigma' = \mu \zeta \nu$ and $\sigma'' = \mu \eta \nu$:
\[
\tau' \mu (\zeta \Red \eta) \nu
\]
Since $\zeta \Eq_S \eta$, we have $\Tail(\sigma') = \Tail(\mu \zeta \nu) = \Tail(\mu \eta \nu) = \Tail(\sigma'')$.

\item The step might apply a rule $(\tau' \zeta, \tau'' \eta) \in S$ to a prefix of $\tau' \sigma'$. In this case, our rewrite step has an empty left whisker, and a right whisker $\nu \in C^*$ with $\Deg(\nu)=0$, so $\sigma' = \zeta \nu$ and $\sigma'' = \eta \nu$:
\[
(\tau' \zeta \Red \tau'' \eta) \nu
\]
Since $\Deg(\nu)=0$, we have $\Tail(\nu) \in \PQA$ by part 1 of \LemmaRef{lemma:tpqa deg}. We consider each possibility:
\begin{enumerate}
\item We have $\Tail(\nu) \in \PAndQ$, in which case $\Tail(\sigma') = \Tail(\sigma'') = \Tail(\nu)$.
\item We have $\Tail(\nu) = a$, in which case $\Tail(\sigma') = \Tail(\zeta\nu) = \Tail(\zeta)$, and $\Tail(\sigma'') = \Tail(\eta\nu) = \Tail(\eta)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

Case 2b above is the only situation where  $\Tail(\sigma')$ does not necessarily equal $\Tail(\sigma'')$, because in general we might have $\Tail(\zeta) \neq \Tail(\eta)$.

Thus, any positive rewrite path over $S$ with source $\tau \sigma \cdot \Pi(s)$ and destination $\tau \sigma$ must contain at least one rewrite step of type 2b. In other words, we have a rewrite step of the form $( \tau' \zeta \Red \tau'' \eta ) \nu$, with $\tau'$, $\tau'' \in T$, $\zeta$, $\eta$, $\nu \in C^*$,  $\Tail(\zeta) = s$, $\Tail(\eta) \neq s$, and $\Tail(\nu) = a$, as claimed.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Reduction strategy]\label{def:rightmost strategy}
Recall that on each iteration of the \index{normal form algorithm}normal form algorithm, we scan the input string for occurrences of a left-hand side of a rule. It may be that the input string contains more than one such occurrence, in which case we are presented with a choice. The specific choice of reduction made by the normal form algorithm is referred to as the \IndexDefinition{reduction strategy}\emph{reduction strategy}:
\begin{itemize}
\item The \IndexDefinition{leftmost reduction strategy}\emph{leftmost} reduction strategy rewrites the first occurrence of a left-hand side.
\item The \IndexDefinition{rightmost reduction strategy}\emph{rightmost} reduction strategy rewrites the last occurrence of a left-hand side.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

With a confluent and terminating reduction relation, every possible reduction strategy will always yield the same normal form when iterated to fixed point. For this reason, we left the strategy unspecified when we first encountered the normal form algorithm (\AlgRef{alg:normal form}). The implementation of the normal form algorithm in the Requirement Machine (\AlgRef{alg:trie normal form}) used the leftmost reduction strategy.

However, in the present context, by making a specific choice of reduction strategy, we can obtain a positive rewrite path with a certain property that we need.

\begin{lemma}[Rightmost strategy]\label{lemma:rightmost}
Suppose that $\lambda \in C^*$, and we have a positive rewrite path from $\lambda$ to $\Lambda(\lambda)$ constructed by the rightmost strategy. If $\mu (\zeta \Red \eta) \nu$ is a rewrite step in this path, and~$\zeta'$ is a proper suffix of $\zeta$, then $\zeta' \nu \in \Irr(S)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
We assume that no left-hand side in $S$ contains any other left-hand side, by part 1 of \RemarkRef{remark:c s assumptions}. Therefore, $\zeta' \in \Irr(S)$, since $\zeta'$ is a proper suffix of a left-hand side $\zeta$. Thus, if $\zeta' \nu$ were to contain the left-hand side of some other rule from~$S$, this left-hand side would either straddle the boundary between $\zeta'$ and $\nu$, or be contained entirely within $\nu$. In either case, the source of our rewrite step, $\mu \zeta \nu$, would contain another reduction with a right whisker that is a proper suffix of $\nu$. This contradicts our assumption that the positive rewrite path was constructed via the rightmost strategy.
\end{proof}

Recall the discussion of regular languages from \SecRef{sec:regular languages}. Since we assumed $S$ to be finite, we know that $\Irr(S)$ is a regular language by \LemmaRef{irr r facts}, and we can apply \LemmaRef{pumping lemma}, the \index{pumping lemma}pumping lemma, to irreducible words of sufficient length to construct longer irreducible words. We will use this to construct two infinite families of irreducible words in $\Irr(S)$, which we then overlap using \LemmaRef{lemma:irreducible overlap} to get an even longer irreducible word. This will yield a contradiction, because we will find that this word is equivalent to a proper prefix of itself.

\begin{definition}[Important constants]
Let $\Ell$ denote the pumping length of $\Irr(S)$, and let $\Enn$ denote the length of the longest left-hand side in $S$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[The first pump]\label{lemma:tpqa first pump}
There exists an $s \in \PAndQ$ and $\lambda$, $\mu \in C^*$ with:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\lambda\mu^n \in \Irr(S)$ for all $n \geq 0$,
\item $\Phi(\lambda) \in \PQA^* \cdot s \cdot a^*$,
\item $\Phi(\mu) \in a^+$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
First, we pick a sufficiently large $n$ with the property that for any word $\tau \sigma$, not necessarily irreducible, with $\tau \in T$, $\sigma \in C^*$, and $\Phi(\tau \sigma) \Eq ta^n$, we have:
\[
|\tau\sigma| \geq \Enn + \Ell + 1
\]
Such an $n$ must always exist, since our alphabet $C$ is finite, and the words $ta^n$ are in distinct equivalence classes of $\Eq$ for distinct $n$, so they become arbitrarily long as we increase $n$.

Now, consider the irreducible word $\Lambda(\Pi(ta^n)) \in \Irr(S)$. By \LemmaRef{lemma:t a decoding}, there exist $\tau \in T$ and $\sigma \in C^*$ with $\Deg(\sigma)=0$, such that:
\[
\Lambda(\Pi(t a^n))=\tau \sigma
\]
Since $\Phi(\tau\sigma) \Eq ta^n$, part 4 of \LemmaRef{lemma:tpqa word problem} says that $\Phi(\tau\sigma) \cdot s \Eq \Phi(\tau\sigma)$ for both $s \in \PAndQ$, or in other words:
\[
\tau\sigma \cdot \Pi(s) \Eq_S \tau\sigma
\]
On the other hand, if we look at the suffix $\sigma$ in isolation, part 3 of the same lemma tells us that we cannot have $\sigma \cdot \Pi(s) \Eq \sigma$ for both $s \in \PAndQ$, since $\Deg(\sigma)=0$. Henceforth, we fix an $s \in \PAndQ$ such that $\sigma \cdot \Pi(s) \NotEq \sigma$, which implies that $\Tail(\sigma) \neq s$.

We compute the normal form of $\tau \sigma \cdot \Pi(s)$ using the rightmost reduction strategy from \DefRef{def:rightmost strategy}. We get a positive rewrite path. By \LemmaRef{lemma:tpqa tail change}, this rewrite path has a step of the form $( \tau' \zeta \Red \tau'' \eta ) \nu$, where $\tau'$, $\tau'' \in T$, $\zeta$, $\eta$, $\nu \in C^*$, and:
\begin{gather*}
\Tail(\zeta) = s\\
\Tail(\eta) \neq s\\
\Tail(\nu) = a
\end{gather*}
Furthermore, our positive rewrite path was constructed with the rightmost reduction strategy, so by \LemmaRef{lemma:rightmost}, we also have $\zeta \nu \in \Irr(S)$, since $\zeta$ is a proper suffix of the left-hand side $\tau' \zeta$ of a rule $(\tau' \zeta, \tau'' \eta) \in S$.

By our choice of $n$, we have $|\tau' \zeta \nu| \geq \Enn + \Ell + 1$, but $|\tau'| = 1$, so $|\zeta \nu| \geq \Enn + \Ell$. However, $|\zeta| < \Enn$, because $\zeta$ is a proper suffix of a left-hand side in~$S$. Therefore, $|\nu| \geq \Ell$.

Applying \index{pumping lemma}the pumping lemma ``from the end'' to $\zeta \nu$ yields a factorization:
\[\zeta \nu = \zeta \nu_0 \nu_1 \nu_2\]
where:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $|\nu_1| > 0$,
%\item $|\nu_1\nu_2| \leq \Ell$,
\item $\zeta \nu_0 \cdot \nu_1^n \cdot \nu_2 \in \Irr(S)$ for all $n \geq 0$.
\end{enumerate}
Since a prefix of an irreducible word is irreducible by \LemmaRef{irr r facts}, we can chop off the suffix $\nu_2$ from $\nu_0 \nu_1^n \nu_2$, and conclude that for all $n \geq 0$:
\[
\zeta \nu_0 \cdot \nu_1^n \in \Irr(S)
\]
We set:
\begin{gather*}
\lambda := \zeta \nu_0 \\
\mu := \nu_1
\end{gather*}
We make use of \LemmaRef{lemma:tpqa tail}. From $\Tail(\nu) = a$, we get $\Phi(\nu) = \Phi(\nu_0\nu_1\nu_2) \in a^+$, and:
\begin{gather*}
\Phi(\mu) = \Phi(\nu_1) \in a^+
\end{gather*}
From $\Tail(\zeta)=s$ and $\Phi(\nu_0) \in a^*$, we get that:
\begin{gather*}
\Phi(\zeta) \in \PQA^* \cdot s \cdot a^* \\
\Phi(\lambda) = \Phi(\zeta \nu_0) \in \PQA^* \cdot s \cdot a^*
\end{gather*}
Here is a picture, with the bottom row showing a regular set containing the image of $\Phi$:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{array}{|c|c|c|}
\hline
\multicolumn{2}{|c|}{\lambda} & \mu \\
\hline
\hline
\zeta & \nu_0 & \nu_1 \\
\hline
\PQA^* \cdot s \cdot a^* & a^* & a^+ \\
\hline
\end{array}
\]
\end{ceqn}
Thus, the two words $\lambda$, $\mu$ are exactly as claimed.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[The second pump]\label{lemma:tpqa second pump}
Let $s \in \PAndQ$. There exist $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma \in C^*$ such that:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\alpha^n \beta \gamma \in \Irr(S)$ for all $n \geq 0$,
\item $\Phi(\alpha) \in a^+$.
\item $\Phi(\beta) \in a^* \cdot s^*$,
\item $\Phi(\gamma) \in s^+$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
We will first construct three words $\alpha'$, $\beta'$, $\gamma$, and then fiddle around to get the desired words $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$. (Note that $\gamma$ remains unchanged.)

By part 1 of \LemmaRef{lemma:tpqa word problem}, $a^i s^j$ is a singleton equivalence class of $\Eq$ for all $i$, $j > 0$. Thus, the irreducible words $\Lambda(\Pi(a^i s^j))$ are all distinct for distinct $(i, j$), and not only is it true that $\Phi(\Lambda(\Pi(a^i s^j))) \Eq a^i s^j$, but in fact $\Phi(\Lambda(\Pi(a^i s^j))) = a^i s^j$.

Since the alphabet $C$ is finite, the irreducible words $\Lambda(\Pi(a^i s^j))$ become arbitrarily long as we increase $i$ and $j$. It follows that for sufficiently large $i$ and $j$, we have:
\[
\Lambda(\Pi(a^i s a^j)) = \alpha' \beta' \gamma
\]
where:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\Phi(\alpha') \in a^+$ and $|\alpha'| \geq \Ell$,
\item $\Phi(\beta') \in a^* \cdot s^*$.
\item $\Phi(\gamma) \in s^+$.
\end{enumerate}
Here is a picture:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{array}{|c|c|c|}
\hline
\alpha' & \beta' & \gamma \\
\hline
\hline
a^+ & a^* \cdot s^* & s^+ \\
\hline
\end{array}
\]
\end{ceqn}
Applying \index{pumping lemma}the pumping lemma ``from the start'' to the word $\alpha' \beta' \gamma$ yields a factorization:
\[
\alpha' \beta' \gamma = \alpha_0 \alpha_1 \alpha_2 \beta' \gamma
\]
where:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $|\alpha_1| > 0$,
%\item $|\alpha_0 \alpha_1| \leq \Ell$,
\item $\alpha_0 \cdot \alpha_1^n \cdot \alpha_2 \beta' \gamma \in \Irr(S)$ for all $n \geq 0$.
\end{enumerate}
We chop off the prefix $\alpha_0$, and conclude that $\alpha_1^n \cdot \alpha_2 \beta' \gamma \in \Irr(S)$ for all $n \geq 0$. We set:
\begin{gather*}
\alpha := \alpha_1 \\
\beta := \alpha_2 \beta'
\end{gather*}
From $\Phi(\alpha) \in a^+$, we get that $\Phi(\alpha_0)$, $\Phi(\alpha_2) \in a^*$, and furthermore since $|\alpha_1|>0$:
\[
\Phi(\alpha_1) \in a^+
\]
By concatenation of regular sets:
\[
\Phi(\beta) = \Phi(\alpha_2 \cdot \beta') \in a^* \cdot s^*
\]
Here is a picture:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{array}{|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
\alpha & \multicolumn{2}{c|}{\beta} & \gamma \\
\hline
\hline
\alpha_1 & \alpha_2 & \beta' & \gamma \\
\hline
a^+ & a^* & a^* \cdot s^* & s^+ \\
\hline
\end{array}
\]
\end{ceqn}
Thus, the three words $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$ constructed above are exactly as claimed.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{We connect the pumps.}
We find $s \in \PAndQ$ and $\lambda$, $\mu \in C^*$ with \LemmaRef{lemma:tpqa first pump}:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{array}{|c|c|}
\hline
\lambda & \mu \\
\hline
\PQA^* \cdot s \cdot a^* & a^+ \\
\hline
\end{array}
\]
\end{ceqn}
Taking the same $s \in \PAndQ$, we find $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $\gamma$ with \LemmaRef{lemma:tpqa second pump}:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{array}{|c|c|c|}
\hline
\alpha & \beta & \gamma \\
\hline
a^+ & a^* \cdot s^* & s^+ \\
\hline
\end{array}
\]
\end{ceqn}
Recalling that $\Rank$ was a homomorphism by definition, we have:
\[\Rank(\mu^{\Rank(\alpha)}) = \Rank(\alpha^{\Rank(\mu)}) = \Rank(\alpha) \cdot \Rank(\mu)\]
But $\Phi(\alpha)$ and $\Phi(\mu)$ are elements of $a^*$, hence:
\[
\Phi(\mu^{\Rank(\alpha)}) = \Phi(\alpha^{\Rank(\mu)}) = a^{\Rank(\alpha) \cdot \Rank(\mu)}
\]
Furthermore, $\alpha$, $\mu \in \Irr(S)$, and when two irreducible words decode to the same word, they're identical:
\[
\alpha^{\Rank(\mu)} = \mu^{\Rank(\alpha)}
\]
Now, we pick a sufficiently large $k > 0$ so that $|\alpha^{k \cdot \Rank(\mu)}| = |\mu^{k \cdot \Rank(\alpha)}| \geq \Enn$, and we set:
\[
\delta := \alpha^{k \cdot \Rank(\mu)} = \mu^{k \cdot \Rank(\alpha)}
\]
We have two irreducible words, the first ending in $\delta$ and the second starting with $\delta$:
\begin{alignat*}{2}
\lambda \cdot {} & \delta & {} \in \Irr(S) \\
& \delta \cdot \beta \gamma & {} \in \Irr(S)
\end{alignat*}
Since $|\delta| \geq \Enn$, we can apply \LemmaRef{lemma:irreducible overlap}:
\[
\lambda \cdot \delta \cdot \beta \gamma \in \Irr(S)
\]
Here is a picture:
\begin{ceqn}
\[
\begin{array}{|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
\lambda & \delta & \beta & \gamma \\
\hline
\hline
\PQA^* \cdot s \cdot a^* & a^+ & a^* \cdot s^* & s^+ \\
\hline
\end{array}
\]
\end{ceqn}

\paragraph{The end falls off.}
Finally, consider the words $\lambda \delta \beta$ and $\lambda \delta \beta \gamma$. The first one is again irreducible, being a prefix of the second, and both are distinct, because $|\gamma| > 0$. Now, watch what happens when we apply $\Phi$ to both words. Since $\Tail(\lambda) = s$ and $|\delta| > 0$, we can repeatedly apply the identity $sa^ns \Eq sa^n$ from \LemmaRef{lemma:rinf complete} to delete all occurrences of $s$ in $\beta$ and $\gamma$:
\begin{alignat*}{2}
& \Phi(\lambda \delta \beta) & {} \Eq \Phi(\lambda) \cdot a^{\Rank(\delta) + \Rank(\beta)} \\
& \Phi(\lambda \delta \beta \gamma) & {} \Eq \Phi(\lambda) \cdot a^{\Rank(\delta) + \Rank(\beta)}
\end{alignat*}
We found two distinct irreducible words in the same equivalence class, a contradiction. Our original assumption that $\Pres{C}{S}$ is both finite and complete cannot stand.
\end{proof}
